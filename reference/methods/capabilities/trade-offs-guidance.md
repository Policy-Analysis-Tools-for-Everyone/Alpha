# Trade-Offs - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main policy-analysis spine and adds only the shared appraisal material that changes how competing outcomes should be compared. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Six - used for dominance, outcome rather than intervention comparisons, magnitude, base cases, commensurability, implied valuation, marginal reasoning, lumpy choices, weighting and narrowing. **[A]** `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called for multi-dimensional risk and opportunity, systemic upside and downside, asymmetric uncertainty, option interaction, path dependence and the limits of forcing open-ended futures into one expected-value calculation. **[T]** `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` - called where improving value, capacity or support creates a material sacrifice elsewhere in the proposition. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user make explicit *what is gained, what is sacrificed, how large the exchange is, which uncertainty could change it, and which value judgment ultimately decides whether the exchange is acceptable*.

## How to use this document

When asked to compare serious options:

1. **Start with projected outcomes, not option labels.**
2. **Check for dominance.**
3. **Identify the few criteria where the serious options genuinely conflict.**
4. **State the size of the gain and sacrifice.**
5. **Keep the base case explicit.**
6. **Use a common metric where it genuinely helps.**
7. **Keep distinct values separate where aggregation would hide the decision.**
8. **Include risk, opportunity and distribution where they matter.**
9. **Reason at the margin where the choice concerns an increment.**
10. **Carry uncertainty through the comparison.**
11. **Narrow to the decision-sensitive exchange.**
12. **Stop before making the final recommendation.**

The aim is to expose the **real choice**, not manufacture one.

# Part 1 - Establish whether a trade-off exists

## 1. Check dominance first [B]

If one option is better on every material evaluative criterion and satisfies the relevant constraints, it may dominate the others.

Before declaring dominance, check:

- missing criteria
- unequal assumptions
- weak forecasts
- distribution
- uncertainty
- binding constraints

If dominance survives, say so.

Do not force a "balanced" comparison where the evidence is one-sided.

## 2. Compare outcomes, not interventions [B]

Weak:

> More inspectors versus a new digital system.

Useful:

> 15-20% more cases detected at £2m extra annual cost versus 5-10% more cases detected at £700k.

The intervention names are not the trade-off.

The expected consequences are.

## 3. Carry magnitude forward [B]

Prefer:

> 200-300 additional cases resolved each year for £4m more annual expenditure.

over:

> better outcomes but higher cost.

Use:

- point estimates
- ranges
- thresholds
- ordinal descriptions

according to the quality of the evidence.

Do not hide weak projection behind `+` and `-`.

## 4. Identify the decision-relevant conflicts [B]

Ask:

> **Which 2 or 3 outcome differences could actually change the choice?**

Examples:

- access versus cost
- safety versus liberty
- speed versus consultation
- targeting versus coverage
- resilience versus short-run efficiency

Do not drag every criterion into the headline trade-off.

# Part 2 - Make comparison explicit

## 5. State the base case [B]

Trade-offs are relative.

Possible base cases include:

- business-as-usual
- another option
- a future baseline after an external change

State it.

Ask whether a plausible change in the base case alters the comparison.

## 6. Seek commensurability where it helps [B][A]

Useful common measures can include:

- cost per additional case
- cost per life-year
- time saved
- emissions avoided
- incidents prevented

Use them where outcomes are genuinely comparable.

Do not force:

- rights
- dignity
- trust
- legitimacy
- irreversible harms

into one metric solely to create a ranking.

## 7. Keep irreducibly different dimensions visible [A]

Where one metric would hide a contested judgment, state the exchange directly.

Example:

> Option A produces a larger reduction in serious harm but requires substantially more intrusive data use.

Then ask:

> **How much intrusion is acceptable for that additional reduction in harm?**

This is a value judgment.

Make it visible.

## 8. Avoid double counting [B]

If 2 criteria partly represent the same consequence, do not count both fully.

Example:

- waiting time
- burden
- satisfaction

may overlap.

The apparent trade-off can be distorted if one benefit is counted several times.

# Part 3 - Bring risk and opportunity into the comparison

## 9. Compare plausible upside and downside [A]

Expected outcomes can hide asymmetry.

For each serious option, ask:

- What is the plausible upside?
- What is the plausible downside?
- Are the tails meaningfully different?
- Is one option more likely to create irreversible harm?
- Is one option more likely to open valuable future possibilities?

Do not invent dramatic scenarios.

Use outcomes supported by a plausible mechanism.

## 10. Include systemic risk and opportunity [A]

A policy can have effects beyond the direct criterion-by-criterion forecast.

Possible systemic risk:

- lock-in
- capacity crowd-out
- dependency
- market concentration
- cascading failure
- political erosion

Possible systemic opportunity:

- new capability
- learning
- spillovers
- cost decline
- stronger complementary investment
- new policy options

Include these where they can change the choice.

Do not use "systemic" as another list of speculative pros and cons.

## 11. Consider path dependence [A]

Ask:

- What does choosing this make easier later?
- What does it make harder?
- Does it close off another path?
- Does it preserve options while uncertainty resolves?

A slightly weaker short-term option may sometimes preserve a much better future choice.

Do not assume present net benefit is the whole trade-off for long-lived decisions.

## 12. Compare packages where interaction matters [A]

If A and B reinforce one another, the real choice may be:

- A
- B
- A + B
- staged A then B

Use the option set actually facing the decision maker.

Do not compare components in isolation when the policy mechanism is collective.

# Part 4 - Reason at the margin

## 13. Focus on the extra exchange [B]

When the decision concerns scale, ask:

> **What does the extra unit of sacrifice buy in extra outcome?**

Example:

> An extra £1m is expected to reduce the waiting list by another 600-900 cases.

Marginal reasoning is often more useful than total programme averages.

## 14. Distinguish average from marginal value [B]

The first increment of an intervention may produce much more than the next.

Do not assume:

> average cost per outcome = cost of expanding further.

Ask what the next increment produces.

## 15. Handle lumpy choices honestly [B]

Some options cannot be divided smoothly.

Examples:

- building infrastructure
- changing legislation
- procuring a platform
- introducing a national scheme

Define the smallest feasible increment.

Do not model fictional fractions of an indivisible policy.

# Part 5 - Preserve distribution and constraints

## 16. Keep who gains and loses visible [B][A]

Where distribution is one of the criteria, state:

- who gains
- who loses
- who pays
- who bears risk
- who receives long-run benefits
- who bears transition cost

Do not let a positive aggregate erase a serious distributional choice.

## 17. Treat constraints differently from tradable objectives [B]

A genuine constraint may include:

- law
- fixed budget
- minimum safety
- non-negotiable right

If an option fails it, another benefit may not compensate.

Clarify whether the requirement is genuinely binding.

Do not let a weighted score trade away a constraint accidentally.

## 18. Use the Strategic Triangle where the exchange is between value, capacity and support [T]

Sometimes the trade-off is not only between outcome criteria.

Examples:

- higher public value requires capacity that cannot be built quickly
- a more deliverable option creates less public value
- a stronger value proposition loses essential political support
- broader support requires concessions that weaken the policy

Use `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` to diagnose that tension.

Then return here to state the sacrifice clearly.

# Part 6 - Keep uncertainty in the trade-off

## 19. Carry ranges through [B][A]

If Option A prevents 30-80 cases, do not convert it into one exact cost-per-case figure without showing the range.

Ask:

- Could plausible uncertainty reverse the trade-off?
- Does one option have much greater uncertainty than another?
- Is the downside asymmetric?

Do not let comparison create more certainty than projection provided.

## 20. Find switchpoints [B]

Ask:

> **At what value does the choice change?**

Examples:

- if uptake falls below X
- if unit cost exceeds Y
- if benefit is less than Z
- if implementation takes longer than N months

A switchpoint can make a contested trade-off much clearer.

## 21. Test sensitivity to value weights [B][A]

Where weights are used:

- vary them
- see whether the ranking changes
- identify which value judgment drives the result

If a tiny change reverses the winner, say so.

Do not present a fragile weighted ranking as settled.

## 22. Distinguish uncertainty from normative disagreement

If both parties accept the outcome estimates but disagree on which outcome matters more, more forecasting will not resolve the disagreement.

If they agree on values but disagree on the effect size, more evidence may help.

Do not send a value conflict back to evidence gathering.

---

# Part 7 - Make implied valuation visible

## 23. Every choice implies a valuation [B]

If Option A costs £2m more for 100 additional cases resolved, choosing A implies that the additional cases are worth at least that sacrifice.

Choosing B implies the opposite.

Ask:

> **Does that implied judgment make sense against the priorities already stated?**

This exposes hidden weighting.

## 24. Use implied value as a consistency check [B]

Work backwards from a tentative preference.

Ask:

- What must we believe this gain is worth?
- Is that consistent with the criteria?
- Is it consistent with comparable decisions?
- Is a hidden value driving the choice?

Do not use this as a rhetorical trick.

Use it to test coherence.

---

# Part 8 - Narrow and deepen

## 25. Find the exchange that actually decides the choice [B]

Ask:

> **What would have to be valued, forecast or understood differently for another option to win?**

That is the trade-off to explain most carefully.

## 26. Remove comparisons that no longer matter [B]

By this stage the analysis may contain many:

- alternatives
- criteria
- outcome cells
- weights
- scenarios

Keep the few elements bearing on the choice.

Do not let the apparatus become the product.

## 27. Return to earlier steps if every option is poor [B]

If no serious alternative is good enough:

- revise the option set
- revisit the problem
- improve evidence
- change criteria
- improve outcome projection

Do not choose the least bad simply because Step Six has arrived.

## 28. Stop before recommendation [B]

A good trade-off statement can end:

> Option A is expected to resolve 200-300 more cases per year than Option B but costs roughly £4m more and creates greater implementation risk. The choice turns mainly on whether that additional outcome is worth the cost and whether the organisation accepts the delivery risk.

That is enough.

Step Seven decides.

# Trade-off scaffold

1. **Serious options:** Which remain live?
2. **Dominance:** Does one clearly dominate?
3. **Base case:** Compared with what?
4. **Conflicting criteria:** Which outcomes pull in different directions?
5. **Magnitude:** What is gained and lost?
6. **Commensurability:** What can be compared on a common basis?
7. **Distinct values:** What should remain separate?
8. **Risk / opportunity:** What asymmetric or systemic effects matter?
9. **Distribution:** Who gains and loses?
10. **Constraints:** What cannot simply be traded away?
11. **Margin:** What does the extra sacrifice buy?
12. **Lumpiness:** What is the real feasible increment?
13. **Uncertainty:** Could ranges reverse the comparison?
14. **Switchpoint:** At what value does the choice change?
15. **Implied valuation:** What does the tentative choice say the gain is worth?
16. **Sensitivity to weights:** Does a small normative change reverse the ranking?
17. **Central exchange:** Which trade-off actually decides the choice?
18. **Stop:** Hand the clarified choice to the decision method.

→ **Trade-off statement:** Compared with `[base case / option]`, `[Option A]` produces `[additional gain with magnitude/range]` while requiring or causing `[additional sacrifice with magnitude/range]`. The main systemic or asymmetric consideration is `[risk/opportunity]`. The choice turns on `[explicit value judgment]` and would change if `[switchpoint or uncertainty]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Comparing interventions rather than outcomes

**Challenge:** translate each option into what happens.

## Pitfall B - Inventing balance where one option dominates

**Challenge:** check dominance first.

## Pitfall C - No magnitude

**Challenge:** state how much is gained and sacrificed.

## Pitfall D - False commensurability

**Challenge:** keep distinct values separate where conversion hides the judgment.

## Pitfall E - Spreadsheet makes the normative choice

**Challenge:** expose weights and implied valuations.

## Pitfall F - Hidden base case

**Challenge:** say what the difference is measured against.

## Pitfall G - Average instead of marginal reasoning

**Challenge:** ask about the next increment.

## Pitfall H - Aggregate hides distribution

**Challenge:** state who gains and loses.

## Pitfall I - Constraint compensated away

**Challenge:** identify what is genuinely non-tradable.

## Pitfall J - Risk reduced to expected value

**Challenge:** keep asymmetric and systemic downside/upside visible where material.

## Pitfall K - Uncertainty disappears

**Challenge:** preserve ranges and switchpoints.

## Pitfall L - Optimal pathway invented

**Challenge:** consider path dependence and future option value where the future is open-ended.

## Pitfall M - Analysis keeps expanding after the choice is clear

**Challenge:** narrow and deepen.

## Pitfall N - Trade-offs quietly become recommendation

**Challenge:** state the exchange, then stop.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The comparison concerns projected outcomes.
- Dominance has been checked.
- The base case is explicit.
- Main gains and sacrifices have magnitude or the best calibrated description available.
- Material uncertainty remains visible.
- Distinct values are not forced into one metric without justification.
- Distribution remains visible where selected as a criterion.
- Binding constraints are not traded away silently.
- The value judgment deciding the exchange is explicit.
- The analysis identifies the small number of comparisons capable of changing the decision.
- The method stops before recommendation.

## Should pass

- Risk and opportunity have been considered where expected values hide important asymmetry.
- Systemic effects or path dependence have been considered where material.
- Marginal reasoning is used for incremental choices.
- Lumpy decisions use real feasible increments.
- Switchpoints are used where useful.
- Weights are tested for sensitivity where used.
- Implied valuations have been checked for consistency.
- Weak options or irrelevant criteria have been removed.

# Shared method calls

## Call `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- risk and opportunity are asymmetric
- system effects matter
- path dependence or lock-in changes the comparison
- options interact
- several important dimensions should not be collapsed into one expected value

## Call `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- the trade-off is materially between public value, capacity and required support
- concessions to gain support reduce public value
- a more valuable option depends on unrealistic capacity

## Call `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- the central trade-off is unstable because one learnable uncertainty dominates it

Do not send a pure value conflict to experimentation.

# One-line reference

> Good trade-off analysis compares projected outcomes rather than option labels, makes the size and uncertainty of the gain and sacrifice visible, preserves distribution and important non-commensurable values, includes systemic risk and opportunity where they matter, and exposes the judgment that actually decides whether the exchange is worth making.
