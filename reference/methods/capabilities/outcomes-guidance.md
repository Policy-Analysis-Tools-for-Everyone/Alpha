# Outcomes - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main policy-analysis spine and adds only the shared material that improves forecasting under uncertainty and system change. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Five - used for doing the projection, causal models, initial conditions, base cases, magnitude, ranges, break-even analysis, sensitivity, adverse scenarios, foreseeable side effects and outcomes matrices. **[A]** `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called for outside-view and reference-class forecasting, direction and pace of change, feedbacks, path dependence, option interaction, systemic effects, model uncertainty and false precision. **[U]** `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` - called when a decision-sensitive projection cannot responsibly be made without further learning. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user state *what each serious option is expected to cause, compared with what, through which mechanism, at what scale and pace, and with what uncertainty*.

## How to use this document

When asked to project or compare likely outcomes:

1. **Start with serious alternatives and selected criteria.**
2. **Project the business-as-usual path as well as new action.**
3. **State the causal pathway.**
4. **Ground it in evidence and current conditions.**
5. **Estimate direction, magnitude and time.**
6. **Use ranges where precision is not justified.**
7. **Take the outside view where comparable cases exist.**
8. **Consider feedbacks, path dependence and option interaction where they can change the forecast.**
9. **Test sensitivity, break-even points and adverse implementation.**
10. **Call the learning method when the forecast is too weak to support the decision.**
11. **Place the resulting projections into an outcomes matrix.**
12. **Stop before deciding how competing outcomes should be traded.**

The job is to make the future **explicit enough to challenge**.

# Part 1 - Make the projection

## 1. Project outcomes, not intentions [B]

Ask for every serious option:

> **If we did this, what would probably happen?**

Do not substitute:

- programme description
- stated objective
- evidence the problem exists
- evidence that a similar intervention exists
- a positive label

An intervention is an action.

An outcome is the consequence expected to follow.

## 2. Separate intention from effect [B]

A:

> rapid-response service

does not prove:

> rapid response.

A:

> prevention programme

does not prove:

> fewer future cases.

Strip intended success out of the option label.

Then project the effect.

## 3. Treat future statements as claims [B]

State what is:

- observed
- inferred
- assumed
- estimated
- uncertain

Do not turn a slight balance of belief into certainty.

Bardach's 51-49 warning matters here: being marginally more likely than not does not justify writing "will".

## 4. Use causal reasoning [B]

A projection should answer:

> **What has to happen between the intervention and the outcome?**

Write the important chain.

Example:

> Confirmation message sent → user sees it → uncertainty about receipt falls → fewer reassurance contacts → contact demand falls.

Then ask:

- Which links are evidenced?
- Which are assumptions?
- Which actor responses could break the chain?

## 5. Use several models if needed [B]

One policy can depend on:

- behaviour
- organisational process
- incentives
- markets
- political response
- implementation
- technology

Use the model needed for the consequence being projected.

Do not protect analytical neatness by ignoring material effects.

# Part 2 - Anchor the projection in reality

## 6. Record the starting conditions [B]

The same intervention can behave differently under different conditions.

Relevant starting conditions may include:

- baseline demand
- staffing
- institutional rules
- current behaviour
- political support
- technical capability
- existing trends
- relationships
- capacity

Do not transplant an effect from another setting without considering where this setting differs.

## 7. Project the base case [B]

Business-as-usual is not today's conditions frozen in time.

Project:

- current policy
- existing trends
- known external change
- likely ongoing implementation

Ask:

> **What would probably happen anyway?**

Then isolate what changes because of the alternative.

## 8. Estimate direction, magnitude and time [B][A]

For each decision-relevant outcome, state:

### Direction

Increase / decrease / no material change / unclear.

### Magnitude

How much?

### Time

When does the effect appear and over what period?

For structural change, also consider **pace**.

2 options can reach similar end states but differ greatly in whether they move fast enough to matter.

## 9. Use a range where a point estimate overstates knowledge [B][A]

A useful projection can contain:

- best current estimate
- plausible range
- assumptions driving the range

Do not use a huge range solely to avoid judgment.

Do not use one precise number solely because a spreadsheet needs one.

# Part 3 - Challenge the forecast

## 10. Take the outside view [A]

Where a credible reference class exists, ask:

> **What actually happened in comparable interventions?**

Compare:

- cost
- duration
- uptake
- delivery
- benefit
- failure

Then ask:

> **Why should this case differ?**

Do not assume the current team is exceptional because it has a detailed plan.

## 11. Challenge systematic optimism [A]

Ask:

- Have similar forecasts historically been too optimistic?
- Does approval depend on favourable numbers?
- Is the forecaster invested in the option?
- Is there independent challenge?
- Does the range capture only random variation or also model and assumption uncertainty?

Do not accuse without evidence.

Do not ignore structural incentives around the forecast.

## 12. Distinguish estimate uncertainty from model uncertainty [A]

A statistical range may describe variation **inside** a model.

It may not capture whether:

- the causal model is wrong
- the relevant context changes
- behaviour adapts
- a feedback appears
- the policy alters the system it is forecasting

State what the uncertainty range actually represents.

## 13. Match precision to the knowledge [A]

Do not report a precise-looking forecast where:

- key inputs are guesses
- one assumption dominates
- comparable cases vary widely
- the model itself is fragile

Use a calibrated range or qualitative statement instead.

# Part 4 - Project dynamic and system effects

## 14. Consider feedbacks where they matter [A]

Ask:

- What reinforces the effect?
- What counteracts it?
- Does success make further success easier?
- Does demand increase as the service improves?
- Does scale reduce cost?
- Does opposition increase as impact becomes visible?

Do not assume linear effects where the mechanism suggests feedback.

## 15. Consider path dependence and lock-in [A]

Ask:

- What becomes easier later if we choose this?
- What becomes harder?
- Does the option lock in a technology, supplier, institution or behaviour?
- Does it preserve future choices?

These are outcomes too.

Do not focus only on the first-year effect.

## 16. Consider interactions among options [A]

If policies are complementary or conflicting, project:

- each option alone
- the relevant package
- sequencing if order matters

Do not assume the total effect of A + B equals the sum of their isolated effects.

## 17. Consider systemic risk and opportunity [A]

Where the policy changes wider relationships or capabilities, ask about:

- spillovers
- crowding out
- new capability
- dependencies
- resilience
- wider adoption
- unintended system effects

Keep only system effects with a plausible mechanism.

Do not use "systemic" as decoration.

# Part 5 - Test uncertainty and failure

## 18. Use break-even reasoning [B]

Where effectiveness is uncertain:

1. What minimum effect would make the option worthwhile?
2. What processes must occur to reach that effect?
3. How plausible is that level?

This converts:

> Will it work?

into:

> **How well does it need to work before it is worth doing?**

## 19. Use switchpoints [B]

If one uncertain quantity drives the choice, find the value at which the preferred option changes.

Examples:

- uptake
- unit cost
- participation
- duration
- effect size

Then ask whether reality is plausibly above or below it.

## 20. Use sensitivity analysis [B]

Ask:

> **How wrong could this assumption be before the conclusion changes?**

Prioritise assumptions with the smallest margin for error.

Do not spend equal effort refining irrelevant uncertainties.

## 21. Test combinations of uncertainty where needed [B][A]

One-at-a-time sensitivity can miss interaction.

Where several uncertain assumptions matter together, use:

- scenarios
- combined sensitivity
- simulation where justified
- plausible pathway comparison

Do not add technical modelling for appearance.

## 22. Write an adverse implementation scenario [B]

Imagine that several years later the policy has underperformed.

Work backwards.

Possible causes:

- delay
- cost growth
- lower uptake
- administrative complexity
- capture
- weak compliance
- political erosion
- capacity overload
- fraud
- standard procedures defeating the design

Use this to expose assumptions that the main forecast treats too favourably.

## 23. Project foreseeable side effects [B]

Do not call foreseeable effects "unanticipated".

Ask about:

- gaming
- moral hazard
- substitution
- displacement
- benefit capture
- burden shifting
- workarounds
- changing incentives

Include material harms in the outcomes matrix.

## 24. Put yourself in other actors' position [B]

The policy changes constraints and incentives.

Affected actors may:

- comply
- resist
- substitute
- avoid
- game
- adapt
- reorganise

Do not forecast as if everyone else remains static.

# Part 6 - Know when projection becomes a learning problem

## 25. Do not fabricate the central estimate [U]

If the most decision-sensitive projection rests on a weak assumption that could realistically be tested, stop and ask:

> **Do we need to learn before pretending to forecast this?**

Examples:

- unclear user behaviour
- unknown uptake
- uncertain operational feasibility
- untested mechanism
- transfer from a very different context

Call `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md`.

## 26. Distinguish forecastable uncertainty from learnable uncertainty [U]

Some uncertainty is best represented as a range.

Some can be reduced cheaply through:

- observation
- interviews
- prototype
- live test
- administrative analysis

Some cannot be resolved before acting.

Use the learning method to decide which is which.

Do not force every uncertainty into a forecast.

# Part 7 - Build the outcomes matrix

## 27. Use alternatives as rows and criteria as columns [B]

Each cell should contain the **projected outcome**.

Prefer:

> 8-12% reduction in repeat contact over 12 months; high uncertainty due to uptake.

Avoid:

> ++

where a more informative description is possible.

## 28. Make labels directional [B]

Weak:

> Access

Better:

> Increase successful access among eligible households

The reader should understand what the column means.

## 29. Order criteria by importance where useful [B]

If exact weights are unjustified, ordering can still indicate importance.

Do not imply equal weight merely because each criterion has one column.

## 30. Revise the matrix [B]

Update it when:

- evidence changes
- options change
- criteria change
- a missing side effect appears
- a forecast weakens

The matrix is a working tool.

It is not the analysis itself.

# Projection scaffold

1. **Alternative:** What action are we projecting?
2. **Base case:** Compared with what?
3. **Criterion / outcome:** What consequence matters?
4. **Causal pathway:** How does the intervention produce it?
5. **Starting conditions:** What facts on the ground matter?
6. **Direction:** What changes?
7. **Magnitude:** By how much?
8. **Pace / timing:** How quickly and over what period?
9. **Range:** What plausible uncertainty should be shown?
10. **Evidence:** What supports the forecast?
11. **Outside view:** What happened in comparable cases?
12. **Assumptions:** What has to be true?
13. **Feedback / path dependence:** Could the effect reinforce, stall or lock in?
14. **Actor response:** How could behaviour change?
15. **Side effects:** What foreseeable harm or spillover matters?
16. **Break-even / switchpoint:** What value changes the decision?
17. **Sensitivity:** Which assumption is most fragile?
18. **Learning check:** Does a material uncertainty need testing rather than guessing?
19. **Matrix:** Record the final projection.

→ **Projection statement:** `[alternative]` is expected to produce `[outcome]` of roughly `[magnitude/range]` over `[time]` compared with `[base case]`, because `[mechanism]`. The estimate is supported by `[evidence/reference class]`, depends most on `[assumption]`, and would materially change if `[switchpoint or uncertainty]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Intervention equals outcome

**Failure:** "create service" becomes "service improves outcome."

**Challenge:** project the actual consequence.

## Pitfall B - Direction without magnitude

**Failure:** "will reduce demand."

**Challenge:** how much, when and compared with what?

## Pitfall C - Precise forecast from weak assumptions

**Failure:** one exact figure masks fragile evidence.

**Challenge:** expose range and model uncertainty.

## Pitfall D - Inside view only

**Failure:** the forecast is based entirely on the current plan.

**Challenge:** use comparable real outcomes where available.

## Pitfall E - Frozen base case

**Failure:** the option is compared with today rather than the likely future without it.

**Challenge:** project business-as-usual too.

## Pitfall F - Linear forecast of a dynamic system

**Failure:** feedbacks, scale and path dependence are ignored.

**Challenge:** include them where they can alter the decision.

## Pitfall G - Actors remain passive

**Failure:** policy changes incentives but behaviour is assumed unchanged.

**Challenge:** project response.

## Pitfall H - Foreseeable harm becomes surprise

**Failure:** gaming or burden shifting appears later as "unanticipated".

**Challenge:** include it now.

## Pitfall I - Uncertainty disappears in the matrix

**Failure:** a wide range becomes one score.

**Challenge:** preserve uncertainty in the cell.

## Pitfall J - Guessing instead of learning

**Failure:** a central estimate is invented where a small test could resolve the critical uncertainty.

**Challenge:** call the learning method.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- Serious alternatives have projected consequences, not only intentions.
- The base case is explicit.
- Important projections have a visible causal pathway.
- Evidence and assumptions are distinguishable.
- Direction, magnitude and time are stated where reasonably possible.
- Precision does not exceed the knowledge.
- Material uncertainty remains visible.
- Foreseeable implementation failure and side effects are considered.
- Preferred options have not been given systematically more favourable assumptions.
- A decision-sensitive projection that is too weak has triggered a learning check rather than fabrication.

## Should pass

- A relevant reference class has been used where one exists.
- Feedbacks and path dependence have been considered where material.
- Option interaction has been considered.
- Break-even or switchpoint analysis has been used where useful.
- Sensitivity has identified the assumptions that matter most.
- The outcomes matrix contains informative projections rather than unexplained scores.

# Shared method calls

## Call `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- reference-class forecasting matters
- system feedbacks or path dependence matter
- several options interact
- pace, accumulation or systemic effects matter
- model uncertainty exceeds the statistical range

## Call `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- a decision-sensitive assumption could be reduced through evidence or testing
- transfer is too uncertain to support a responsible forecast
- implementation or behavioural response is unknown enough to change the choice

## Call `evidence-guidance.md` when:

- the evidentiary basis of a projection is weak or disputed
- assumptions are being treated as facts
- source relevance or transfer needs deeper assessment

# One-line reference

> Good outcome projection states what each serious option is expected to cause compared with a realistic base case, explains the mechanism and evidence, estimates direction, magnitude and pace without false precision, challenges the forecast with real-world comparators and system effects, and turns an ungrounded forecast into a learning question rather than a guess.
