# Storytelling - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main storytelling spine and adds the UCL Personal Policy Problem memo as a specific output format. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Eight - used for the Grandma Bessie test, audience, medium, narrative structure, serious alternatives, recommendation, objections, compression, background discipline and the distinction between doing analysis and telling its story. **[P]** UCL Personal Policy Problem instructions, adapted from Policy Design and Delivery at HKS - used as a format contract for a concise professional policy memo containing Issue, Stakeholders, Analysis, Criteria, Options, Recommendation, Risks and Uncertainties, and Implementation. The UCL instructions say the memo will typically use around 2 pages, with endnotes outside the page count, and may be accompanied by a 1-page cover letter in the course setting. (Archived at `../../sources/ucl-ppc-one-pager-instructions.pdf`; this file is the canonical method, and PPC memo mode paraphrases the format rather than reproducing it, so skills cite this file rather than the PDF.) **[V]** `../../writing/anti-ai-writing-style.md` - used as the separate output-quality layer for voice, rhythm, formatting and known AI writing habits. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user *turn mature policy reasoning into something another person can understand, use and decide from*. The analytical methods stay upstream. This method decides what the audience needs to see, how the reasoning should be ordered, and which output format fits the task.

## How to use this document

When asked to turn policy analysis into a memo, briefing, note or concise policy story:

1. **Check that the analysis is mature enough to tell.**
2. **Name the audience and the decision or action the output should support.**
3. **Apply the Grandma Bessie test.**
4. **Choose the output mode.**
5. **Build the story around the audience's decision rather than the analytical workflow.**
6. **Use only the evidence, alternatives, projections, trade-offs and recommendation that matter to that story.**
7. **Keep observed evidence, future projection, uncertainty and judgment distinguishable.**
8. **Make the recommendation or decision point easy to find.**
9. **Use the PPC memo structure when the user asks for that format or when a compact professional policy memo is the right output.**
10. **Apply the writing-quality layer after the analytical structure is sound.**
11. **Return to the owning analytical method if the story exposes a missing argument.**

Good policy writing compresses the analysis without flattening it.

# Part 1 - Check there is a story to tell

## 1. Do not use writing to repair unfinished analysis [B]

Before drafting, check whether the case can answer:

- What is the problem?
- What decision is being made?
- What serious alternatives remain?
- What is each expected to cause?
- What trade-off matters most?
- What does the current analysis recommend?
- What uncertainty could still change that answer?

If several of those are missing, the output is not ready for polish.

Return to the relevant method.

A confident tone cannot repair a missing causal argument or a fake option set.

## 2. Apply the Grandma Bessie test [B]

Explain the analysis in plain language to an intelligent person who knows little about the issue.

In roughly 1 minute, they should be able to understand:

- the problem
- why it matters
- the choice
- the answer
- why that answer follows

If the explanation needs a long institutional history or several minutes of technical language, identify what is still unclear.

The short version is a test of reasoning.

It is also the core of the eventual summary.

## 3. Keep a short spoken version available [B]

Be able to answer:

> What have you been working on, and what does the analysis say?

in a short conversation.

This helps test whether the main claim survives compression.

Do not turn it into a slogan.

# Part 2 - Start with audience and purpose

## 4. Identify the real audience [B]

Ask:

- Who will read or hear this?
- Who has to decide?
- Who may advise that person?
- What do they already know?
- What are they likely to challenge?
- What do they need to know before they can act?
- Could the output be forwarded or quoted without the analyst present?

The same analysis may need different treatment for:

- a minister
- a senior official
- a board
- a technical group
- delivery partners
- affected groups
- a public audience

Do not assume the person who commissioned the analysis is the only audience that matters.

## 5. Name the job of the output

A policy output may need to:

- secure a decision
- recommend a course of action
- present a decision-ready comparison
- explain an emerging issue
- brief someone before a meeting
- record why a decision was made
- identify what needs further work

Complete:

> **After reading this, the audience should be able to `[understand / decide / approve / challenge / act]`.**

If that sentence is vague, the document will probably be vague too.

## 6. Think about how the output may travel [B]

Ask:

- Which sentence might be quoted alone?
- Which caveat would be dangerous to lose?
- Which term could be misunderstood?
- Does the recommendation still make sense outside the immediate conversation?
- Does a second audience need a different version?

Do not manipulate evidence because a document may travel.

Write so that the core reasoning can survive being repeated by someone else.

# Part 3 - Choose the output mode

## 7. Use a policy story when the user needs the reasoning more than a fixed template

A policy story is a flexible narrative for explaining:

- the problem
- the choice
- the serious alternatives
- their expected effects
- the central trade-off
- the recommendation or decision point
- the main uncertainty

This can become:

- a briefing note
- a short narrative
- an email
- speaking notes
- a presentation structure
- a decision summary

Use the audience's needs to decide the order.

Do not reproduce the Eightfold Path as headings.

## 8. Use the PPC memo mode when a compact professional memo is required [P]

The UCL Personal Policy Problem format contains:

1. Issue
2. Stakeholders
3. Analysis
4. Criteria
5. Options
6. Recommendation
7. Risks and Uncertainties
8. Implementation

The UCL instructions say the memo should typically fit around 2 pages. Endnotes sit outside that page total. In the course setting, a 1-page cover letter can add context about the authoriser or drafter. [P]

Treat those as format rules for this mode.

Do not treat the section order as the analytical workflow the user must have followed.

## 9. Use another format only when the user or context requires it

A submission, ministerial advice note, board paper or presentation may impose another structure.

Use that structure when known.

The storytelling principles still apply:

- audience first
- problem early
- serious choices only
- evidence tied to claims
- trade-off visible
- recommendation locatable
- uncertainty preserved

Do not force the PPC headings onto a format that has its own contract.

# Part 4 - Build the core policy story

## 10. Open with the issue [B][P]

The audience should quickly understand what requires attention.

The opening should establish, where known:

- what is happening
- who is affected
- scale or direction
- why it warrants attention
- what decision follows

For PPC mode, the **Issue** section is the concise problem definition and may be only a few words or a few sentences depending on how much the decision maker already knows. [P]

Do not begin with a long history.

## 11. Use background only when it changes the decision [B]

Include history or context when it explains:

- why the problem exists
- a critical driver
- an institutional constraint
- the expected effect of an option
- why the current decision exists

Cut background that merely proves the analyst did the research.

A generic "Background" section often becomes a storage cupboard for material that has not found a job in the argument.

## 12. Give every section a job [B]

Ask of each paragraph or section:

> **What question does this answer that the audience now needs answered?**

If it has no clear job:

- cut it
- move it to supporting material
- combine it with the section it actually supports

Do not make the reader construct the argument from a set of accurate but disconnected facts.

## 13. Use only serious alternatives [B]

The final story should normally show the small number of options still worth considering.

Do not carry forward:

- exploratory ideas
- decoys
- obviously infeasible options
- cosmetic variants

If business-as-usual remains a serious choice, include it.

Compare the recommendation with the strongest realistic competitor.

## 14. Tie evidence to the claim it supports [B]

Avoid evidence dumps.

For each important factual claim, ask:

> **What evidence establishes this, and why does the audience need it here?**

Use citations or endnotes where the format requires them.

Do not make the reader infer why a statistic appears.

## 15. Preserve evidence, projection and judgment as different things [B]

The story should allow the reader to tell the difference between:

### Evidence

What is observed or established.

### Projection

What is expected to happen.

### Uncertainty

What remains unknown or contested.

### Judgment

Why the analysis prefers one course over another.

Compression must not turn:

> evidence suggests

into:

> will happen.

## 16. Make the central trade-off visible [B]

The audience should be able to answer:

> **What do we gain, and what do we give up?**

Do not bury the main exchange inside a dense options table.

If one value judgment decides the choice, state it.

## 17. Make the recommendation easy to find [B][P]

Where a recommendation is required, state:

- what should happen
- the main reason
- any condition capable of changing the answer

For PPC mode, the Recommendation section should be short and direct. [P]

Do not make the reader infer the recommendation from the final paragraph.

## 18. Address the strongest serious objection [B]

Ask:

> **What would the most credible critic of this recommendation say?**

Then:

- state the concern fairly
- address the evidence
- explain whether it changes the recommendation
- preserve what remains unresolved

Do not add a collection of weak objections for the appearance of balance.

# Part 5 - PPC memo mode

Use this section when the user asks for a PPC/PPP memo or another compact professional policy memo where this structure fits.

## 19. Issue [P]

Purpose:

> Give the decision maker the concise problem definition.

Include only what they need to understand the issue.

Depending on familiarity, this can range from a short phrase to a few sentences.

Good Issue content answers:

- What is wrong or at stake?
- For whom?
- How large or consequential is it?
- Why is a decision needed?

Do not turn Issue into background history.

## 20. Stakeholders [P]

Purpose:

> Give the reader a practical grasp of the political and institutional actor system.

The UCL format specifically asks for prominent stakeholders and concise analysis of their:

- motivations
- beliefs
- resources

If the decision maker has an important interest that is not obvious, include it without turning the section into lobbying. [P]

Use `stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` for the underlying analysis.

In the memo, compress it to what changes political feasibility or the decision.

## 21. Analysis [P]

Purpose:

> Explain the critical drivers and dynamics of the issue before comparing options.

This section is separate from the option analysis.

Include:

- critical causes or mechanisms
- system dynamics that matter
- main risks or opportunities already visible
- evidence needed to understand why the problem persists

Adjust depth to what the decision maker already knows.

The UCL instructions explicitly warn against filling this section with background the reader already knows. [P]

## 22. Criteria [P]

Purpose:

> State the compact set of standards that will make the later comparison intelligible.

Include:

- the core policy goal
- material legal, financial or technical feasibility
- material political feasibility
- other decision-relevant values

Keep the list compact.

Do not copy the whole criteria analysis into the memo.

The criteria section should prepare the reader to understand the pros and cons of the options.

## 23. Options [P]

Purpose:

> Compare the serious courses of action.

The UCL instructions say professional practice varies in how many options are appropriate. The standard often moves towards 3 distinct options and normally includes some form of continuing current policy. [P]

Our alternatives method adds an important qualification:

**Do not manufacture 3 options merely to satisfy a convention.**

For each serious option, show:

- what it is
- the main mechanism
- projected outcome
- important advantage
- important disadvantage
- how it performs against the criteria

Use descriptive option titles.

Do not use titles that assume success.

## 24. Recommendation [P]

Purpose:

> Tell the decision maker what the analysis recommends and why.

Keep it short.

State:

- recommended option
- decisive rationale
- material condition if required

Do not repeat the full options section.

## 25. Risks and Uncertainties [P]

Purpose:

> Tell the decision maker what remains capable of changing the case.

The UCL instructions suggest reflecting on:

- value
- operational capacity
- political feasibility

when identifying the important unknowns. They also ask what evidence or method would address those unknowns in the next iteration. [P]

Use judgment.

Do not make every part of the recommendation sound unknowable.

Do not present confidence beyond what the analysis can support.

A useful entry states:

> `[uncertainty]` matters because `[decision consequence]`. We would reduce it through `[evidence / test / review condition]`.

## 26. Implementation [P]

Purpose:

> Tell the decision maker what should happen if they agree.

The UCL format asks for:

- who
- what
- when
- where

The actions should build from the recommendation rather than restate the options analysis. [P]

Early-stage memos may give more space to de-risking and uncertainty.

Later-stage memos may give more space to implementation. [P]

Only include implementation actions supported by the preceding analysis.

Do not invent delivery detail merely to fill the section.

## 27. Use the page limit as a decision about priority [P]

The UCL instructions describe memo writing as a craft and say sections may be longer, shorter or omitted depending on the circumstances. The typical memo has finite space, around 2 pages. [P]

That means the writer must decide what the audience most needs.

Do not make every section the same length.

A 2-page memo is not a shrunken 20-page report.

# Part 6 - Compress without damaging the analysis

## 28. Cut the research diary [B]

The reader usually does not need:

- every source consulted
- every rejected option
- every version of the problem
- every meeting
- every analytical dead end

Keep what establishes:

- the problem
- mechanism
- important evidence
- options
- consequences
- trade-off
- recommendation
- uncertainty

Use supporting material for the rest if needed.

## 29. Cut repetition before cutting reasoning [B]

If the document is too long, remove:

- duplicated claims
- repeated context
- several examples proving the same point
- restated option descriptions
- repeated caveats

Protect:

- causal logic
- important magnitude
- trade-off
- material uncertainty
- recommendation rationale

Do not cut the one sentence that explains why the evidence supports the conclusion.

## 30. Qualify where it matters [B]

Repeated caveats can make the argument unreadable.

Use calibrated wording:

- likely
- roughly
- current evidence suggests
- within the tested setting
- conditional on

Then place the fuller uncertainty where it belongs.

Do not scatter the same caveat through every paragraph.

## 31. Use tables only when they reduce work for the reader [B]

A table is useful when it makes comparison easier.

It is weak when:

- it needs a long explanation
- cell labels are vague
- magnitude disappears
- uncertainty disappears
- the central trade-off becomes harder to see

Do not use a matrix because policy memos are expected to look analytical.

# Part 7 - Apply the writing-quality layer

## 32. Apply `../../writing/anti-ai-writing-style.md` after the policy structure is sound [V]

The writing file is the source of truth for voice and surface quality.

It requires, among other things:

- short paragraphs
- direct language
- active voice
- natural contractions
- numbers as digits
- no em dash
- varied sentence length
- restrained formatting
- concrete detail
- no mechanical transitions
- no AI filler or hype
- no negative reframe constructions in published prose
- no meta commentary about what the writer is about to say [V]

Do not copy the full style rules into this method.

Call the style file as the final output check.

## 33. Let analytical terminology survive where it earns its place

Plain language does not require stripping out every policy term.

Keep technical language when:

- it is precise
- the audience knows it
- replacing it would take more words
- the concept itself matters

Explain or replace jargon that makes the reader do unnecessary work.

## 34. Avoid institutional prose

Prefer:

> DVLA would change the rule.

over:

> A change to the rule would be undertaken by DVLA.

Prefer:

> The evidence is weak.

over:

> There are limitations in relation to the available evidence base.

Use the people and organisations doing the work as subjects where possible.

## 35. Stop when the point is made [V]

Do not add a closing paragraph that repeats the recommendation and summary for a third time.

If the document has landed the argument, end.

# Part 8 - Return upstream when the story breaks

## 36. A weak Issue section may be a problem-framing problem

Call `problem-definition-guidance.md` when:

- the issue cannot be stated plainly
- several levels of problem are mixed
- the frame contains a hidden solution
- magnitude or public significance is missing

Do not wordsmith around it.

## 37. A weak stakeholder section may be an analysis gap

Call `stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` when:

- motivations are guessed
- influence is unclear
- the authorising environment is missing
- actor relationships matter to feasibility

Do not fill a memo with generic stakeholder labels.

## 38. A weak Analysis section may be an evidence or causal gap

Call:

- `evidence-guidance.md`
- `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md`

when the memo cannot explain:

- what drives the issue
- what is known
- what remains uncertain

Do not use background material to fill the space.

## 39. A weak Criteria or Options section belongs upstream

Call:

- `criteria-guidance.md`
- `alternatives-guidance.md`

when:

- criteria are generic
- options are cosmetic variants
- there is a straw alternative
- business-as-usual is missing without reason

Do not repair the memo by inventing sharper labels.

## 40. A weak recommendation may be an outcomes, trade-off or decision problem

Call:

- `outcomes-guidance.md`
- `trade-offs-guidance.md`
- `decision-guidance.md`

when the writer cannot say:

- what the options are expected to cause
- what the central exchange is
- why the preferred option wins
- what could change the answer

Writing is the final test of the analytical chain.

# Story-building scaffold

1. **Audience:** Who is this for?
2. **Purpose:** What should they understand or decide?
3. **Format:** Policy story / PPC memo / specified external format.
4. **Grandma Bessie version:** What is the 1-minute explanation?
5. **Issue:** What requires attention?
6. **Decision:** What choice is in front of the reader?
7. **Necessary context:** What does the reader need that they do not already know?
8. **Serious alternatives:** Which options remain?
9. **Evidence:** Which facts are needed to understand the comparison?
10. **Projection:** What is each option expected to cause?
11. **Trade-off:** What is gained and sacrificed?
12. **Recommendation:** What should happen and why?
13. **Contrary case:** What is the strongest serious challenge?
14. **Uncertainty:** What could materially change the recommendation?
15. **Action:** What happens next if the reader agrees?
16. **Compression:** What can be cut without weakening the decision?
17. **Voice check:** Apply `../../writing/anti-ai-writing-style.md`.
18. **Upstream check:** Does any weak section reveal unfinished analysis?

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - The Eightfold Path becomes the table of contents

**Failure:** the document tells the story of the analytical method.

**Challenge:** organise around what the audience needs to understand the decision.

## Pitfall B - Background dump

**Failure:** history and context arrive before the reader knows why they matter.

**Challenge:** keep only context that changes the present analysis.

## Pitfall C - Evidence dump

**Failure:** facts and citations appear without a claim.

**Challenge:** attach evidence to reasoning.

## Pitfall D - Every option from the working process survives

**Failure:** the reader has to work through exploratory and weak choices.

**Challenge:** show serious options only.

## Pitfall E - Recommendation hidden

**Failure:** the reader cannot quickly tell what the analyst proposes.

**Challenge:** state it directly.

## Pitfall F - Uncertainty stripped out for force

**Failure:** a tentative projection becomes certain prose.

**Challenge:** preserve the uncertainty that matters to the choice.

## Pitfall G - Caveats swamp the argument

**Failure:** every sentence carries a qualification.

**Challenge:** state the calibrated main claim, then group the important uncertainty.

## Pitfall H - PPC becomes 8 equal boxes

**Failure:** each section gets the same space regardless of what the decision needs.

**Challenge:** use finite space according to the case.

## Pitfall I - Three options are manufactured for the PPC template

**Failure:** the memo adds a decoy because 3 is conventional.

**Challenge:** use the number of serious options the analysis supports.

## Pitfall J - Implementation repeats the option

**Failure:** the section says the same thing again with verbs added.

**Challenge:** state who does what, when and where.

## Pitfall K - Risks section becomes a disclaimer

**Failure:** everything is described as uncertain.

**Challenge:** select the few unknowns capable of changing the recommendation.

## Pitfall L - Writing polish hides a gap

**Failure:** elegant prose conceals a weak mechanism, fake trade-off or unsupported recommendation.

**Challenge:** return upstream.

## Pitfall M - AI voice enters the policy artefact

**Failure:** filler, hype, mechanical transitions, repetitive structure or generic machine phrasing weaken the writing.

**Challenge:** run the separate writing-quality check before returning the output.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The audience and purpose are clear.
- The output mode is explicit.
- The story passes the Grandma Bessie test.
- The issue appears early enough for the reader to understand why the work matters.
- The decision or recommendation is clear.
- Only serious alternatives receive substantial attention.
- Evidence is tied to claims.
- Projection remains distinguishable from established evidence.
- The central trade-off is visible.
- Material uncertainty remains visible.
- The recommendation follows from the underlying analysis.
- The preferred option is compared with the strongest credible alternative where a comparison is needed.
- The strongest serious objection has not been ignored.
- The final structure does not mechanically narrate the Eightfold Path.
- A weak analytical section has not been repaired through invented prose.
- The writing-quality layer has been applied.

## PPC mode must also pass

- Issue is concise and suited to what the decision maker already knows.
- Stakeholders includes motivations, beliefs and resources at a proportionate level.
- Analysis explains critical drivers rather than generic background.
- Criteria is compact enough to prepare the option comparison.
- The Options section projects outcomes and relates pros and cons to the criteria.
- The number of options reflects the real choice rather than a fixed convention.
- Recommendation is short and easy to locate.
- Risks and Uncertainties focuses on decision-relevant unknowns and what to do about them.
- Implementation states who, what, when and where where that detail is known.
- Space is allocated according to the stage and decision rather than evenly across sections.

## Should pass

- Technical detail is proportionate to the audience.
- Tables reduce the reader's work.
- Supporting detail has been moved out of the core story where appropriate.
- The document can be repeated accurately by another person.
- The output is short enough for the audience and format.
- Citations or endnotes follow the requested format.
- The ending stops once the decision and next action have landed.

- political feasibility cannot be summarised responsibly

- the story describes interventions but cannot say what they are expected to cause

- the comparison lacks a clear statement of what is gained and sacrificed

- the analysis cannot state a recommendation, accepted trade-off or review condition

- a compact value, capacity and required-support readout helps explain risks or viability

## Analysis methods

The upstream files own the reasoning.

- selection
- ordering

- audience fit
- format
- final writing quality

## PPC format

The PPC structure is an **output mode**.

It is not a replacement for:

- problem definition
- stakeholder analysis
- evidence work
- criteria selection
- alternative construction
- outcome projection
- trade-offs
- decision

## Writing style

`../../writing/anti-ai-writing-style.md` owns the voice and surface rules.

This method tells the agent **when to apply them**.

It does not duplicate the full writing specification.

## Evaluation

`agent-evaluation-guidance.md` owns evaluation of whether the agent told the story well.

A polished memo and a satisfied user do not by themselves prove that the underlying policy analysis was sound.

# One-line reference

> Good policy storytelling starts with the audience and decision, compresses mature analysis around the problem, serious choices, expected consequences, central trade-off and recommendation, uses a format such as the PPC memo when that helps the reader act, and sends weak reasoning back upstream instead of hiding it under polished prose.
