# Evidence - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main policy-analysis spine and adds only the complementary material that changes how the agent should behave. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Two and Part II - used for the distinction between data, information and evidence; the purposes of evidence gathering; value-of-information reasoning; educated guesses; research strategy; interviews; disagreement; access; timing and credibility. **[S]** Andrea Saltelli and Mario Giampietro, *What is wrong with evidence based policy, and how can it be improved?* (2017) - used for the risk that evidence work deepens one framing while excluding alternatives, the idea of "uncomfortable knowledge", scrutiny of models and quantification, and the distinction between evidence informing a decision and evidence being recruited to justify a settled position. **[U]** `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` - the canonical shared method for critical uncertainty, competing explanations, choosing a learning move, experimentation and decisions about what to learn next. **[R]** `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called selectively for the outside view, reference-class forecasting, forecast bias, model uncertainty and false precision. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user work out *what evidence bears on the decision, what is known versus inferred or assumed, how strong and applicable that evidence is, what important perspectives or frames may be missing, and whether any further evidence gathering is worth doing*. This method does not assume that more evidence is always better.

## How to use this document

When asked to assess an evidence base, challenge a claim, plan research, or decide what needs to be learned next:

1. **Name the decision or claim.** Evidence is evidence *for something*.
2. **Map the current evidence position.** Separate data, information, evidence, inference, assumption, value judgment and unknown.
3. **Check the framing.** Ask whether the current problem frame is determining what counts as relevant evidence and excluding another plausible reading.
4. **Assess the evidence against the claim it is being used to support.**
5. **Look for disagreement, missing perspectives and uncomfortable knowledge.**
6. **Identify the decision-sensitive uncertainty.**
7. **Ask whether resolving it is worth the cost.**
8. **Choose the next evidence move proportionately.** If active learning or experimentation is needed, call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md`.
9. **Use forecast-specific checks where relevant.** If an important claim concerns future costs, benefits or uptake, call the relevant parts of `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md`.
10. **Stop when more evidence is unlikely to improve the decision enough to justify its cost.**

The goal is not a large evidence base. It is a **better-founded decision**.

# Part 1 - Establish what the evidence is for

## 1. Start with the decision or claim [B]

Do not begin with:

> What research should we do?

Begin with:

> **What decision, belief, projection or policy claim are we trying to inform?**

Examples:

- How large is the problem?
- What is causing it?
- Which group is most affected?
- Will this intervention work here?
- Can the organisation implement it?
- What is likely to happen if nothing changes?
- Which option is preferable?
- Does evidence from somewhere else apply in this setting?

If the evidence request has no clear analytical purpose, clarify it before gathering more material.

## 2. Keep data, information and evidence distinct [B]

Bardach uses these terms differently.

### Data

Facts, or representations of facts, about the world.

These can be:

- quantitative
- qualitative
- documentary
- observational
- organisational
- behavioural
- institutional

### Information

Data that have been organised or interpreted so they have meaning.

### Evidence

Information that bears on an important belief about:

- the problem
- its causes
- the operating context
- a policy option
- a projected outcome
- implementation
- another decision-relevant claim

A report does not become evidence merely because it contains statistics.

Ask:

> **What belief should this information strengthen, weaken or change?**

## 3. Separate the current evidence position into distinct kinds of statement

For important claims, label what you currently have.

### Evidence

An observation, source, measurement or documented fact that bears directly on the claim.

### Inference

A conclusion drawn from evidence.

Example:

> Evidence: repeat contact is concentrated in the first 14 days after application.  
> Inference: uncertainty immediately after application may be contributing to contact.

### Assumption

Something the analysis currently relies on without enough direct evidence.

Example:

> Applicants will read the confirmation message.

### Value judgment

A judgment about what is desirable, fair, acceptable or worth prioritising.

Example:

> A small increase in administrative cost is acceptable if it materially reduces uncertainty for applicants.

### Unknown

Something important that has not yet been established.

Do not allow an inference or assumption to become a "fact" simply because it has been repeated through several drafts.

## 4. Use evidence for one of Bardach's main purposes [B]

Evidence gathering commonly serves 3 jobs.

### Understand the problem

- nature
- scale
- distribution
- trend
- symptoms
- relevant history

### Understand the concrete setting for action

- workloads
- budgets
- behaviour
- institutional arrangements
- organisational capability
- political attitudes
- relationships
- legal or administrative conditions

### Learn from policies or practices elsewhere

- what has been tried
- what happened
- under what conditions
- what appears transferable

The purpose changes as the analysis develops.

Do not gather "more evidence" without knowing which of these jobs it is meant to do.

# Part 2 - Check the frame before deepening the evidence

## 5. Ask what framing makes this evidence relevant [S]

Saltelli and Giampietro argue that evidence work is shaped by a prior framing of the problem.

Once one frame is selected:

- certain variables become important
- certain sources become relevant
- certain indicators are chosen
- certain models appear natural
- other interpretations can disappear from view

Before investing heavily in one evidence programme, ask:

> **What problem framing is this evidence programme assuming?**

Then:

> **What would become relevant if the problem were framed differently?**

This is not a requirement to keep every possible frame alive forever.

It is a check against mistaking one analytical lens for the problem itself.

## 6. Look for plausible alternative framings [S]

For a material claim, ask whether another credible framing could lead to:

- different evidence
- different affected groups
- a different causal explanation
- different options
- different measures of success

Example:

A high level of repeat customer contact might be framed as:

- poor customer understanding
- poor communication
- poor service visibility
- excessive processing time
- rational reassurance-seeking under uncertainty
- a mismatch between the service's process and users' expectations

Each frame directs evidence gathering differently.

Do not research one explanation deeply before checking whether a materially different explanation remains plausible.

## 7. Seek uncomfortable knowledge [S]

Saltelli and Giampietro use the idea of **uncomfortable knowledge** for information or perspectives that sit awkwardly with the dominant framing or institutional story.

Ask:

- What evidence would be inconvenient for the current explanation?
- What is known by people outside the dominant analytical group?
- What evidence is being excluded because it does not fit the preferred model?
- Which affected group sees the issue differently?
- What result would weaken the preferred intervention?

Do not treat uncomfortable evidence as automatically correct.

Treat its exclusion as something that needs a reason.

## 8. Watch for policy-based evidence [S]

Evidence gathering stops being genuinely informative when the desired policy is already fixed and evidence is being selected mainly to justify it.

A warning sign is:

> **No plausible finding would change the proposed course of action.**

Ask:

- What evidence would weaken the current view?
- What finding would cause redesign?
- What finding would cause the team to stop?
- Are disconfirming sources being sought as seriously as supporting ones?

If the answer is "nothing could change the recommendation", describe the exercise accurately as advocacy, documentation or implementation support rather than open evidence gathering.

# Part 3 - Judge evidence against the claim

## 9. Ask whether the evidence actually answers the question

For each important item of evidence:

- What claim is it being used to support?
- Does it observe that claim directly?
- Or is there an inferential step?
- How large is that inferential step?
- What other explanation could fit the same observation?

Do not grade evidence abstractly as "strong" or "weak".

Evidence can be excellent for one claim and poor for another.

## 10. Distinguish relevance from authority

A prestigious or technically sophisticated source can still be poorly matched to the current question.

Ask:

- Is the source studying the same outcome?
- The same population?
- The same intervention?
- The same mechanism?
- A sufficiently similar institutional setting?
- A relevant time period?
- The decision-relevant scale?

Do not allow source prestige to substitute for applicability.

## 11. Treat transfer as a claim that needs evidence [U]

Evidence that something happened elsewhere establishes what happened **there**.

Using it here requires another claim:

> **The conditions that produced the result are similar enough for the lesson to travel.**

Check differences in:

- population
- place
- institutions
- incentives
- delivery model
- scale
- capability
- prior exposure
- political context
- timing

Use `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` where transfer uncertainty is decision-sensitive.

Do not call something "best practice" as though context were irrelevant.

## 12. Seek disagreement deliberately [B][S]

Bardach warns that sources often arrive bundled with their preferred problem definition and causal story.

Saltelli and Giampietro make the same problem visible at the level of framing.

Seek serious disagreement about:

- the facts
- measurement
- causes
- applicability
- implementation
- expected effects
- what the problem actually is

Where time is short, a useful move is:

> Ask a knowledgeable source who would disagree most strongly with their account, and why.

Disagreement can reveal:

- missing evidence
- hidden assumptions
- competing frames
- uncertain causal links
- political vulnerabilities

Do not create artificial "balance" where evidence is genuinely one-sided.

## 13. Calibrate qualitative evidence [B]

Terms such as:

- difficult
- strong
- common
- serious
- likely
- supportive
- frustrating
- rare

can mean very different things to different people.

Ask:

- What does that mean in observable terms?
- Compared with what?
- How often?
- For whom?
- Over what period?
- What would count as "high" or "low"?

Do not turn an informant's adjective into a precise analytical finding without calibration.

## 14. Treat lived and operational knowledge as evidence for the questions it can answer [B][U]

Frontline staff, affected people and practitioners can provide important evidence about:

- experience
- workflow
- implementation
- informal practice
- constraints
- incentives
- mechanisms
- unrecorded workarounds

They may be less able to establish:

- prevalence across a whole population
- causal effect
- counterfactual outcomes

Use each source for what it can genuinely tell you.

Do not dismiss experiential evidence because it is not experimental.

Do not generalise one experience into a population estimate.

# Part 4 - Use quantitative evidence responsibly

## 15. Ask what the number depends on [S]

A quantitative result is not self-explanatory.

Ask:

- What was measured?
- What was excluded?
- Which assumptions were required?
- Which categories were chosen?
- What model produced the result?
- What happens if a key assumption changes?

Saltelli and Giampietro warn that quantification can create an impression of prediction and control while hiding framing choices and tacit assumptions.

Do not treat the presence of a number as evidence of objectivity.

## 16. Translate important models into plain language [S]

For any model central to the decision, be able to explain:

- what goes in
- what relationship is assumed
- what comes out
- what conditions must hold for the result to be meaningful

Ask:

> **What does this model believe about the world?**

If the answer cannot be stated intelligibly, do not rely on mathematical complexity as a substitute for scrutiny.

## 17. Check whether the model is being used to explore or to justify [S]

A model can help explore:

- possible effects
- scenarios
- sensitivities
- assumptions

The risk is that the same model becomes proof of the policy it was built to examine.

Ask:

- Was the model used to explore competing possibilities?
- Were alternative assumptions tested?
- Or was it calibrated around one preferred story?

Do not allow the modelling process to displace observation of what happens in reality.

## 18. Match precision to knowledge [S][R]

Do not report more precision than the underlying evidence can support.

Warning signs include:

- many decimal places on uncertain inputs
- one central estimate where the plausible range crosses materially different decisions
- precise probabilities for poorly understood events
- narrow confidence intervals around a model with weak assumptions

If a range or qualitative description better represents the state of knowledge, use it.

For deeper model uncertainty and false-precision checks, call `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md`.

## 19. Distinguish risk from deeper uncertainty where it affects evidence strategy [S][U]

A useful high-level distinction is:

- **risk** - the relevant outcomes and odds are reasonably knowable
- **uncertainty** - important odds are not known
- **ignorance** - relevant unknowns may not yet be recognised
- **indeterminacy** - outcomes depend on open causal chains, behaviour or institutional response

Do not force deeper uncertainty into a risk calculation simply because the analytical template expects a probability.

Use `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` for the full learning response.

# Part 5 - Decide what evidence is worth gathering next

## 20. Think before collecting [B]

Before recommending another:

- dataset
- literature search
- survey
- interview programme
- analysis
- experiment

ask:

> **If the result came out differently from our current expectation, what decision would change?**

If no realistic result would change the analysis, the evidence has low decision value.

## 21. Use value-of-information reasoning [B]

The value of additional evidence increases with:

- the chance that it changes the decision
- the chance that the changed decision produces a better outcome
- the size of the difference between those outcomes

The cost includes:

- time
- money
- access
- delay
- staff effort
- opportunity cost
- political or organisational cost

Do not demand research-grade certainty for every unknown.

An educated guess can be the right analytical move when:

- the issue is low stakes
- the decision is reversible
- the current range would not change the choice
- better evidence is disproportionately expensive

Keep the guess labelled as a guess.

## 22. Identify the critical uncertainty [U]

Where several evidence gaps exist, ask:

> **Which unresolved question is most capable of changing what we should do next?**

Do not create a shopping list of all missing knowledge.

Prioritise the gap that affects:

- the problem frame
- the mechanism
- the option
- the projected outcome
- implementation
- transfer
- the final choice

The full diagnostic belongs in `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md`.

## 23. Choose the lightest credible evidence move [B][U]

Possible next moves include:

- inspect existing data
- retrieve a document
- review literature
- interview one informed person
- observe real practice
- speak to affected people
- compare another jurisdiction
- analyse a natural variation
- prototype
- rapid test
- quasi-experiment
- RCT

Choose the method that fits the uncertainty.

Do not run a large research exercise when one observation or conversation could resolve the question.

Do not use a small qualitative exercise to make a causal claim it cannot support.

## 24. Define what would count before gathering the evidence [U]

For decision-sensitive evidence, state in advance:

- what is being investigated
- what would strengthen the current view
- what would weaken it
- what would remain ambiguous
- what each result would cause the team to do

This guards against finding an interpretation after seeing the result.

If active learning or experiment design is needed, call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md`.

## 25. Know when to stop [B][U]

Evidence gathering can always continue.

Stop when:

- the decision-sensitive uncertainty is sufficiently reduced
- another result is unlikely to change the choice
- the remaining uncertainty is unavoidable
- acting now is proportionate and reversible
- the cost or delay of more evidence exceeds its likely decision value

Do not confuse completeness with quality.

# Part 6 - Build an evidence strategy

## 26. Start with what is already known [B]

Before commissioning anything new, take stock of:

- known facts
- previous analysis
- existing administrative data
- previous evaluations
- frontline knowledge
- relevant practice elsewhere
- unresolved disputes
- assumptions already embedded in plans

Use this first pass to sharpen the questions.

Do not repeat research because the existing evidence has not been organised.

## 27. Search through more than one route [B]

Useful routes include:

- academic literature
- government sources
- audit and oversight bodies
- operational data
- professional bodies
- advocacy organisations
- subject experts
- practitioners
- affected people
- documents that identify people
- people who identify documents
- analogous cases
- other jurisdictions

Accessibility and relevance are not the same thing.

The easiest source can dominate simply because it is easy to find.

## 28. Use analogies cautiously [B]

An analogy can generate:

- a hypothesis
- a policy variable
- a causal mechanism
- a useful source

It can also mislead.

Ask:

> **Which structural feature makes the analogy relevant?**

and:

> **Which difference could break it?**

Do not transfer an intervention simply because the surface problem looks similar.

## 29. Treat access and timing as part of the strategy [B]

Some evidence sources are:

- hard to reach
- politically exposed
- defensive
- available once
- capable of opening other sources

Approach early where the source can:

- identify better evidence
- open access
- explain the field
- prevent wasted research

Consider waiting where:

- the source is hostile and a better-informed conversation will be more useful
- access is scarce
- premature engagement could close doors
- sensitive evidence is unlikely to emerge until incentives change

There is no universal order.

## 30. Protect the credibility of the analysis without turning research into theatre [B]

Where relevant:

- consult serious opposing views
- anticipate which claims will be challenged
- use credible sources for decision-critical claims
- document uncertainty honestly
- ensure affected or operational perspectives have not been ignored

Do not gather prestigious sources simply to decorate an already-supported claim.

Credibility should come from the quality of the reasoning and evidence.

# Part 7 - Forecast-specific evidence checks

Use these only where evidence is being used to estimate a future:

- cost
- duration
- uptake
- benefit
- demand
- implementation performance
- another projected outcome

The full method lives in `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md`.

## 31. Take the outside view where possible [R]

Ask:

> **What actually happened in comparable cases?**

A relevant reference class can challenge an inside-view forecast based mainly on the current plan.

Do not invent a reference class.

State why the chosen cases are comparable.

## 32. Look for systematic optimism or incentives [R]

Ask:

- Have similar forecasts historically been too optimistic?
- Does the forecasting process reward lower costs or higher benefits?
- Is the forecaster invested in approval?
- What evidence justifies expecting this case to outperform comparable ones?

Do not accuse people of strategic misrepresentation without evidence.

Do challenge the forecasting environment.

## 33. Separate uncertainty in the estimate from uncertainty in the model [R][S]

A forecast may have:

- a statistical range
- uncertain assumptions
- an uncertain causal model
- an uncertain operating context

The first does not automatically capture the others.

State what kind of uncertainty the range actually represents.

# Evidence scaffold

Work through these in order where useful.

1. **Decision / claim:** What is this evidence meant to inform?
2. **Current claim:** What do we presently believe?
3. **Evidence:** What directly bears on it?
4. **Inference:** What are we concluding from that evidence?
5. **Assumptions:** What are we relying on without enough evidence?
6. **Value judgments:** What part of the claim is evaluative rather than empirical?
7. **Current frame:** How has the problem been defined?
8. **Alternative frame:** What other plausible reading could change what evidence matters?
9. **Uncomfortable knowledge:** What evidence or perspective could weaken the dominant account?
10. **Source fit:** Does the evidence actually answer this question?
11. **Applicability:** Does it transfer to this population, setting, intervention and scale?
12. **Disagreement:** What serious contrary evidence or interpretation exists?
13. **Quantification check:** What assumptions, model or categories sit beneath the numbers?
14. **Critical uncertainty:** Which evidence gap could most change the decision?
15. **Value of more evidence:** Would resolving it justify the cost and delay?
16. **Next move:** What is the lightest credible way to reduce the uncertainty?
17. **Decision rule:** What would different findings cause us to do?
18. **Stopping point:** When is the evidence position good enough for the next decision?

→ **Evidence position:** We currently believe `[claim]` because `[evidence]`. This requires the inference `[inference]` and depends on `[assumptions]`. The strongest alternative frame or contrary evidence is `[challenge]`. The most decision-sensitive uncertainty is `[uncertainty]`. The next evidence move should be `[move]` because `[reason]`. We should stop gathering when `[stopping condition]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Data accumulation masquerading as evidence

**Failure:** many reports, statistics or interviews with no clear claim they bear on.

**Challenge:** what decision or belief would this change?

## Pitfall B - Evidence becomes a source list

**Failure:** sources are described without stating what each establishes.

**Challenge:** connect every important source to a claim.

## Pitfall C - Assumption becomes fact

**Failure:** an unsupported proposition is repeated until it appears evidential.

**Challenge:** label evidence, inference, assumption and unknown explicitly.

## Pitfall D - One frame defines all relevant evidence

**Failure:** the analysis goes deep on one causal story without testing whether another framing changes the evidence question.

**Challenge:** state at least one plausible alternative frame where the issue is genuinely contested.

## Pitfall E - Policy-based evidence

**Failure:** only supporting evidence is gathered for a decision already made.

**Challenge:** state what evidence could weaken or reverse the current view.

## Pitfall F - Uncomfortable knowledge is excluded

**Failure:** contrary evidence or affected perspectives are dismissed because they do not fit the dominant analytical model.

**Challenge:** explain why the excluded knowledge is irrelevant rather than ignoring it.

## Pitfall G - Prestige substitutes for relevance

**Failure:** a famous paper is treated as decisive despite studying a different population, intervention or context.

**Challenge:** test source fit.

## Pitfall H - "Worked elsewhere" becomes "will work here"

**Failure:** external evidence is transferred without checking contextual conditions.

**Challenge:** make transfer itself a claim.

## Pitfall I - Qualitative evidence over-generalised

**Failure:** one compelling interview becomes a claim about prevalence.

**Challenge:** use the evidence for experience or mechanism unless broader inference is justified.

## Pitfall J - Quantification becomes authority

**Failure:** a precise number is treated as more credible because it is numerical.

**Challenge:** inspect assumptions, frame, model and measurement.

## Pitfall K - Model output displaces reality

**Failure:** the organisation manages the modelled indicator rather than observing the underlying phenomenon.

**Challenge:** ask what real-world observation the model is meant to represent.

## Pitfall L - False precision

**Failure:** exact-looking numbers exceed the quality of the underlying knowledge.

**Challenge:** use a range or calibrated qualitative statement.

## Pitfall M - Every uncertainty becomes risk

**Failure:** probabilities are invented for poorly understood causal chains or unknowns.

**Challenge:** distinguish risk from deeper uncertainty.

## Pitfall N - Researching everything uncertain

**Failure:** the evidence plan becomes a complete map of ignorance.

**Challenge:** identify the critical uncertainty capable of changing the next decision.

## Pitfall O - More research after the answer is stable

**Failure:** additional work is commissioned despite being unlikely to change the choice.

**Challenge:** apply value-of-information reasoning.

## Pitfall P - Experimentation becomes the default evidence move

**Failure:** every gap produces a pilot or RCT.

**Challenge:** choose the lightest method that fits the question; call the uncertainty method where active testing is genuinely useful.

## Pitfall Q - Forecast uses only the inside view

**Failure:** a plan's own assumptions dominate despite relevant evidence from comparable cases.

**Challenge:** seek a reference class.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The evidence work is tied to a **specific decision, claim or projection**.
- Data, information and evidence are not treated as interchangeable.
- Important **inferences, assumptions and value judgments** are visible.
- The current framing has been checked for plausible alternatives where framing could materially change the evidence.
- Important contrary evidence or uncomfortable knowledge has not been silently excluded.
- Evidence is judged against the **claim it is being used to support**.
- External evidence is not assumed to transfer automatically.
- Numerical precision does not exceed the quality of the underlying evidence and model.
- The most decision-sensitive evidence gap is identifiable.
- Further evidence gathering is justified by its likely decision value.
- Active testing is not prescribed automatically.
- The next evidence move has a reason for being proportionate to the uncertainty.
- The method has a stopping point.

## Should pass

- The evidence covers the relevant purpose: problem, operating context and/or learning from practice elsewhere.
- Serious disagreement has been sought where the issue is genuinely contested.
- Qualitative claims have been calibrated where possible.
- Affected and frontline perspectives have been used where they can expose experience, mechanism or implementation evidence.
- Important quantitative results have been translated into their assumptions and model logic.
- Forecasts have used the outside view where a credible reference class exists.
- Bias or incentives around high-stakes forecasts have been considered.
- The analyst can state what evidence would change the current view.
- The evidence strategy has avoided unnecessary cost, delay or research effort.

## Process reminder

- Evidence gathering is iterative.
- Evidence does not arrive independently of a problem frame.
- Strong evidence for one claim may be weak evidence for another.
- More evidence can deepen a bad framing as easily as a good one.
- The objective is enough justified knowledge for the next responsible decision, not exhaustive certainty.

# Shared method calls

This file owns the **evidence position**.

It should call shared methods rather than reproduce them.

## Call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- several competing explanations remain live
- the key evidence gap concerns problem, mechanism, constraint, option, implementation, effect or transfer uncertainty
- the user needs to identify a critical uncertainty
- active inquiry, prototype or experiment may be the best next move
- the team needs a hypothesis, evidence plan or decision rule
- the question is whether to learn more or act now

Do not reproduce the full experimentation method here.

## Call `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- an important claim is a forecast of cost, duration, uptake or benefit
- reference-class forecasting could challenge an inside-view estimate
- forecast incentives or systematic optimism matter
- model uncertainty is larger than the statistical range implies
- numerical precision is masking deeper uncertainty

Do not reproduce the full appraisal method here.

## Call `strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- an evidence gap concerns whether a proposition creates public value
- an operational-capacity claim is assumption-heavy
- a political-feasibility claim lacks evidence about the actors whose support is required

The triangle identifies the dimension.

This evidence method investigates the claim.

# Canonical boundaries across the method library

## Problem

`problem-definition-guidance.md` owns the working problem statement.

Evidence may challenge the frame, but should not silently redefine the problem.

## Stakeholders

`stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` owns motivations, beliefs, resources, power and relationships.

This method owns the quality and confidence of the evidence used to make those stakeholder claims.

## Alternatives

`alternatives-guidance.md` owns option generation.

Evidence may show that an option is implausible or inspire another mechanism, but should not score or choose the option.

## Criteria

`criteria-guidance.md` owns what matters and what counts as better.

Evidence can inform facts relevant to a criterion.

It cannot convert a value judgment into an empirical one.

## Outcomes

`outcomes-guidance.md` owns the projection of future effects.

This method owns the evidentiary basis and confidence underneath those projections.

## Trade-offs

`trade-offs-guidance.md` owns the exchange between outcomes.

Evidence informs the magnitude and uncertainty of the exchange.

## Decision

`decision-guidance.md` owns the recommendation.

Evidence informs the choice but does not remove accountable judgment.

## Evaluation

`agent-evaluation-guidance.md` owns evaluation of the AI agent.

Do not use this evidence method as a substitute for agent evaluation.

# One-line reference

> Good evidence work starts with the decision or claim, separates what is observed from what is inferred or assumed, checks whether the current frame is excluding relevant knowledge, judges evidence by how well it bears on the question and context, and gathers more only when reducing a decision-sensitive uncertainty is worth the cost.
