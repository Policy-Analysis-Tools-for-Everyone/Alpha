# Stakeholder Analysis — Agent Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method uses Eugene Bardach as the policy-analysis spine and deliberately deepens it with four complementary sources. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition — especially Appendix C on public and nonprofit institutions and Appendix D on the dynamics of gathering political support, plus stakeholder-relevant guidance elsewhere in the book. Bardach does **not** present stakeholder analysis as a standalone Eightfold Path step. **[W]** David L. Weimer and Aidan R. Vining, *Policy Analysis: Concepts and Practice*, Fifth Edition, pp. 274–279 — especially the practical political-feasibility questions and the `Actors → Motivations → Beliefs → Resources` worksheet. **[F]** Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, *Getting to Yes*, Second Edition, Chapters 1–3 — especially separating people from the problem and distinguishing underlying interests from stated positions. **[P]** Brian S. Mandell, Stephen Petraeus, Guhan Subramanian and Lisa Cox, “Eight Types of Power City Leaders Can Use When Negotiating” (2022) — used to deepen the analysis of political resources and power. **[R]** Esko Reinikainen, “Understanding Government as a Complex System through Network Mapping” (2020), using the supplied transcript — used to add a relationship and network layer. The transcript is auto-generated, so this document uses it for concepts and method rather than precise quotation. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user *identify who matters to a policy problem or proposal, understand what sits underneath their current position, assess what power and resources they actually possess, see how actors relate to one another as a system, and reason about how political support or opposition may change over time*. This is not a framework tour. It is a working method for producing an evidence-aware stakeholder analysis.

## How to use this document

When asked to understand stakeholders around a policy problem, proposal, decision, or implementation challenge:

1. **Anchor the analysis.** State what problem, proposition, decision, or change the actors are reacting to.
2. **Identify the relevant actors broadly.** Include substantive interests, formal decision makers, affected groups, implementers, influential outsiders, and internal factions where relevant.
3. **Build the Weimer & Vining worksheet.** For each serious actor, record `Actor → Motivations → Beliefs → Resources`.
4. **Look beneath stated positions.** Use *Getting to Yes* to distinguish positions from the interests, needs, concerns, fears, and constituencies that may be producing them.
5. **Interrogate power.** Replace vague labels such as “high power” with the actual resources and types of power the actor can exercise in this case.
6. **Identify the arena.** Ask where the relevant decision is really made and what formal and informal rules operate there.
7. **Map relationships.** Look beyond the organisation chart to who actually communicates, influences, trusts, connects, blocks, brokers, or carries information across boundaries.
8. **Move from static to dynamic analysis.** Identify supporters, active allies, opponents, uncommitted interests, sequencing, likely countermobilisation, and changing conditions.
9. **Keep uncertainty visible.** Treat claims about motives, beliefs, power, and relationships as evidence claims rather than facts by default.
10. **Use the self-check rubric** before treating the analysis as decision-ready.

The goal is not to classify everyone. The goal is to understand the **system of actors around the work well enough to make better analytical, engagement, and political judgments**.

## The overall model

Use the sources for different jobs rather than blending them into one undifferentiated framework:

- **Bardach [B]** provides the policy-analysis spine: attitude and influence, supporters and allies, opposition, uncommitted interests, implementation actors, timing, sequencing, political resources, concessions, and the dynamics of building support.
- **Weimer & Vining [W]** tell us what to know about each actor: **motivations, beliefs, resources**, and the **arena** in which those resources can be exercised.
- **Getting to Yes [F]** tells us how to get underneath the actor’s stated position: distinguish **positions from interests**, recognise perceptions and multiple constituencies, and avoid mistaking disagreement with the person for disagreement about the problem.
- **Eight Types of Power [P]** tells us what kind of power sits inside the broad “resources” category rather than reducing influence to a high/medium/low score.
- **Reinikainen [R]** tells us how those actors relate to one another as a **network**, including informal influence, boundary spanners, clusters, bottlenecks, and relationships that do not appear in formal structures.

Do not force every source into every case. Use the lightest combination needed to understand the stakeholder system.

## Core rules

### 1. Start with the thing stakeholders are reacting to [B]

A stakeholder is not simply “important to the topic.”

Anchor the map to something specific:

- a problem definition
- a proposed course of action
- a decision
- a programme or organisational change
- an implementation challenge
- an emerging recommendation

The same actor may support the goal but oppose the means, support one framing but resist another, or change position as the proposal changes.

If the object of the analysis is vague, stakeholder positions will be vague too.

### 2. Identify actors broadly — but for a reason [B][W]

Weimer & Vining distinguish 2 overlapping routes into the map:

- actors with a **substantive interest** in the issue
- actors with **official standing** in the decision arena

Bardach adds actors whose behaviour shapes implementation and political support.

Relevant actors can therefore include:

- formal decision makers
- elected or appointed officials
- agencies and organisational units
- internal professional, ideological, or bureaucratic factions
- organised interest groups
- affected communities
- front-line workers
- co-producers
- public, nonprofit, or commercial partners
- finance, procurement, audit, legal, personnel, or other control bodies
- experts whose judgment carries weight
- media or public figures who can influence how the issue is framed
- groups likely to become active as the issue develops

Do not add names simply to make the map look comprehensive. For each actor, be able to say **why they matter to this problem, decision, or delivery system**.

### 3. Use the Weimer & Vining worksheet as the core actor analysis [W]

For each serious actor, build this table:

| Actor | Motivations | Beliefs | Resources |
|---|---|---|---|
| `[individual / group / organisation / faction]` | `[what they are trying to protect, achieve, avoid, or preserve]` | `[what they appear to believe about the problem, proposal, consequences, or other actors]` | `[what they can actually bring to bear]` |

Keep the columns distinct.

- **Motivations** are what the actor wants, values, fears, protects, or is rewarded for.
- **Beliefs** are what the actor thinks is true about the problem, proposal, likely consequences, feasibility, legality, fairness, or behaviour of others.
- **Resources** are the means through which the actor could affect the outcome.

A stakeholder’s position may be partly explained by all three, but it is not a substitute for any of them.

### 4. Do not assume actors have one motivation [W][F]

Actors often hold several motives at once.

An elected official may care about:

- substantive policy outcomes
- constituents
- party
- re-election
- reputation
- future career

A civil servant may care about:

- substantive values
- professional standards
- organisational resources
- workload
- implementation risk
- maintaining effectiveness or legitimacy

An organisation may contain factions whose motivations differ.

Do not write:

> Motivation: protect budget.

when the evidence suggests several competing considerations.

Where it matters, ask which motivation is likely to dominate **in this decision and under these conditions**.

### 5. Separate positions from interests [F]

A **position** is what an actor currently says should happen.

An **interest** is a need, desire, concern, fear, value, constraint, or objective that helps explain why that position makes sense to them.

Example:

- **Position:** “We oppose mandatory reporting.”
- **Possible interests:** protect professional discretion; avoid additional workload; reduce legal exposure; maintain trust with service users; avoid a precedent that shifts responsibility.

Do not infer an interest merely because it sounds plausible.

Use questions such as:

- **Why does this position make sense from their point of view?**
- **What are they trying to protect, gain, avoid, or preserve?**
- **Why have they not already made the decision we want them to make?**
- **What do they think would happen if they said yes?**
- **What do they think would happen if they said no?**

Treat the answers as hypotheses until evidenced.

### 6. Understand perception without confusing it with fact [F][W]

Political behaviour is shaped by what actors **believe**, not only by what is objectively true.

An actor may resist because they believe:

- the policy will cost jobs
- another organisation cannot deliver
- the proposal is legally risky
- accepting it will look like weakness
- their constituency will punish them
- another actor cannot be trusted

The analyst may think the belief is wrong. It still belongs in the stakeholder analysis if it is shaping behaviour.

Record separately:

- **what the actor appears to believe**
- **what evidence supports that assessment**
- **whether the belief itself is well supported**

Understanding their point of view is not the same as endorsing it.

### 7. Treat organisations as collections of actors when necessary [B][F][W]

“The Treasury,” “the union,” “the department,” or “the public” may conceal meaningful differences.

Look for:

- professional factions
- ideological divisions
- different operational roles
- leadership versus front line
- centre versus local units
- different constituencies
- people with formal authority versus people with informal influence

Fisher and Ury warn against assuming everyone on “the other side” has the same interests. Bardach similarly directs attention to internal factions, leadership, culture, and communications.

Split an organisation into separate actors when those differences could change the analysis.

### 8. Treat resources as actual means of influence, not a vague power score [W][P]

Weimer & Vining treat political resources broadly: votes, vetoes, agenda control, money, membership, expertise, reputation, relationships, professional authority, information, and access can all matter.

Use the Eight Types of Power as a diagnostic when “power” is otherwise too vague:

| Category | Type | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Formal | **Institutional** | Authority that comes from a formal role or position |
| Formal | **Convening** | Ability to bring actors together and shape the agenda |
| Formal | **Veto** | Ability to stop or block a decision or agreement |
| Formal | **Shift** | Ability to move between acceptable deals or positions without prohibitive political or personal cost |
| Informal | **Moral suasion** | Ability to frame an issue in moral or ethical terms that changes how alternatives are perceived |
| Informal | **Nuisance** | Ability to create attention, disruption, publicity, or pressure despite limited formal authority |
| Mixed | **Momentum** | Power created by the perception that a course of action is advancing and others are attaching themselves to it |
| Mixed | **Coalitional** | Ability to mobilise a network of allies and direct their resources toward a cause |

Do **not** force all 8 categories onto every actor.

Instead ask:

> “You have described this actor as powerful. What can they actually do here?”

Record the relevant resource or power type and the arena in which it can be exercised.

### 9. Distinguish position, intensity, and influence [B]

For each important actor, these are separate questions:

- What is their current position?
- How strongly do they hold it?
- How much influence do they have over the relevant outcome?
- What gives them that influence?

A strongly opposed actor with little ability to affect the decision is different from a mildly sceptical actor with a veto.

Do not collapse these into one “power-interest” score.

### 10. Identify the political arena [W][B]

Stakeholder influence is exercised somewhere.

Ask:

- Where will the relevant decision actually be made?
- Who has formal standing there?
- What are the written rules?
- What unwritten practices or traditions matter?
- Who controls the agenda or timing?
- What counts as a decision in this arena: vote, ministerial approval, budget settlement, judicial decision, procurement award, executive action, operational consent?
- Can the issue be shifted into another arena?

Weimer & Vining emphasise that actors who expect to lose in one arena may try to move the issue elsewhere.

Bardach similarly treats calendars, institutional procedures, and arenas as part of political strategy.

A resource only matters if it can be exercised in a relevant arena.

### 11. Look beyond formal structure to the actual network [R]

Organisation charts describe formal structure.

Network analysis asks what is **actually happening**.

Where relevant, investigate:

- who communicates with whom
- who is trusted
- who people go to for expertise or technical support
- who influences decisions despite lacking formal authority
- who connects groups that are structurally separated
- where information or resources flow
- who needs to say something for others to listen
- where there are missing or weak relationships

Do not assume hierarchy describes influence accurately.

### 12. Ask what kind of network matters [R]

There is no single definitive stakeholder network.

Different questions may require different relationship maps, for example:

- **communication** — who talks to whom?
- **influence** — whose views shape others?
- **decision** — where do decisions really pass?
- **expertise/support** — who relies on whom to get work done?
- **coalition** — who mobilises with whom?
- **cross-boundary** — who connects otherwise separate organisations, professions, or communities?

State which relationship is being mapped.

Do not combine every relationship into one incomprehensible “hairball.”

### 13. Look for boundary spanners, brokers, clusters, bottlenecks, and gaps [R][P]

A relationship map can reveal features an actor table cannot.

Look for:

- **boundary spanners** — people already connecting silos or communities
- **brokers** — actors through whom access, information, or influence passes
- **clusters** — groups that are more connected internally than with the rest of the system
- **bottlenecks** — actors overloaded because decisions, expertise, or communication route through them
- **single points of failure** — relationships or people whose absence would fragment the system
- **missing links** — important groups that should interact but do not
- **latent structures** — existing relationships that could support collaboration without creating a new formal structure

This can change the political analysis. An actor may matter because of **where they sit in the network**, not because of formal status.

### 14. Include implementation actors, not only approval actors [B][R]

A policy can be formally adopted and still fail because the people who must enact it, cooperate with it, or adapt around it do not behave as assumed.

Consider:

- front-line workers
- managers and supervisors
- co-producers
- vendors and partners
- local delivery organisations
- people who hold tacit knowledge
- control or assurance bodies
- groups whose cooperation is operationally necessary

Ask who can quietly slow, reshape, reinterpret, or enable implementation.

### 15. Distinguish supporters from allies [B]

A **supporter** favours the proposal.

An **ally** is a supporter who is also prepared to do something useful, such as:

- recruit other supporters
- open access
- supply expertise or legitimacy
- provide resources
- mobilise a constituency
- neutralise an opponent

Passive approval is not the same as coalition capacity.

### 16. Keep uncommitted actors visible [B]

Not every actor is “for” or “against.”

Some may be:

- undecided
- conditionally supportive
- weakly attached
- waiting for more evidence
- waiting to see whether the proposal is viable
- potentially hostile but not mobilised
- capable of being neutralised without becoming a supporter

These actors can be politically important because they may determine whether a coalition reaches enough support.

### 17. Treat support and opposition as dynamic [B][P][R]

A stakeholder map is a snapshot.

Political support develops over time.

Ask how the picture could change through:

- new evidence
- reframing
- participation in design
- changing political conditions
- visible momentum
- coalition growth
- a new relationship
- a different arena
- opponent mobilisation
- implementation experience
- concessions
- shifts in public attention

Do not report current positions as permanent properties of actors.

### 18. Sequence engagement where relationships compound [B][P][R]

The most powerful actor is not always the best first actor.

An early conversation may be valuable because that person can:

- validate feasibility
- lend legitimacy
- create access to someone else
- connect otherwise separate groups
- recruit allies
- make the proposal look viable
- surface hidden opposition
- reveal how the system actually works

Ask:

> **Who changes the odds of reaching the next important actor?**

This is a network and coalition question, not simply a ranking exercise.

### 19. Treat political resources as usable and sometimes depletable [B]

Bardach highlights resources that may be spent over time, including:

- money
- staff capacity
- access
- expertise
- legitimacy
- goodwill
- favours
- debts owed
- bargaining chips
- policy concessions

Some resources become less available once used.

Do not recommend using every political resource immediately. Ask when it has the greatest value.

### 20. Treat concessions as substantive and coalition choices [B][F]

A concession can win or neutralise one actor while damaging something else.

Ask:

- What substantive objective is being given up?
- Who benefits?
- Which existing allies may feel betrayed or weakened?
- Does the concession create a precedent?
- Does it solve the underlying interest or merely split positions?
- Is there another way to meet the interest at lower policy cost?

Do not call movement toward agreement automatically good.

### 21. Use engagement to test the map [B][F][W]

Stakeholder analysis is full of claims that can be wrong.

Engagement can test:

- motivations
- beliefs
- interests
- likely behaviour
- internal factions
- perceived risks
- hidden constraints
- relationships
- willingness to deploy resources

Use direct contact, observed behaviour, documents, previous decisions, informed intermediaries, and other evidence where available.

Do not contact people merely to “validate” a conclusion already fixed.

### 22. Mark evidence, inference, and unknowns [B][W]

Statements such as these are analytical claims:

- “The minister will oppose this.”
- “Staff mainly care about workload.”
- “The regulator has enough influence to block it.”
- “Organisation A trusts Organisation B.”
- “This person is the key broker.”

For important claims, mark whether the basis is:

- **observed / evidenced**
- **reported by a source**
- **inferred**
- **hypothesis**
- **unknown**

Do not invent motives, relationships, or political positions.

## Primary artefact: the Weimer & Vining political analysis worksheet [W]

Use this as the default actor-level artefact:

| Actor | Motivations | Beliefs | Resources |
|---|---|---|---|
|  |  |  |  |

### Populate it carefully

**Actor**
- Name the relevant individual, group, organisation, or faction.
- Split organisations where internal differences matter.

**Motivations**
- What are they trying to achieve, protect, avoid, preserve, or be seen to do?
- What constituencies, professional values, incentives, risks, or relationships matter?
- Which motives may conflict?

**Beliefs**
- What do they believe about the problem?
- What do they believe about the proposed action?
- What do they expect will happen?
- What do they believe about other actors, feasibility, legitimacy, risk, or fairness?
- How confident are we that this really is their belief?

**Resources**
- What can they actually use to influence approval, implementation, public perception, or other actors?
- Which of the 8 power types helps specify that resource?
- Where can the resource be exercised?

### Keep these as annotations rather than bloating the core table

For important actors, add concise notes on:

- **role / basis for inclusion**
- **current position and intensity**
- **arena**
- **evidence / confidence**

The 4-column table remains the analytical core.

## Interests diagnostic: looking beneath the position [F]

Use this only where the actor’s stated position does not adequately explain their behaviour.

For the actor in question:

1. **State the position.**
2. Ask **why** that position might serve their interests.
3. Ask **why not** — what makes the decision you want them to make unattractive from their point of view?
4. List the likely interests, needs, fears, constraints, or constituencies involved.
5. Identify which are:
   - shared with others
   - compatible
   - different but potentially complementary
   - genuinely conflicting
6. Mark which interests are evidenced and which remain hypotheses.

A useful record is:

> **Position:** `[what they currently say]`  
> **Underlying interests:** `[needs / concerns / fears / objectives]`  
> **Perceived choice:** `[what they think saying yes/no would mean]`  
> **Evidence/confidence:** `[basis]`

Do not use this exercise to psychoanalyse the actor. Its purpose is to understand the decision as they may currently see it.

## Power diagnostic: what can this actor actually do? [P][W]

Do not ask simply “How powerful are they?”

Ask:

- What **formal authority** do they hold?
- Can they **convene** relevant actors or control the agenda?
- Can they **veto**, delay, or block?
- How much **room to shift** do they have between acceptable positions?
- Can they exercise **moral suasion**?
- Can they create **nuisance, attention, or reputational pressure**?
- Is **momentum** increasing their influence?
- Can they mobilise **coalitional power** through relationships and allies?
- What other resources identified by Weimer & Vining matter: votes, money, expertise, constituency, information, professional status, relationships, or procedural authority?

Then ask:

> **Does the actor have this power in theory, or are they actually willing and able to use it here?**

Potential power is not the same as deployed power.

## Required-support check: the authorising environment [B][W][T]

The full stakeholder map answers "who matters and why". This check answers a narrower question:

> **Whose agreement is the proposition actually dependent on?**

Run it whenever the work has moved from understanding a problem to proposing, funding or delivering something.

From the wider ecosystem, pull out the subset whose **authorisation, funding, legal permission, operational cooperation, legitimacy or non-opposition** is required for the proposition to proceed. For each, record:

> `[actor]` supplies `[authorisation / funding / legal permission / operational cooperation / legitimacy / non-opposition]`. Without it, `[what fails]`. Current position: `[supportive / conditional / uncommitted / opposed / unknown]`. Basis: `[evidence / inference / unknown]`.

3 rules keep this honest.

**Required is not the same as interested.** A group can be deeply affected and have no ability to stop the proposition, and an obscure control function can hold a decisive veto. Both belong in the analysis, for different reasons. Do not let this check quietly become the whole stakeholder map, and do not let a small required-support list justify dropping affected groups who lack power.

**Non-opposition counts.** Some actors never have to say yes. They only have to not say no, not brief against it, or not withdraw a service the proposition depends on. Record what they would have to refrain from doing.

**Support for the purpose is not support for the delivery model.** An actor may endorse the aim and still refuse the cost, the timetable, the reporting burden or the risk once it becomes concrete. Where current support rests on a cheaper, faster or easier version of the proposition than the one being proposed, mark the support as fragile and say what would expose it.

Keep this to the consequential few. If the list runs past about 6 actors, either the proposition is genuinely dependent on an unusual number of consents, which is itself a finding worth stating, or the list has drifted back into general stakeholder mapping.

Use `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` for the narrower political-feasibility judgment: whether the required support is sufficient, why it is absent where it is absent, and what would have to change. This method identifies who is required and what they hold. The shared method assesses whether the proposition is supported enough to proceed.

Do not add these columns to the 4-column actor table. Keep the required-support list as a separate short view.

## Network layer: how do the actors relate? [R]

Do not automatically create a formal network diagram. A lightweight relationship map is usually enough.

For important relationships, record:

> `[Actor A] → [Actor B]: [relationship type] — [evidence / significance]`

Useful relationship types include:

- communicates with
- trusts
- seeks advice from
- provides expertise to
- formally authorises
- informally influences
- funds
- depends on
- introduces / brokers access to
- collaborates with
- conflicts with
- mobilises with
- supplies information to
- supervises
- blocks
- spans a boundary between

Then ask:

- Where are the **boundary spanners**?
- Who are the **brokers**?
- Which **clusters** appear?
- Where are the **bottlenecks**?
- Which relationships are missing?
- Which actors appear influential because of their network position rather than title?
- Does the formal structure differ from the actual pattern of work or influence?

### If an intervention in the network is relevant

Reinikainen describes 3 broad relationship-level moves:

- **create links** where useful connections are missing
- **remove or reduce links** that are harmful or distort the system
- **change the role of a node** where influence, expertise, decisions, or workload are concentrated in the wrong place

Do not recommend a network intervention simply because the map makes one visible. Tie it to the policy or implementation objective.

## Dynamic political readout [B]

After the actor and relationship analysis, summarise the political system.

### Required support
- Whose authorisation, funding, legal permission, operational cooperation, legitimacy or non-opposition is the proposition dependent on?
- What does each of them hold, and what fails without it?
- Is any of that support conditional on a version of the proposition that is no longer being proposed?

### Support
- Who currently supports the proposition?
- Which supporters are **active allies**?
- What can those allies actually contribute?

### Opposition
- Who opposes?
- How intense is the opposition?
- Is it organised, latent, or conditional?
- What resources could opponents deploy?

### Uncommitted
- Who is undecided or weakly attached?
- What appears to matter to them?
- What might move or neutralise them?

### Decision and implementation
- Who can formally approve, reject, delay, amend, fund, or redirect?
- Who can make implementation succeed or fail even without formal decision rights?

### Sequence
- Which actor should be understood or engaged first?
- Who unlocks access, legitimacy, information, coalition support, or implementation capability?
- What should be conserved for later?

### Change conditions
- What new evidence, event, relationship, arena, concession, or shift in momentum could materially change the map?

## Stakeholder-analysis scaffold

Work through these in order where useful; skip steps the user has already answered.

1. **Name the focal problem, proposition, or decision.**
2. **Identify actors with substantive interests.**
3. **Identify actors with official standing.**
4. **Add affected groups and implementation actors.**
5. **Split organisations into factions where necessary.**
6. **Build the Weimer & Vining table: Actor → Motivations → Beliefs → Resources.**
7. **Record current position and intensity separately.**
8. **For important or puzzling actors, distinguish position from underlying interests.**
9. **Interrogate vague “power” claims using the Eight Types of Power and other concrete resources.**
10. **Identify the arena and its formal and informal rules.**
11. **Map the relationships that actually matter: communication, trust, influence, dependency, brokerage, coalition, or support.**
12. **Identify boundary spanners, brokers, clusters, bottlenecks, missing links, and informal influencers where relevant.**
13. **Classify the dynamic political position: supporter, ally, opponent, uncommitted, conditional, or unclear.**
14. **Run the required-support check where something is being proposed: whose authorisation, funding, permission, cooperation, legitimacy or non-opposition is the proposition dependent on?**
15. **Identify what is evidence, inference, hypothesis, or unknown.**
16. **Ask what could change the map and in what sequence.**
17. **Identify the most valuable next learning or engagement move.**

→ **Stakeholder position:** `[actor] + [motivation] + [belief] + [resources/power] + [current position] + [arena] + [key relationships] + [evidence/confidence] + [change condition]`.

## Anti-patterns

### Pitfall A — A list of names masquerading as analysis

**Failure:** identifying stakeholders without explaining why they matter.

**Challenge:** put each serious actor through the `Motivations → Beliefs → Resources` test.

### Pitfall B — A 2×2 power/interest grid treated as the whole method

**Failure:** collapsing motivations, beliefs, formal authority, informal influence, relationships, and political dynamics into 2 scores.

**Challenge:** use a grid only as a rough visual aid, never as the analysis itself.

### Pitfall C — Treating stated position as motivation

**Failure:** “They oppose the proposal, therefore their motivation is opposition.”

**Challenge:** ask what interest, belief, fear, constraint, or constituency sits underneath the position.

### Pitfall D — Treating an organisation as one mind

**Failure:** “The department supports it.”

**Challenge:** ask whether leadership, professions, delivery teams, control functions, or local units hold materially different motivations or beliefs.

### Pitfall E — Equating formal hierarchy with real influence

**Failure:** assuming the most senior person is necessarily the person others listen to.

**Challenge:** map informal advice, trust, expertise, brokerage, and communication.

### Pitfall F — Writing “high power” without saying what the actor can do

**Failure:** power becomes an unexplained score.

**Challenge:** identify the concrete resource or power type and the arena in which it can be exercised.

### Pitfall G — Ignoring affected groups because they lack formal power

**Failure:** treating political influence as the criterion for whether someone belongs in the map.

**Challenge:** distinguish **being affected** from **being influential**. Both may matter, for different reasons.

### Pitfall H — Mapping approval but not implementation

**Failure:** focusing only on people who can say yes to a policy.

**Challenge:** identify who must interpret, operate, cooperate, supply, comply, or adapt for it to work.

### Pitfall I — Inventing motives and relationships

**Failure:** plausible-sounding psychology appears as fact.

**Challenge:** mark inference and uncertainty explicitly and identify what evidence would resolve it.

### Pitfall J — Treating the map as static

**Failure:** current support and opposition are presented as permanent.

**Challenge:** identify change conditions, sequencing, momentum, arena shifts, and likely countermobilisation.

### Pitfall K — Assuming every supporter is an ally

**Failure:** passive approval is counted as usable coalition power.

**Challenge:** ask what the supporter will actually do.

### Pitfall L — Over-engineering the network map

**Failure:** producing a dense diagram that is difficult to interpret and does not change any decision.

**Challenge:** map only the relationship type needed for the current question and extract the few structural features that matter.

- **Biggest stakeholder uncertainty:** …
- **Most useful next move:** …

Only produce all 4 when the case warrants it. For a small problem, the Weimer & Vining table plus a short relationship and dynamics note may be enough.

## Self-check rubric

Before returning a stakeholder analysis, confirm:

### Must pass — block on any failure

- The analysis is anchored to a **specific problem, proposition, decision, or implementation challenge**.
- Important actors have been analysed in terms of **motivations, beliefs, and resources**, not merely named.
- **Positions have not been mistaken for interests**.
- Claims about motives, beliefs, power, and relationships are **evidenced, labelled as inference, or marked uncertain**.
- Vague “power” judgments have been translated into **actual resources or forms of power**.
- The relevant **decision arena** has been identified where political feasibility matters.
- The analysis includes **implementation actors** where their behaviour affects success.
- It distinguishes **supporters from active allies**.
- Important **relationships or routes of influence** have been considered rather than relying only on formal structure.
- Where something is being proposed, the actors whose **authorisation, funding, permission, cooperation, legitimacy or non-opposition is required** have been identified separately from the wider ecosystem.

### Should pass — note the gap if missing

- Actors with both **substantive interests** and **official standing** have been considered.
- Internal factions have been separated where treating an organisation as one actor would mislead.
- Multiple motivations and constituencies have been considered for important actors.
- Perceptions and beliefs are separated from the analyst’s view of the facts.
- Informal power, coalitional power, and network position have been considered where formal authority gives an incomplete picture.
- Boundary spanners, brokers, clusters, bottlenecks, or missing links have been considered where relationships materially affect the outcome.
- The map includes **uncommitted or conditional actors**, not only supporters and opponents.
- Sequencing, timing, and changing political conditions have been considered.
- Concessions are assessed for both their policy cost and their effect on existing allies.
- Required support has been tested for whether actors endorse the delivery model as well as the purpose.
- Affected groups without power have not disappeared because they are not required for approval.
- A clear evidence gap or next learning move has been identified where uncertainty matters.

### Process reminder

- Stakeholder analysis is **iterative**. The map should change when the problem framing, proposal, evidence, political arena, coalition, or implementation context changes.
- The purpose is not to categorise people permanently. It is to understand **how an actor system is currently working, why, and what that means for the policy analysis**.
- Keep the method proportional. A simple case may need 5 actors and 3 relationships; a complex case may need a much richer map.

## Shared method calls

This file owns the **actor system**: who is involved, what they want and believe, what they can bring to bear, how they relate, and how that picture could change.

It should call other methods rather than absorb them.

### Call `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- the question has narrowed to whether the required support is sufficient for the proposition to proceed
- support is absent and the reason needs diagnosing rather than labelling as a lack of political will
- required supporters may endorse the purpose while rejecting the delivery model
- improving political support would cost public value or operational feasibility

### Call `evidence-guidance.md` when:

- an important claim about a motivation, belief, relationship or power resource is inference presented as fact
- the analysis needs to know how strong the basis for a stakeholder judgment actually is

### Call `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- a decision-relevant stakeholder claim could be tested through direct contact, observed behaviour, documents or previous decisions
- engagement is being proposed and needs a stated learning question rather than a general round of consultation

### Call `decision-guidance.md` when:

- the question is who has the incentive and ability to carry a proposal, or who resists because they lose authority, resources or discretion

## Scope boundary

This method stops at **understanding the stakeholder system and its political dynamics**.

It can identify:

- underlying interests
- power and resources
- relationships
- possible coalition dynamics
- likely engagement needs
- political constraints and opportunities
- which actors the proposition is dependent on

It does **not** by itself decide:

- which policy option is best
- what decision criteria should dominate
- what constitutes public value
- whether the required support is sufficient to proceed
- whether a negotiation tactic is ethically or strategically appropriate
- how to design a full negotiation strategy

Those belong to the methods that own them, named above. This is not a stage in a sequence: stakeholder analysis can be the first thing a user needs or the last, and the map should be revisited whenever the problem, proposal, evidence or arena changes.

## One-line reference

> Good stakeholder analysis starts with Bardach’s political reality, uses Weimer & Vining to understand each actor’s motivations, beliefs and resources, looks beneath stated positions to underlying interests, specifies the real forms of power actors can exercise, and maps the relationships through which influence, information, support and implementation actually move.
