# Criteria - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main policy-analysis spine and adds only the shared material that changes how criteria should be chosen. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Four - used for evaluative versus practical criteria, primary objectives, efficiency, equity, rights, process values, legality, political acceptability, robustness, improvability, weighting and operationalisation. **[A]** `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called to decide whether conventional appraisal is a good fit or whether non-marginal change, heterogeneous effects or fundamental uncertainty require a wider set of criteria and multiple dimensions. **[T]** `strategic-triangle-guidance.md` - called lightly where public purpose, operational capacity or required support materially shapes what should count in the appraisal. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user decide *what outcomes matter, what counts as better, which requirements are constraints rather than tradable values, and how to make the evaluative choices visible before comparing policy options*.

## How to use this document

When asked to define, improve or challenge policy criteria:

1. **Return to the problem and decision.**
2. **Decide what kind of appraisal problem this is.**
3. **State the primary public objective.**
4. **Add the other outcome dimensions that materially matter.**
5. **Separate evaluative criteria from practical constraints.**
6. **Make distribution, rights and affected groups explicit where relevant.**
7. **State the direction of each criterion.**
8. **Operationalise criteria without confusing the proxy with the value.**
9. **Make weighting or priority judgments visible.**
10. **Stop before projecting or scoring options.**

The criteria stage defines **what a good outcome would mean**. It should not predict which option produces it.

# Part 1 - Decide what kind of appraisal problem this is

## 1. Start with the decision

Criteria are selected for a real choice.

Ask:

- What decision is being made?
- What problem is it meant to improve?
- Which alternatives are likely to be compared?
- What kinds of consequence could plausibly change the choice?

Do not begin with a generic checklist.

## 2. Use the appraisal gate where it matters [A]

Before deciding how narrow or broad the criteria set needs to be, ask whether the decision is:

### Relatively marginal

The policy changes something within a broadly stable system.

### Materially non-marginal

The policy may alter:

- institutions
- market structure
- technologies
- incentives
- relationships
- future pathways
- the shape of the service or system itself

Also ask whether:

### Effects are relatively homogeneous

A common measure captures most of what matters.

### Effects are heterogeneous

Different groups, interests or outcome dimensions experience materially different consequences.

And whether:

### Important uncertainty is quantifiable

Plausible ranges or probabilities are meaningful.

### Important uncertainty is fundamental

The system, model, future pathway or relevant probabilities are not well enough known for one expected-value calculation to carry the decision.

If marginality, homogeneity and quantifiable uncertainty are broadly reasonable, conventional cost-effectiveness or cost-benefit criteria may be useful.

If they are not, preserve more dimensions rather than forcing the problem into one metric.

Do not reject conventional appraisal merely because the policy is ambitious.

## 3. Make the desired direction explicit [A]

For structural or long-running change, ask:

> **What direction do we want the system to move in?**

Examples:

- fewer people entering crisis
- greater access
- lower carbon intensity
- less reliance on manual processing
- greater resilience
- more prevention

Direction is an evaluative choice.

Do not present it as though the evidence selected it.

# Part 2 - Define what matters

## 4. Derive the primary criterion from the problem [B]

The primary criterion usually expresses the improvement sought in the problem definition.

Problem:

> Too many eligible households lose support because applications fail before completion.

Primary criterion:

> **Reduce the proportion of eligible households that fail to complete the process.**

If the primary criterion does not correspond to the problem, revisit one of them.

## 5. Keep the primary objective clear without pretending it is the only value [B]

One primary objective helps keep the analysis intelligible.

Other consequences may still matter enough to alter the decision.

Add criteria for material:

- benefits
- harms
- costs
- distributional effects
- rights
- safety
- process effects
- implementation effects
- long-run system effects

Do not create a criterion for every conceivable consequence.

## 6. Distinguish public purpose from political support [T]

Where useful, use the Strategic Triangle to ask:

- What public result is being claimed?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs or harms?
- Are affected interests missing from the political picture?

Political support may be a practical criterion.

It is not proof that the policy creates public value.

## 7. Treat efficiency as one value, not the whole decision [B]

Efficiency may ask whether aggregate benefits exceed aggregate costs or whether a fixed objective can be achieved at lower cost.

It is useful.

It can also hide:

- distribution
- rights
- unpriced values
- who can express willingness to pay
- long-run system change

Use it where it answers a real part of the decision.

Do not use it as a complete definition of public value.

## 8. Distinguish cost-effectiveness from benefit-cost analysis [B]

**Cost-effectiveness** compares the resources needed to achieve a defined outcome.

**Benefit-cost analysis** attempts to value both costs and benefits in a common monetary unit.

Use the lighter form where it answers the question.

Do not monetise an outcome merely because a decision template expects a ratio.

## 9. Make equity and distribution concrete [B][A]

Do not write:

> Equity.

Ask:

- Equity between whom?
- Of what?
- Over what period?
- Who gains?
- Who loses?
- Who bears transition cost?
- Who bears risk if the policy fails?
- Is the concern equality of access, opportunity, outcome, treatment, burden or something else?

A distributional criterion should tell the analyst what pattern counts as better.

## 10. Include rights or non-tradable values where relevant [B]

Some decisions include:

- legal rights
- privacy
- liberty
- equality before the law
- minimum safety standards
- procedural protections

Do not assume every such claim is absolute.

Do not assume it can be compensated away by enough benefit elsewhere.

Clarify whether it operates as:

- a binding constraint
- a strong presumption
- a value to be balanced

## 11. Include process values where the process itself matters [B]

Possible process criteria include:

- participation
- openness
- accessibility
- fairness
- non-arbitrariness
- transparency

Do not assume more participation is always better.

Ask who can participate, whose voice dominates and what the process is meant to achieve.

# Part 3 - Separate values from practical constraints

## 12. Distinguish evaluative criteria from practical criteria [B]

### Evaluative criteria

Judge whether projected outcomes are good or bad.

Examples:

- effectiveness
- distribution
- safety
- rights
- cost
- public experience

### Practical criteria

Test whether the option can survive adoption and implementation.

Bardach highlights:

- legality
- political acceptability
- robustness
- improvability

Keep these distinct.

A highly valuable outcome may still depend on an infeasible option.

## 13. Treat legality as a real constraint where it is one [B]

Ask:

- Is there authority to act?
- Is the legal position clear?
- Is a new power required?
- Is legal uncertainty material?

Do not invent a legal conclusion.

Where necessary, flag the need for legal advice.

## 14. Treat political acceptability dynamically [B][T]

Ask:

- Who can authorise, fund, block or withdraw cooperation?
- How broad or intense is support or opposition?
- Is current support tied to an unrealistic delivery assumption?
- What would have to change for the option to become acceptable?

Use the stakeholder method for deeper actor analysis.

Do not write "politically infeasible" as though current conditions are permanent.

## 15. Test robustness [B]

Ask whether satisfactory outcomes remain plausible if implementation includes:

- delay
- administrative distortion
- lower uptake
- higher cost
- confusion
- gaming
- weaker compliance
- political pressure

Robustness is a criterion where the policy has to work under realistic rather than perfect conditions.

## 16. Test improvability [B]

Some designs can adapt as experience accumulates.

Ask:

- Can the policy be changed without losing its core purpose?
- Can implementers correct weak details?
- Can weak components be stopped?
- Does flexibility create capture or drift risk?

Do not treat flexibility as automatically beneficial.

# Part 4 - Classify criteria by role

## 17. Separate objectives, constraints and secondary values [B]

A useful structure is:

### Primary objective

The main improvement sought.

### Constraints

Conditions that must be satisfied.

### Secondary values

Outcomes where more or less is preferable but which do not define the whole choice.

Example:

> **Maximise** successful completion  
> **Subject to** legal compliance and the budget ceiling  
> **While reducing** avoidable burden and protecting equitable access

This is often clearer than a flat list of 12 equally weighted criteria.

## 18. Give every criterion a direction [B]

Prefer:

- maximise completion
- minimise annual operating cost
- reduce disparity between groups
- satisfy statutory requirements
- increase resilience

Avoid:

- cost
- access
- fairness
- governance
- customer experience

A criterion should make clear what better means.

## 19. Keep conceptual value and metric separate [B]

Example:

**Conceptual criterion**

> Reduce burden on applicants.

**Possible proxy**

> Average additional minutes required per application.

The proxy is evidence about the criterion.

It is not the criterion itself.

State important limitations.

## 20. Quantify where meaningful, not for appearance [B][A]

Use:

- counts
- rates
- costs
- time
- thresholds
- ranges

where they clarify comparison.

Use explicit qualitative judgment where the value cannot be measured defensibly.

Do not turn every criterion into a 1-5 score.

# Part 5 - Keep aggregation and weighting honest

## 21. Ask whether a common metric helps or hides [A]

A common measure is useful where outcomes are genuinely commensurable.

It becomes misleading where conversion hides a contested judgment.

Ask:

> **Would putting these outcomes into one metric make the choice clearer, or quietly decide how much each value counts?**

If the latter, keep the dimensions separate.

## 22. Make weights visible [B][A]

Where criteria receive different priority, state:

- who set the priority
- why
- whether it is political, legal, ethical or analytical
- whether modest changes in weight could reverse the eventual decision

Do not let a weighted table appear neutral.

## 23. Look for double counting

Criteria can overlap.

Examples:

- waiting time
- burden
- satisfaction

may partly represent the same underlying experience.

Ask whether the criteria capture distinct consequences.

Do not reward one benefit 3 times because it has 3 labels.

## 24. Name underrepresented interests [B][T]

The formal political process may underweight:

- future generations
- people with little organised influence
- diffuse taxpayers
- minorities
- people outside the deciding jurisdiction

The analyst may surface these interests.

Do not silently substitute the analyst's own values for accountable judgment.

# Criteria scaffold

1. **Decision:** What choice is being made?
2. **Problem:** What condition should improve?
3. **Appraisal gate:** Marginal or non-marginal? Homogeneous or heterogeneous? Quantifiable or fundamental uncertainty?
4. **Primary objective:** What is the main public result?
5. **Direction:** What counts as better?
6. **Additional outcomes:** What important benefits, harms or distributions could change the choice?
7. **Rights / process:** Are there values that need explicit protection?
8. **Practical criteria:** What legal, political, robustness or improvability tests matter?
9. **Role:** Objective / constraint / secondary value.
10. **Operational measure:** What indicator could represent the criterion?
11. **Proxy limitation:** What does the measure miss?
12. **Weight / status:** Primary / binding / high importance / secondary / contested.
13. **Aggregation check:** Which dimensions can be combined? Which should remain separate?
14. **Double-count check:** Are any criteria overlapping?
15. **Stop:** Do not score options yet.

→ **Criteria set:** `[primary objective] + [material outcome dimensions] + [binding practical constraints]`, with each criterion showing `[direction] + [role] + [measure/proxy] + [status or weighting]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Generic checklist

**Failure:** every policy gets effectiveness, efficiency, equity, feasibility and acceptability.

**Challenge:** derive criteria from the actual decision.

## Pitfall B - Criteria judge the option directly

**Failure:** "Option A is effective."

**Challenge:** criteria judge projected outcomes. Outcomes come next.

## Pitfall C - Equity without a distribution

**Failure:** the criterion is just "equity".

**Challenge:** specify who, what and which pattern counts as better.

## Pitfall D - Efficiency becomes public value

**Failure:** the highest aggregate net benefit is treated as the whole answer.

**Challenge:** preserve distribution, rights and other relevant values.

## Pitfall E - One metric everywhere

**Failure:** heterogeneous values are forced into one total.

**Challenge:** keep dimensions separate where conversion hides the judgment.

## Pitfall F - Hidden weighting

**Failure:** scoring rules quietly determine the winner.

**Challenge:** state who set the priorities and why.

## Pitfall G - Metric substitution

**Failure:** the easiest available measure becomes the objective.

**Challenge:** keep conceptual criterion and proxy separate.

## Pitfall H - Flat list

**Failure:** every criterion appears equally important.

**Challenge:** identify primary objective, constraints and secondary values.

## Pitfall I - Political support treated as value

**Failure:** a popular option is assumed to serve the public interest.

**Challenge:** keep public value and support separate.

## Pitfall J - Criteria stage becomes appraisal

**Failure:** options are already being ranked.

**Challenge:** define the standards now. Project and compare later.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The criteria connect to a real decision and working problem.
- The primary objective is visible.
- The appraisal gate has been considered where the nature of change affects the criteria set.
- Important heterogeneous effects are not averaged away by default.
- Evaluative criteria are separated from practical constraints.
- Every important criterion has a direction or threshold.
- Equity, fairness or justice are specified rather than named vaguely.
- Conceptual values are not silently replaced by convenient proxies.
- Weighting judgments are visible.
- Important non-commensurable values are not forced into one metric.
- Options have not been scored prematurely.

## Should pass

- Opportunity cost is visible where scarce resources matter.
- Rights and process values have been considered where relevant.
- Robustness and improvability have been considered for implementation-heavy choices.
- Underrepresented interests have been considered.
- Duplicate criteria have been removed.
- The final set is small enough to support a clear comparison.

# Shared method calls

## Call `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- the decision may involve non-marginal change
- effects are materially heterogeneous
- fundamental uncertainty matters
- one common metric may hide the real choice
- direction of system change matters

## Call `strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- public purpose is unclear
- operational feasibility should be treated as a practical constraint
- required political support materially affects viability

## Call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` only where:

- a criterion cannot yet be operationalised because the relevant outcome or mechanism is too uncertain
- learning itself is a condition for later appraisal

Do not turn criteria selection into experiment design.

# One-line reference

> Good criteria make clear what public outcome matters, which other consequences and constraints can change the choice, what better means for each dimension, and where value judgments or non-commensurable outcomes must remain visible rather than being hidden inside a score.
