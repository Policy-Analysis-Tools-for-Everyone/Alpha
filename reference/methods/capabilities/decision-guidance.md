# Decision - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the policy-analysis spine and adds only the existing shared and product material that changes how the final choice should be made. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Seven and the closing guidance from Step Six - used for actually choosing, using difficulty deciding as a diagnostic, narrowing to serious alternatives, testing adoption and implementation, the twenty-dollar-bill test, status-quo resistance and the need for an actor with enough incentive and ability to carry the proposal. **[T]** `strategic-triangle-guidance.md` - called as a final check on public value, operational feasibility and required political support. **[U]** `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` - called when the remaining uncertainty may justify learning before full commitment, especially where the choice can be staged or reversed. **[A]** `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called for lock-in, path dependence, review conditions, multi-dimensional judgment and the decision record. **[P]** MDEE.MD product roadmap - used for the project-level requirements that accountable choices stay with people, high-impact or contested decisions receive human review and specialist input, and decision records preserve unresolved uncertainty, dissent and conditions for review. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user *reach a reasoned policy choice, state why that choice follows from the preceding analysis, test whether it is viable enough to act on, identify what could overturn it, and preserve the reasoning for later review*. The agent supports the decision. It does not take accountable public authority from the person or institution responsible for it.

## How to use this document

When the analysis has reached the point where a choice is needed:

1. **Name the decision and the decision maker.**
2. **Reduce the field to the serious alternatives.**
3. **Choose on the current analysis.**
4. **State the decisive reason and the trade-off being accepted.**
5. **If the choice still feels difficult, diagnose why.**
6. **Ask whether further learning is worth delaying the choice.**
7. **Check whether the decision can be staged, reversed or reviewed.**
8. **Run a compact Strategic Triangle check.**
9. **Apply the twenty-dollar-bill test.**
10. **Test the strongest serious contrary case.**
11. **Record unresolved uncertainty, dissent and conditions for review.**
12. **Keep accountable judgment with the authorised person or institution.**
13. **Move to storytelling once the choice is clear enough to communicate.**

Step Seven should be short in live use.

Most of the work should already have been done.

# Part 1 - Make the choice

## 1. Name the decision

Do not begin with:

> Which option is best?

Begin with:

> **What decision is being made, by whom, at what level of commitment?**

Examples:

- approve full adoption
- fund the next stage
- choose between 2 policy packages
- continue current policy
- stop an intervention
- run a bounded test before wider commitment
- legislate
- procure
- recommend one option to an authorised decision maker

The level of commitment matters because the same evidence may justify a reversible trial but not a national permanent decision.

## 2. Identify who has accountable authority [P]

The agent may help analyse and recommend.

It does not become the accountable decision maker.

Identify:

- who can formally choose
- who is accountable for the consequences
- whether specialist approval is required
- whether legal, ethical, equality, safety or technical review is needed

For high-impact or contested decisions, the project requires human review, specialist input and direct attention to rights, equity, ethics and affected groups.

Do not write as though an AI-generated recommendation settles the decision.

## 3. Narrow to the serious alternatives [B]

By this stage, weak and exploratory options should have fallen away.

Ask:

- Which alternatives still have a credible claim?
- Why were the others removed?
- Are we comparing the preferred option with the strongest realistic competitor?
- Is business-as-usual still serious enough to retain?

Do not preserve a large menu merely because the options stage started broad.

## 4. Choose [B]

Bardach's instruction is direct:

> **Pretend you are the decision maker and decide what to do based on the analysis.**

Do not retreat into:

- there are advantages and disadvantages
- it depends
- there is no perfect answer
- the final choice is political

Those may all be true.

They do not tell us what the current analysis supports.

A decision can be conditional or provisional and still be a decision.

## 5. State the decisive reason

Complete:

> **Prefer `[option]` because `[decisive reason]`.**

The decisive reason should come from work already done:

- stronger expected outcome
- acceptable trade-off
- binding constraint
- better feasibility
- lower serious downside
- preservation of future options
- stronger public-value case
- another established criterion

Do not invent a new criterion at the finish line.

## 6. State the trade-off being accepted

Complete:

> **This choice accepts `[sacrifice / risk / cost]` in exchange for `[gain]`.**

This makes the value judgment visible.

If the choice depends on giving one value greater priority, say so.

Do not hide the judgment inside a score.

# Part 2 - Use difficulty deciding as evidence

## 7. Treat decision difficulty as a diagnostic [B]

If choosing remains unusually difficult, ask:

> **What exactly is stopping the choice?**

Possible answers:

- the central trade-off is still unclear
- a key outcome forecast is too weak
- a criterion is contested
- implementation is not credible
- political support is uncertain
- a serious option is missing
- one uncertainty could reverse the choice
- the value judgment has not been made

Then return to the method that owns the gap.

Do not add another page of caveats when the real problem is unfinished analysis.

## 8. Distinguish uncertainty from indecision [B][U]

Uncertainty is normal.

Ask:

- Could the remaining uncertainty realistically change the preferred option?
- Is it already reflected in ranges or scenarios?
- Can we act responsibly while preserving room to change?
- Would another learning exercise produce enough decision value to justify delay?

Do not demand certainty.

Do not use uncertainty as an excuse to avoid judgment.

## 9. Decide whether to learn more or act [U]

Call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when a material unknown remains.

Ask:

- What is the critical uncertainty?
- Would resolving it change the decision?
- What is the lightest credible learning move?
- What does learning cost in money, time and delay?
- What happens if we act now and learn during delivery?

Possible decisions include:

- choose now
- choose conditionally
- stage the commitment
- run a bounded test
- gather one missing piece of evidence
- defer because the current evidence is not decision-ready

"Learn more" needs a stated question and decision rule.

# Part 3 - Consider reversibility and commitment

## 10. Ask how reversible the decision is [U][A][P]

Before demanding more evidence, ask what happens if the choice proves wrong.

A more reversible choice may justify action under greater uncertainty.

A hard-to-reverse choice deserves stronger scrutiny.

Consider:

- financial sunk cost
- legal commitment
- infrastructure
- contracts
- organisational restructuring
- technology lock-in
- effects on rights or safety
- public expectations
- political commitment
- dependency on a supplier or platform
- closure of future options

Do not turn reversibility into a simple yes/no label.

State what is difficult to undo and why.

## 11. Preserve useful future choices where that matters [A]

Ask:

- Does this option close off another path?
- Does it create lock-in before important uncertainty resolves?
- Could a staged choice preserve more useful future options?
- Does delay itself create lock-in or lost opportunity?

Preserving choice has value under uncertainty.

It also has costs.

Do not assume delay or flexibility is automatically preferable.

## 12. Use staged commitment where it fits the decision [U][A]

A staged decision can separate:

- what we are willing to commit to now
- what requires more evidence
- what conditions permit the next stage

Examples:

- initial cohort then expansion
- limited funding then release of later funds
- prototype then operational rollout
- temporary regulation then review
- one component first, another after evidence arrives

Staging should correspond to a real uncertainty or delivery dependency.

Do not use staging solely to avoid choosing.

# Part 4 - Run the Strategic Triangle check

## 13. Ask whether the proposition is worthwhile enough [T]

Use a compact check:

- What public result is expected?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears cost, harm or risk?
- Does the recommendation still address the original problem?
- Compared with the strongest alternative, is the value case good enough?

Do not reopen the whole criteria exercise.

This is a final coherence check.

## 14. Ask whether the proposition is deliverable enough [T]

Check the capacity assumptions that could defeat the recommendation.

Ask:

- Is the required funding realistically obtainable?
- Is there legal authority?
- Does the necessary technical capability exist?
- Are the people and skills available?
- Can the organisations involved manage the work?
- Does the evidence of feasibility match the intended scale and pace?

Do not assume capacity will appear after approval.

## 15. Ask whether the required support is sufficient [T]

Identify the actors whose backing, consent, funding, cooperation or non-opposition is needed.

Ask:

- Do they support the purpose?
- Do they believe the delivery model?
- Could they block, delay or withdraw something essential?
- Is support conditional on assumptions the analysis no longer believes?

Do not equate general popularity with political feasibility.

## 16. State the binding weakness [T]

The proposition may be:

- viable enough across all 3 dimensions
- valuable and supported, but capacity is weak
- valuable and deliverable, but support is weak
- deliverable and supported, but the public-value case is weak
- weak on several dimensions

Ask:

> **Is the weakness acceptable for this stage, repairable before action, or serious enough to change the choice?**

Perfect strategic fit is not required.

Known weaknesses need a reasoned response.

# Part 5 - Apply the twenty-dollar-bill test

## 17. Ask why the idea is not already happening [B]

Bardach's test is:

> **If this is such a good idea, why is it not happening already?**

The point is to find an assumption that the analysis has missed.

Possible explanations include:

- actor resistance
- institutional fragmentation
- weak incentives
- missing authority
- funding
- implementation difficulty
- lack of ownership
- a hidden downside
- a poor previous attempt
- insufficient evidence until now

Do not answer with generic language.

Find the actual reason or mark it unknown.

## 18. Look for status-quo resistance [B]

Ask:

- Who loses authority?
- Who loses resources?
- Who loses discretion?
- Who carries new work or risk?
- Which routine must change?
- Who has the means to resist?

Use stakeholder analysis rather than guessing motives.

If the recommendation assumes powerful actors will simply cooperate, it is incomplete.

## 19. Look for the missing owner or policy entrepreneur [B]

Ask:

- Who has reason to carry this?
- Who has enough authority or access?
- Who can coordinate the required actors?
- Who is willing to spend organisational or political capital?
- Who remains responsible after initial approval?

A policy can create public value and still go nowhere because no actor has enough incentive to make it happen.

## 20. Treat a failed twenty-dollar-bill test as a redesign prompt [B]

Failure does not automatically kill the policy.

Possible redesigns include:

- change the scale
- change the sequencing
- change the delivery body
- change who bears cost
- add a capability-building stage
- change the institutional arrangement
- address a blocking interest
- return to the alternatives method

Do not protect the original option simply because it reached the decision stage.

# Part 6 - Test the strongest contrary case

## 21. Ask for the serious argument against the recommendation [P]

The decision stage needs challenge.

Do not ask for a long generic list of risks.

Ask:

> **What is the strongest evidence-based case that the preferred option is wrong?**

A serious contrary case may argue that:

- the causal mechanism is weak
- the forecast is optimistic
- a value has been underweighted
- implementation will fail
- a distributional harm is unacceptable
- another option preserves more future choice
- political or legal assumptions are weak

State it fairly.

Do not construct an easy objection solely to dismiss it.

## 22. Distinguish dissent from unresolved evidence [P]

A dissenting view can arise because people disagree about:

### Facts

What is happening?

### Forecasts

What will happen?

### Values

What matters more?

### Risk tolerance

Which error is less acceptable?

### Feasibility

What can actually be delivered?

### Authority

Who should decide?

Record which kind of disagreement remains.

Do not call a value disagreement an evidence gap.

## 23. Record material dissent [P]

Where a serious reviewer, stakeholder or analyst reaches a different conclusion, record:

- the alternative view
- its main reason
- what evidence supports it
- why the final recommendation differs
- what future evidence would strengthen the dissenting case

Do not require consensus as a condition for decision.

Do not erase serious dissent from the record once a choice is made.

## 24. Use direct challenge for high-impact cases [P]

Where the decision is high-impact or contested, seek appropriate human review.

Depending on the case, that may include:

- legal
- financial
- operational
- scientific
- technical
- equality
- ethics
- safety
- affected-community expertise

The agent can identify the need.

It should not pretend to substitute for specialist judgment it does not have.

# Part 7 - Decide whether the case is ready

## 25. Use a decision-ready test

A case is decision-ready when:

- the serious alternatives are clear
- the main evidence is good enough for the decision
- the criteria are visible
- the decision-relevant outcomes have been projected as far as reasonably possible
- the main trade-off is explicit
- material uncertainty is understood
- public value, capacity and required support are good enough for the proposed level of commitment
- the strongest serious contrary case has been considered
- the accountable decision maker can understand what they are accepting

Decision-ready does not mean certain.

## 26. Say when the case is not decision-ready

Do not force a recommendation if a specific unresolved issue could plausibly reverse it.

State:

> **Not decision-ready because `[gap]`.**

Then state:

> **The next move is `[specific analytical or learning action]`.**

Weak:

> More work is needed.

Useful:

> The choice between A and B turns on whether the service can absorb 15,000 additional cases per month. Current evidence does not establish that. Test operational capacity before recommending national adoption.

## 27. Do not reject every imperfect option

All serious policy options have weaknesses.

Ask whether the weakness is:

- understood
- tolerable
- repairable
- monitorable
- capable of reversing the choice

Do not demand perfect feasibility or complete political agreement.

Use the standard appropriate to the level of commitment.

# Part 8 - Record the decision

## 28. Preserve the reasoning [A][P]

The decision record should allow a later reviewer to reconstruct:

- what was decided
- by whom
- which alternatives were serious
- why the chosen option was preferred
- what trade-off was accepted
- what value judgment mattered
- what evidence and forecasts carried the choice
- what uncertainty remained
- what dissent remained
- what capacity or support conditions mattered
- what would justify reopening the decision

Do not turn the record into a duplicate of the full analysis.

## 29. State the decision conditionally where necessary [B][U]

Possible forms:

### Recommendation

> Proceed with Option B.

### Conditional recommendation

> Proceed with Option B if the implementation cost remains below £X and the legal route is confirmed.

### Staged recommendation

> Approve Stage 1 for 6 months, with wider rollout conditional on uptake and delivery thresholds.

### Not decision-ready

> Do not choose between A and B until the capacity question is resolved.

Use the form that accurately reflects the evidence.

## 30. Record the switchpoint where known [A][U]

If the recommendation changes at a known threshold, state it.

Examples:

- cost
- uptake
- effect
- delivery time
- safety outcome
- opposition from a required actor

This makes later review much easier.

## 31. Record review conditions [A][P]

A review condition should be something that can materially change the case.

Examples:

- cost exceeds threshold
- uptake falls below milestone
- legal position changes
- a key actor withdraws support
- an expected feedback fails to appear
- a serious harm emerges
- a better option becomes feasible
- delivery evidence undermines the causal theory

Do not add a generic "review in 12 months" unless timing itself matters.

## 32. Distinguish a review condition from normal monitoring

Monitoring asks:

> Is the policy operating?

A decision review asks:

> **Has something changed enough that the original choice should be reconsidered?**

Keep those separate.

## 33. Preserve authorship and accountability [P]

The record should distinguish:

- analysis produced by the agent
- user-supplied evidence
- specialist advice
- proposed recommendation
- the person or institution that accepted the decision

Do not let the presence of AI-generated analysis obscure who made the choice.

# Decision scaffold

1. **Decision:** What exactly is being decided?
2. **Accountable decision maker:** Who has authority and responsibility?
3. **Serious alternatives:** Which options remain?
4. **Current choice:** What does the analysis support?
5. **Decisive reason:** Why?
6. **Accepted trade-off:** What are we giving up for what?
7. **Value judgment:** Which priority does the choice reveal?
8. **Decision difficulty:** What, if anything, still prevents confidence?
9. **Critical uncertainty:** Could it reverse the choice?
10. **Learn or act:** Is further evidence worth the delay?
11. **Reversibility:** What is difficult to undo?
12. **Staging:** Could commitment be broken into meaningful stages?
13. **Public-value check:** Is the proposition worthwhile enough?
14. **Capacity check:** Is it deliverable enough at the proposed scale and pace?
15. **Support check:** Is the required support sufficient?
16. **Twenty-dollar-bill test:** Why is this not happening already?
17. **Ownership:** Who has the incentive and ability to carry it?
18. **Contrary case:** What is the strongest serious argument against?
19. **Dissent:** What disagreement remains and of what kind?
20. **Decision readiness:** Ready / conditional / staged / not ready.
21. **Review condition:** What future evidence should reopen the choice?
22. **Record:** Preserve rationale, uncertainty and accountability.

→ **Decision statement:** `[Accountable decision maker]` should `[decision]` because `[decisive reason]`. This accepts `[main trade-off / value judgment]`. The case is `[ready / conditional / staged / not ready]` because `[reason]`. The choice depends most on `[material assumption or uncertainty]`. It should be reconsidered if `[review condition]`. The strongest serious contrary case is `[dissent / challenge]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Refusing to choose

**Failure:** the output ends with pros and cons.

**Challenge:** state what the current analysis supports.

## Pitfall B - Recommendation by instinct

**Failure:** the choice does not follow from criteria, outcomes and trade-offs.

**Challenge:** name the decisive reason.

## Pitfall C - New criterion at the finish line

**Failure:** a concern appears only because it favours the preferred option.

**Challenge:** return to criteria and make the change visible.

## Pitfall D - Uncertainty used to avoid judgment

**Failure:** any unknown blocks action.

**Challenge:** ask whether the unknown could actually reverse the choice.

## Pitfall E - False confidence

**Failure:** a small analytical advantage becomes a categorical recommendation.

**Challenge:** use conditional or staged language where warranted.

## Pitfall F - Learning for its own sake

**Failure:** more research is recommended without a decision it could change.

**Challenge:** apply the uncertainty method.

## Pitfall G - "Reversible" used casually

**Failure:** a decision is called reversible despite sunk cost, legal commitment, lock-in or harm.

**Challenge:** state what is actually hard to undo.

## Pitfall H - Staging as indecision

**Failure:** commitment is broken into stages without a learning or delivery reason.

**Challenge:** state what condition governs the next stage.

## Pitfall I - Political feasibility means capitulation

**Failure:** a worthwhile option is rejected simply because someone currently opposes it.

**Challenge:** diagnose whether support can credibly change.

## Pitfall J - Capacity assumed after approval

**Failure:** the policy depends on capability appearing later.

**Challenge:** state who builds it and how.

## Pitfall K - Twenty-dollar-bill test skipped

**Failure:** an apparently obvious improvement has no explanation for why it is not already happening.

**Challenge:** find the barrier or mark it unknown.

## Pitfall L - No owner

**Failure:** "government" or "the organisation" is expected to carry the recommendation.

**Challenge:** identify an actor with incentive and ability.

## Pitfall M - Weak challenge

**Failure:** the contrary case is a straw objection.

**Challenge:** state the strongest serious argument against the recommendation.

## Pitfall N - Dissent erased

**Failure:** disagreement disappears once the final option is chosen.

**Challenge:** record material dissent and what would strengthen it.

## Pitfall O - AI treated as decision maker

**Failure:** the agent presents its recommendation as authorised public judgment.

**Challenge:** identify the accountable person or institution.

## Pitfall P - Generic review date

**Failure:** the record says "review later" with no condition linked to the decision.

**Challenge:** name the evidence or event that should reopen the choice.

## Pitfall Q - Endless decision record

**Failure:** the record repeats the entire case.

**Challenge:** preserve only what another reviewer needs to understand why the choice was made and when it should change.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The decision and accountable decision maker are clear.
- The serious alternatives have been narrowed.
- The method reaches a choice, conditional choice, staged choice or explicit not-ready judgment.
- The decisive reason follows from the preceding analysis.
- The accepted trade-off and material value judgment are visible.
- Decision difficulty has been diagnosed rather than hidden.
- Material uncertainty has been tested for whether it could reverse the choice.
- Reversibility or lock-in has been considered where commitment is significant.
- Public value, operational feasibility and required support have received a compact final check.
- The twenty-dollar-bill test has been considered.
- The proposal has an identifiable actor capable of carrying it.
- The strongest serious contrary case has been heard.
- Material dissent remains visible.
- High-impact or contested decisions retain appropriate human and specialist review.
- The agent does not present itself as the accountable authority.
- A decision record preserves the rationale and any condition that should reopen the choice.

## Should pass

- A staged commitment has been considered where it could reduce costly uncertainty.
- A known switchpoint is recorded.
- Review conditions are tied to evidence or events capable of changing the recommendation.
- The recommendation reconnects to the original problem and primary criterion.
- Weak options have been removed rather than retained for symmetry.
- A failed twenty-dollar-bill test has triggered redesign where appropriate.
- The record distinguishes analysis from accepted human judgment.

# Shared method calls

This file owns the **final analytical choice**.

## Call `strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- public value, delivery capacity or required support could defeat the preferred option
- one of those dimensions is the binding weakness
- the decision needs a final test of whether the proposition is viable enough for its current stage

Use a compact readout.

Do not reproduce the whole triangle.

## Call `uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- a decision-sensitive uncertainty remains
- the choice may be staged
- a reversible action could produce useful evidence
- the question is whether to learn more or act now
- a review condition needs to connect to a real learning question

## Call `risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- lock-in or path dependence matters
- preserving future choices matters
- asymmetric risk or opportunity affects the final choice
- a decision record needs to preserve appraisal assumptions and review conditions

## Call `stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` when:

- the source of support or opposition is unclear
- status-quo resistance needs diagnosis
- the actor expected to carry the policy may not have enough power, incentive or access

Do not guess stakeholder motives here.

# Canonical boundaries across the method library

## Problem

`problem-definition-guidance.md` owns the problem.

The decision method only checks that the recommendation still addresses it.

## Evidence

`evidence-guidance.md` owns the evidence position.

The decision method asks whether the remaining uncertainty is good enough for action.

## Alternatives

`alternatives-guidance.md` owns construction and redesign of the option set.

If the preferred option fails a final viability test, return there.

## Criteria

`criteria-guidance.md` owns what counts as better.

Do not invent a new value at the decision stage.

## Outcomes

`outcomes-guidance.md` owns future projections.

Do not repair a weak forecast with confident decision language.

## Trade-offs

`trade-offs-guidance.md` owns the exchange between outcomes.

This method chooses which side of that exchange to accept.

## Story

`storytelling-guidance.md` owns how the recommendation is communicated.

The internal analytical choice should be made before deciding how explicitly to present it.

## Evaluation

`agent-evaluation-guidance.md` owns whether the AI agent performed this method well.

Do not interpret a user accepting the recommendation as proof that the decision method worked.

# One-line reference

> A good policy decision makes the current choice explicit, shows the trade-off and judgment behind it, tests whether uncertainty justifies learning or staged commitment, checks public value, capacity and required support, challenges the preferred option with the strongest serious contrary case, and leaves the accountable human decision and the conditions for review visible.
