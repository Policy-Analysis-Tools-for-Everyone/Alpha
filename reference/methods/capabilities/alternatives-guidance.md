# Alternatives - Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method keeps Eugene Bardach as the main policy-analysis spine and adds Peter J. May where his work changes how the agent should generate options. **[B]** Eugene Bardach, *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis: The Eightfold Path to More Effective Problem Solving*, Fourth Edition, Step Three, Appendix B and related design material - used for starting broad, business-as-usual, learning as an option, causal and system models, strategic thrusts, variants, continuous choices, design problems, transition and learning from practice elsewhere. **[M]** Peter J. May, *Hints for Crafting Alternative Policies* (1981) - used for the idea that alternatives are tentative hypotheses rather than discovered answers; iteration between problem framing and option design; the dangers of stock solutions, top-down prescription, straw alternatives and do-everything packages; and his practical method of identifying policy variables, finding their feasible manipulations, then packaging different combinations into coherent strategies. **[U]** `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` - called when the sensible course is to learn before committing, or when a proposed option should be tested before wider adoption. **[R]** `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` - called when options interact as packages or pathways, create material lock-in, or need appraisal as part of wider system change. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user *generate a serious set of policy choices, understand what can actually be changed, turn policy variables into plausible strategies, distinguish strategies from variants, and narrow the menu without manufacturing decoys*. This method stops before detailed appraisal.

## How to use this document

When asked to generate, improve, challenge or organise policy options:

1. **Return to the working problem.** What condition is the user trying to change?
2. **Keep the problem and options iterative.** Early options may expose a weak problem frame, and a revised problem may reveal different options.
3. **Identify what can actually be manipulated.** What rules, incentives, resources, processes, responsibilities, thresholds, channels or other policy variables can the relevant actor influence?
4. **Identify feasible manipulations of those variables.**
5. **Package different combinations into coherent strategies.**
6. **Include business-as-usual and consider learning as an option where appropriate.**
7. **Check for stock solutions, top-down assumptions, straw alternatives and do-everything packages.**
8. **Separate strategic alternatives from implementation, financing, scale and delivery variants.**
9. **Consider combinations and pathways where options interact.**
10. **Narrow to a small set of serious choices only after exploring the choice space.**
11. **Stop before scoring them.** Criteria, outcome projection and trade-offs belong later.

The goal is not to produce 3 options. The goal is to create a set of **genuine choices**.

# Part 1 - Treat alternatives as hypotheses, not answers

## 1. Do not assume the problem contains its own solution [M]

May rejects the idea that defining the problem correctly automatically reveals the answer.

A problem definition can:

- reveal important conditions
- suggest possible intervention points
- expose constraints
- make some actions more plausible than others

It does not uniquely determine a policy.

Several courses of action may address the same problem.

Do not infer:

> "The problem is X, therefore the solution is Y."

Ask instead:

> **What different actions could plausibly improve these conditions?**

## 2. Treat alternatives as tentative hypotheses [M]

May describes policy alternatives as tentative hypotheses about courses of action that might transform an undesirable set of conditions into a better one.

That means an option is not:

- a discovered truth
- a guaranteed solution
- a complete answer to every dimension of the problem

It is a proposition about action that will later need to be tested against:

- evidence
- criteria
- projected outcomes
- feasibility
- trade-offs

A useful stance is:

> **If we changed these parts of the system in this way, we think the problem would improve because...**

Do not build success into the option label.

## 3. Expect every serious option to have shortcomings [M]

May warns against searching for a uniformly best alternative.

Public-policy goals often conflict.

A proposal may improve one dimension while:

- costing more
- creating another burden
- proving harder to implement
- shifting risk
- helping some groups more than others

The purpose of the options stage is to create plausible courses of action whose differences can later be examined.

Do not try to make every option solve everything.

## 4. Iterate between problem framing and option design [M][B]

May argues against a rigid sequence in which the problem is fully defined and then handed off to option generation.

Early option thinking can reveal:

- that the problem has been framed too narrowly
- that the presumed root cause is not manipulable
- that a relevant constraint was omitted
- that another level of the problem is more useful
- that the current actor lacks the means to act at the level first described

Likewise, revising the problem can reveal new options.

Use iteration deliberately.

Do not keep a weak problem frame merely because it was completed in an earlier step.

## 5. Keep the problem within the reach of the relevant actor [M]

May's idea of feasible manipulation depends on who is doing the acting.

A national government, local authority, regulator, hospital, charity and frontline team have different policy variables available to them.

Ask:

- Who is the relevant actor?
- What can they directly change?
- What can they influence but not control?
- What sits outside their authority?
- Could another actor be brought into the strategy?

Do not generate options whose central action sits outside the decision maker's real reach without saying what additional authority or partnership would be required.

# Part 2 - Find what can actually be changed

## 6. Identify the main policy variables [M]

May's practical method begins with the factors that can be manipulated to address the problem.

A **policy variable** is something about the policy system that the relevant actor could plausibly alter.

Examples might include:

- eligibility
- price
- tax rate
- subsidy
- information
- timing
- staffing
- participation requirements
- incentives
- penalties
- enforcement intensity
- service channel
- decision rights
- funding formula
- benefit level
- sequencing
- responsibility
- access rules
- reporting requirements

The right variables depend on the problem.

Do not begin with a generic menu of policy instruments if the mechanism and context suggest a different set of choices.

## 7. Derive variables from the problem and operating context [M][B]

Useful variables often become visible through:

- a causal account of the problem
- operational process
- incentives
- actors' behaviour
- institutional rules
- constraints
- bottlenecks
- existing policy design
- frontline practice

Ask:

> **What factors in the current system could we change that would plausibly affect the problem?**

If you cannot name the variable an option is changing, the option may be too vague.

## 8. Use backward reasoning from where behaviour actually occurs [M]

May draws on Richard Elmore's backward-mapping idea.

Policies designed solely from the top can look sensible in formal plans but fail where real behaviour occurs.

Ask:

- Where does the relevant behaviour actually happen?
- Who has discretion at that point?
- What shapes their choices?
- What could change at the point of delivery, compliance or use?
- Which higher-level action would make that local change possible?

Do not assume formal authority produces the intended behaviour automatically.

## 9. Identify the feasible manipulations of each variable [M]

Once an important variable is identified, ask what settings or changes are genuinely possible.

For example:

**Variable:** participation requirement

Possible feasible manipulations:

- voluntary
- voluntary with incentive
- conditional participation
- mandatory participation for a defined group

**Variable:** service channel

Possible feasible manipulations:

- digital only
- digital default with assisted route
- mixed digital and telephone
- face-to-face for defined cases

The point is not to enumerate every imaginable value.

The point is to identify the **meaningful feasible range**.

## 10. Let constraints shape feasible manipulations [M]

May makes feasibility part of option construction rather than something checked only at the end.

Relevant constraints may include:

- law
- budget
- administrative capability
- technology
- staffing
- authority
- political conditions
- time
- physical infrastructure

A constraint should shape the feasible range.

It should not automatically collapse the whole option space to the status quo.

Ask:

> **What is genuinely infeasible, and what is merely difficult under current arrangements?**

Use the Strategic Triangle where a deeper capacity or support check is required.

## 11. Distinguish manipulable from non-manipulable conditions [M]

Some causal factors may matter greatly but be outside the actor's control.

A useful analysis distinguishes:

- direct policy variables
- factors that can be influenced indirectly
- contextual conditions that must be accepted for now

Do not turn every causal factor into an intervention.

Sometimes the best policy response works around a condition rather than trying to change it.

# Part 3 - Generate without relying on stock answers

## 12. Avoid misclassifying the problem to fit a familiar toolkit [M]

May warns that analysts can force a complex problem into a familiar category because the category comes with familiar solutions.

For example, defining a problem entirely as:

- allocation
- distribution
- information
- enforcement
- market failure

can make one family of interventions appear inevitable.

Use categories as prompts.

Do not let them determine the option set before the actual system has been understood.

## 13. Challenge stock solutions [M]

A stock solution is a familiar intervention applied because it often appears in the analyst's toolkit.

Examples might include:

- provide information
- create an app
- increase enforcement
- add a subsidy
- impose a charge
- create a new team
- introduce a reporting requirement

Ask:

> **What mechanism in this problem makes this intervention relevant?**

A familiar tool can be the right answer.

Its familiarity is not evidence that it fits.

## 14. Check whether the option treats a symptom or a cause [M]

May notes that quick fixes may address symptoms while leaving deeper causes untouched.

Ask for each option:

- What condition does it change directly?
- Is that condition a symptom, mechanism or root cause?
- If it treats only the symptom, is that still worthwhile?
- Is a deeper intervention feasible?
- Is the symptom treatment needed while the deeper change develops?

Do not reject symptom treatment automatically.

Sometimes a bounded symptom response is the best feasible course.

Name what it does and does not address.

## 15. Look sideways for ideas [B][M]

Useful option sources include:

- other jurisdictions
- previous policy episodes
- other organisations
- substantively different problems with a similar mechanism
- local practice already solving part of the problem
- existing frontline workarounds

May calls attention to analogies across:

- time
- issue areas
- organisations

Use them to generate ideas about policy variables and possible manipulations.

Do not import the full policy package without checking what differs.

## 16. Scout existing practice before assuming novelty [U]

Where relevant, ask:

- Who is already trying to solve this?
- Has a frontline team already developed a workaround?
- Is another place using a different configuration of the same policy variables?
- What can that practice teach us about feasible manipulations?

Scouting is option generation.

It is not proof of effectiveness.

Use `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` if the transfer or effect needs testing.

# Part 4 - Package manipulations into strategies

## 17. Build strategies from combinations of variables [M]

May's central method is:

1. identify policy variables
2. identify feasible manipulations of each
3. combine them into coherent strategies

Do not treat each individual manipulation as a complete option if the real policy requires several elements to work together.

A strategy might combine:

- an eligibility rule
- a funding model
- a participation requirement
- a delivery channel
- an incentive
- an implementation arrangement

The combination should make sense as a whole.

## 18. Do not create "low, medium, high" packages mechanically [M]

May warns that simply combining:

- all low settings
- all medium settings
- all high settings

rarely creates sensible strategies.

Different variables may need different settings to create a coherent option.

For example, a strong participation requirement might need:

- narrower eligibility
- a different incentive
- more implementation support

Package elements according to the logic of the strategy, not a mathematical pattern.

## 19. Give each strategy a clear strategic thrust [B]

After packaging the elements, state what distinguishes the strategy.

Examples:

- increase compliance through incentives
- make participation mandatory for a narrow cohort
- reduce the need for applications through automatic eligibility
- shift responsibility from the individual to the provider
- reduce demand through upstream prevention

The strategic thrust should explain the core intervention logic.

Do not use labels such as:

- better service
- effective model
- stronger option
- user-friendly solution

Those describe desired qualities, not policy choices.

## 20. Separate strategy from implementation variant [B][M]

A strategy may have variants concerning:

- scale
- funding source
- delivery body
- timetable
- geography
- channel
- procurement
- staffing model

Ask:

> **Would changing this feature alter the underlying mechanism of action?**

If no, it is probably a variant.

Keep variants under the parent strategy until there is a reason to compare them separately.

## 21. Treat strategies as lumpy packages [M]

May notes that strategies are often imperfect combinations of different policy elements.

Do not expect every element to point in exactly the same ideological or administrative direction.

A plausible package may combine:

- incentives and regulation
- universal and targeted elements
- central standards and local discretion
- immediate mitigation and longer-term change

Judge coherence by whether the elements work together toward the intended mechanism.

Do not reject a useful package because it is not conceptually pure.

## 22. Allow combined options where combination is part of the mechanism [B][M][R]

Some actions are alternatives.

Others are complements.

May notes that a combination of policy instruments may outperform either one alone.

Where interaction matters, consider:

- option A
- option B
- A + B
- staged A then B
- portfolio approaches

Use `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` where interactions, feedbacks or packages materially affect the appraisal.

Do not create packages solely to produce a "do everything" option.

# Part 5 - Include the base case and learning choices

## 23. Include business-as-usual [B]

Do not call it "do nothing".

The world continues to change.

The base case should capture relevant:

- current policy
- trends
- existing implementation
- expected external change

Ask:

> **What is likely to happen if no new course is adopted?**

This is the baseline later outcome projections should compare against.

## 24. Treat "learn first" as a possible strategy where uncertainty is binding [B][U]

Sometimes the serious alternative is not immediate adoption.

It may be:

- investigate
- prototype
- run a bounded live test
- stage the commitment
- delay one part while resolving a critical uncertainty

This belongs in the options menu only where learning could materially improve the later decision.

Use `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` to define:

- the uncertainty
- the learning move
- what evidence would change the decision

Do not use "pilot" as a vague compromise option.

## 25. Separate a learning strategy from an implementation pilot [U]

A learning strategy exists to reduce a stated uncertainty.

An implementation pilot may exist mainly to:

- phase rollout
- manage capacity
- reduce operational risk

They can overlap.

Ask:

> **What exactly are we trying to learn, and what decision will the result change?**

If there is no answer, do not label routine phased implementation as experimentation.

# Part 6 - Avoid fake choice

## 26. Do not create 3 options because 3 feels normal [M][B]

May explicitly warns against "dummying up" alternatives.

A weak menu often looks like:

- broken status quo
- implausible extreme
- preferred middle option

This creates the appearance of choice while ensuring one option wins by design.

Do not do it.

There is no ideal number of alternatives.

## 27. Retain only alternatives a reasonable decision maker might choose [M]

The final set should contain serious contenders.

An exploratory list can include:

- extreme options
- provocative boundaries
- ideas intended to reveal the choice space

The final decision menu should not retain them merely as decoys.

Ask:

> **Could a reasonable decision maker choose this option after seeing its real consequences?**

If not, explain why it is still present or remove it.

## 28. Avoid the do-everything option [M]

A policy package that tries to address:

- every cause
- every affected group
- every objective
- every political concern

may become:

- prohibitively expensive
- difficult to implement
- internally contradictory
- impossible to evaluate

Ask:

> **Which dimensions of the problem must this strategy address, and which can it reasonably leave outside its scope?**

A bounded strategy can be stronger than a comprehensive-looking package.

## 29. Avoid false differentiation [M][B]

Options are not genuinely different because:

- one is larger
- one is smaller
- one uses another delivery body
- one starts 6 months later

if their core mechanism is the same.

Those may be meaningful variants.

Do not present them as distinct strategies unless the variation changes the decision in a substantive way.

## 30. Avoid false singularity [M]

The opposite error is to collapse genuinely different strategies into one broad category.

For example, "improve compliance" could conceal very different mechanisms:

- information
- incentives
- enforcement
- automation
- changing the rule itself

Separate them where the mechanism, consequences or trade-offs differ materially.

# Part 7 - Reduce a large option space without losing the choice

## 31. Expect many possible combinations [M]

If several policy variables have several feasible settings, the theoretical number of strategies can become large.

Do not attempt to compare every combination.

Use judgment to reduce the space.

## 32. Drop variables that do not materially distinguish strategies [M]

May's prison example shows that some variables can prove relatively unimportant to the strategic comparison.

If a variable:

- changes little across serious strategies
- does not materially affect outcomes
- belongs mainly to implementation

move it out of the high-level option set.

Do not let low-value detail make alternatives look more different than they are.

## 33. Increase the distance between alternatives where needed [M]

If several strategies differ only slightly, compare fewer, more meaningful configurations.

The purpose is to expose genuine choices.

For continuous variables:

- establish a feasible range
- choose policy-relevant points
- avoid tiny increments that do not change the reasoning

Do not confuse exhaustive enumeration with analytical quality.

## 34. Narrow only after the wider space has been explored [B][M]

An initial broad menu protects against premature closure.

A final narrow menu protects against cognitive overload.

May notes practical limits including:

- data
- analytical capacity
- number of criteria
- the decision maker's ability to compare many strategies

The narrowing should be explainable.

Record:

- what was merged
- what was removed
- why
- whether any politically salient alternative was excluded

## 35. Do not score options while you are still constructing them

Some basic feasibility judgment is unavoidable during design.

But detailed scoring against criteria too early can cause the existing criteria to suppress novel alternatives before they are properly formed.

At this stage ask:

> **Is this plausible enough to deserve serious comparison?**

Leave:

- formal criteria
- outcome estimates
- detailed trade-offs
- final ranking

to the later methods.

# Part 8 - Design systems and transitions deliberately

## 36. Treat some options as system designs [B]

Some policy choices are not one action.

They are a configuration of:

- rules
- roles
- processes
- channels
- technology
- incentives
- governance
- resources

In these cases, construct a coherent candidate system before comparing it.

Do not compare isolated components that cannot operate independently.

## 37. Use a target or resource envelope to focus open-ended design [B]

Where the design space is very large, use a working constraint.

Possible approaches:

### Target first

Define a desired level of improvement and develop a plausible configuration capable of producing it.

### Resource first

Define a plausible budget or capacity envelope and develop the strongest configuration achievable within it.

These are design devices.

The target or resource envelope can later be revised.

## 38. Distinguish the steady-state design from the route to it [B]

Ask separately:

### Future operating model

What does the policy look like once established?

### Transition

How do we move from today's system to that state?

Transition may require:

- temporary capacity
- sequencing
- legislation
- parallel systems
- migration
- negotiation
- staged adoption
- training
- learning

Do not reject a strong long-term design because the first transition concept is weak.

Improve the transition.

Do not accept a strong end-state option if no plausible transition can be found.

## 39. Use stakeholder and practitioner reaction to improve the strategy [B][M]

Input from:

- implementers
- affected people
- technical specialists
- partners
- decision makers

can expose:

- impossible manipulations
- hidden variables
- incentives the design missed
- political barriers
- operational dependencies
- useful existing practice

Use `stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` where the actor system itself needs analysis.

Do not treat consultation as a vote on which option wins.

# Alternative-construction scaffold

Use this where the user needs help creating or repairing the option set.

## A. Reconnect to the problem

1. **Working problem:** What condition are we trying to improve?
2. **Relevant actor:** Who has to choose or act?
3. **Mechanism:** What appears to be holding the problem in place?
4. **Frame check:** Does early option thinking reveal a better problem formulation?

## B. Find the manipulable variables

5. **Policy variables:** What can the relevant actor change?
6. **Indirect influence:** What can be influenced but not controlled?
7. **Context:** What important conditions cannot currently be changed?
8. **Feasible manipulations:** What meaningful settings are possible for each variable?

## C. Widen the option space

9. **Current proposals:** What is already on the table?
10. **Business-as-usual:** What happens under present trends?
11. **Stock-solution check:** Are familiar tools being assumed without a mechanism?
12. **Analogies / scouting:** What can be learnt from other places, times, organisations or local workarounds?
13. **Learning option:** Is resolving a critical uncertainty a serious alternative?

## D. Package strategies

14. **Combinations:** Which variable settings form coherent packages?
15. **Strategic thrust:** What distinguishes each package?
16. **Variants:** Which differences belong underneath the same strategy?
17. **Interactions:** Should any strategies be combined or sequenced?
18. **Transition:** Can the system move from current state to the proposed state?

## E. Narrow

19. **Straw check:** Could a reasonable decision maker choose each retained option?
20. **Do-everything check:** Is any package attempting too much?
21. **Distinctness check:** Do options use meaningfully different mechanisms?
22. **Cognitive check:** Can the serious menu be compared?
23. **Final menu:** Keep only the options needed for the next analytical stage.

→ **Alternatives menu:** `[business-as-usual where relevant] + [serious strategy A] + [serious strategy B] + [serious strategy C if warranted] + [learning/staged strategy where warranted]`. For each, state `[strategic thrust] + [main policy variables/manipulations] + [mechanism] + [important variants or transition issue]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - The problem supposedly contains the answer

**Failure:** one solution follows automatically from the problem statement.

**Challenge:** identify other plausible manipulations of the system.

## Pitfall B - Stock solution

**Failure:** a familiar policy tool is proposed without showing why it fits this problem.

**Challenge:** state the mechanism and policy variable it changes.

## Pitfall C - Misclassified problem

**Failure:** the issue is forced into a familiar category because that category comes with familiar interventions.

**Challenge:** reopen the frame and inspect what was excluded.

## Pitfall D - Top-down prescription

**Failure:** formal policy design ignores behaviour and discretion at the point where the policy is used or delivered.

**Challenge:** work backwards from real behaviour.

## Pitfall E - Symptom treatment presented as root-cause reform

**Failure:** an immediate fix is described as though it changes the underlying mechanism.

**Challenge:** say what level of the problem it addresses.

## Pitfall F - Three options for the sake of three

**Failure:** one preferred option, one extreme and one broken status quo form a fake menu.

**Challenge:** retain only serious contenders.

## Pitfall G - Do-everything package

**Failure:** an option attempts to satisfy every goal through one sprawling programme.

**Challenge:** define what the strategy must address and what it can leave out.

## Pitfall H - Low / medium / high masquerading as strategy

**Failure:** every policy variable is moved together to create 3 mechanically different packages.

**Challenge:** combine variable settings according to a coherent mechanism.

## Pitfall I - Implementation variant masquerading as a new strategy

**Failure:** the same mechanism appears as several options with different providers, budgets or start dates.

**Challenge:** group them under one strategic thrust.

## Pitfall J - Genuine strategies collapsed together

**Failure:** very different mechanisms are hidden under one broad heading.

**Challenge:** separate options where the causal logic and consequences differ.

## Pitfall K - Imported policy treated as ready-made

**Failure:** an intervention from another setting is copied without checking contextual differences.

**Challenge:** extract the useful variable or mechanism, then redesign for this context.

## Pitfall L - Pilot as compromise

**Failure:** "run a pilot" is added because the team cannot decide.

**Challenge:** name the uncertainty and what the pilot would allow the team to decide.

## Pitfall M - Package interaction ignored

**Failure:** options are assessed only individually even though they reinforce or undermine one another.

**Challenge:** identify combinations and pathways where interactions are material.

## Pitfall N - End state without transition

**Failure:** a compelling future design has no credible route from the current system.

**Challenge:** treat transition as part of the option design.

## Pitfall O - Option construction becomes appraisal

**Failure:** scores, weights and detailed outcome judgments eliminate novel options before they have been properly constructed.

**Challenge:** require plausibility now; compare formally later.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The menu contains **courses of action**, not desired outcomes.
- The relevant **decision maker or actor** is clear enough to judge what can be manipulated.
- Serious options have identifiable **policy variables or intervention points**.
- The final menu contains genuinely different **strategic mechanisms**, not cosmetic variants.
- Business-as-usual has been considered.
- No option assumes success through its name.
- No final option is retained merely as a straw alternative.
- The menu has not been forced to contain a predetermined number of options.
- A learning option is used only where it resolves a stated decision-sensitive uncertainty.
- Detailed criteria or outcome scoring has not silently selected the winner during option construction.

## Should pass

- Problem framing and option generation have been treated iteratively.
- Feasible manipulations have been identified for the important policy variables.
- Behaviour at the point of implementation or use has informed the design where relevant.
- Stock solutions have been challenged for fit.
- Symptom-level and mechanism-level interventions have been distinguished.
- Analogies or existing practice have been used as idea sources without assuming transfer.
- Strategies have been built from coherent combinations rather than mechanical low / medium / high settings.
- Important variants have been nested under their parent strategy.
- Policy packages or pathways have been considered where measures interact.
- Steady-state design and transition have been separated where needed.
- The final menu is small enough for serious comparison.

## Process reminder

- Alternatives and problem framing develop together.
- There is rarely one policy waiting to be discovered.
- Feasibility constrains option construction, but current arrangements should not be treated as immovable without examination.
- A broad initial search and a narrow final menu are compatible.
- The point is to create real choices before asking which one wins.

# Shared method calls

This file owns **construction of the choice set**.

It should call shared methods rather than copy them.

## Call `../shared/uncertainty-and-learning-guidance.md` when:

- the user does not know enough to choose a substantive intervention
- "learn first" may be a serious strategy
- a proposed option should be tested before full commitment
- scouting raises transfer uncertainty
- a pilot or experiment needs an explicit learning question and decision rule

This file decides whether learning belongs in the option set.

The shared uncertainty method designs the learning move.

## Call `../shared/risk-opportunity-appraisal-guidance.md` when:

- options interact materially
- a package or pathway should be compared with isolated interventions
- an option could create path dependence or lock-in
- preserving future options matters
- the change is sufficiently structural that the choice architecture itself should reflect system effects

Do not perform the full appraisal inside option generation.

## Call `../shared/strategic-triangle-guidance.md` when:

- the feasible range depends on uncertain operational capacity
- an option assumes authority, funding or cooperation that may not exist
- a strategically interesting option requires a different authorising environment
- public purpose, capacity and required support appear to be pulling the design in different directions

Use the triangle to expose the constraint or redesign need.

Return here to construct the option.

## Problem

`problem-definition-guidance.md` owns the working problem.

This file may reveal that the frame needs revision.

It should not silently rewrite the problem without making that iteration visible.

`stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md` owns actor interests, beliefs, power and relationships.

This method uses those findings only to improve option design.

## Evidence

`evidence-guidance.md` owns what is known and what needs investigation.

Options can generate new evidence questions.

Do not answer them by assertion here.

## Criteria

`criteria-guidance.md` owns what matters and what counts as better.

Basic feasibility can narrow the option space.

Formal evaluation against criteria happens later.

## Outcomes

`outcomes-guidance.md` owns what each strategy is expected to cause.

Do not build desired outcomes into option labels.

## Trade-offs

`trade-offs-guidance.md` owns the exchange between projected outcomes.

This method should create alternatives worth comparing, not decide the exchange.

## Decision

`decision-guidance.md` owns the recommendation.

Do not use option construction to steer the user covertly towards one preferred answer.

## Story

`storytelling-guidance.md` owns how the final serious alternatives are communicated.

The final story need not describe the full exploratory option set.

## Evaluation

`agent-evaluation-guidance.md` owns evaluation of the AI agent.

Do not treat real-user feedback on option quality as automatic evidence that the method itself needs changing without evaluation.

# One-line reference

> Good option construction treats policies as hypotheses rather than discovered answers, identifies what the relevant actor can actually change, turns feasible manipulations of important policy variables into coherent strategies, tests them against the real operating context, and narrows only after a genuine choice set has been created.
