# Risk-Opportunity Appraisal - Shared Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method deliberately combines 4 sources that solve different parts of the appraisal problem. **[S]** Simon Sharpe, Jean-Francois Mercure, Jorge Vinuales, Matthew Ives, Michael Grubb, Hector Pollitt, Florian Knobloch and Femke J. M. M. Nijsse, *Deciding how to decide: Risk-opportunity analysis as a generalisation of cost-benefit analysis* (2021) - used as the main appraisal spine, especially its tests of marginality, heterogeneity and uncertainty, and its risk-opportunity analysis for situations of non-marginal change. **[F]** Bent Flyvbjerg and Dirk W. Bester, *The Cost-Benefit Fallacy: Why Cost-Benefit Analysis Is Broken and How to Fix It* (2021) - used to challenge the accuracy and incentives behind ex-ante cost and benefit forecasts, and to add the outside view, reference-class forecasting and independent scrutiny. **[P]** Silvio O. Funtowicz and Jerome R. Ravetz, *The Worth of a Songbird: Ecological Economics as a Post-Normal Science* (1994) - used to keep uncertainty, value commitments, quality, burden of proof and plurality of legitimate perspectives visible when appraisal cannot be reduced to one precise metric. **[K]** Rainer Kattel, Mariana Mazzucato, Josh Ryan-Collins and Simon Sharpe, *The Economics of Change: Policy Appraisal for Missions, Market Shaping and Public Purpose* (2018) - used for dynamic efficiency, path dependence, emergence, portfolio effects, milestones, spillovers and appraisal of purposeful change through time. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user decide *what kind of appraisal fits the decision, how to compare risks and opportunities without forcing every outcome into one number, how to challenge forecast bias and false precision, and how to preserve accountable judgment when important values remain contested*. This is a shared method. It does not replace Bardach's criteria, outcomes, trade-offs or decision steps.

## How to use this document

When a user needs to appraise or compare serious policy options:

1. **Name the decision and the intended change.**
2. **Decide how to decide.** Test whether the situation is reasonably marginal, homogeneous and probabilistically knowable.
3. **Choose the appraisal posture.** Conventional cost-effectiveness or cost-benefit reasoning may be useful in some cases. Broader risk-opportunity reasoning is needed where one or more assumptions break down materially.
4. **State the public purpose or desired direction.**
5. **Set useful system boundaries.**
6. **Identify interactions, feedbacks, path dependence and possible lock-in where they matter.**
7. **Assess significant risks and opportunities across the dimensions that matter.**
8. **Quantify where the evidence supports it. Keep important non-quantifiable outcomes visible.**
9. **Challenge forecasts.** Use the outside view and look for optimism, strategic incentives and weak reference classes.
10. **Check the quality of uncertainty claims.** Do not allow precise-looking numbers to carry more certainty than the underlying knowledge.
11. **Ask whose knowledge and values are missing where uncertainty and contestation are high.**
12. **Compare individual options and combinations where interactions matter.**
13. **Leave irreducible value judgments visible for accountable decision makers.**
14. **Record why the choice was made and what evidence would justify revisiting it.**

The method should help the user choose an appraisal approach that fits the decision rather than applying the same analytical machinery to every problem.

# Part 1 - Decide how to decide

## 1. Start with the decision, not the appraisal tool [S]

Do not begin with:

> Should we run a CBA?

Begin with:

> **What decision are we trying to inform, and what kind of change could it cause?**

Name:

- the policy choice
- the relevant alternatives
- the intended outcome
- the scale
- the time horizon
- whether the intervention is expected to leave the surrounding system broadly unchanged or alter it materially

Appraisal is a means of informing the decision.

Do not choose the method before understanding the decision situation.

## 2. Test marginality [S][K]

Sharpe et al. argue that conventional social CBA and social cost-effectiveness analysis are most at home where the intervention is **marginal**.

Ask:

- Is the policy expected to leave the wider economic or institutional setting broadly unchanged?
- Are prices, relationships, technologies and market structures likely to remain sufficiently stable?
- Or is the point of the policy to alter those conditions?

Examples of potentially non-marginal change include:

- creating or restructuring a market
- changing a technological pathway
- large institutional reform
- major regulatory change
- system-wide service reform
- interventions intended to create tipping effects or new feedbacks
- policies whose benefits depend on widespread adoption or interaction with other measures

If the intervention is deliberately trying to change the underlying system, do not treat "all else equal" as a neutral assumption.

## 3. Test heterogeneity [S][K]

Conventional aggregation is easier where differences among actors and outcomes do not materially alter the policy judgment.

Ask:

- Do different groups experience materially different benefits, costs or risks?
- Do actors have different roles, incentives or capacities?
- Does distribution matter to the decision?
- Could one aggregate figure conceal who wins and who loses?
- Are several outcome dimensions important for different reasons?

If heterogeneity is material, preserve it.

Do not collapse a distributional or political question into the experience of an imaginary average person.

## 4. Test uncertainty [S][P][K]

Sharpe et al. distinguish situations where important outcomes and probabilities can be estimated with useful confidence from those involving **fundamental uncertainty**.

Funtowicz and Ravetz deepen this by distinguishing several kinds of uncertainty rather than treating them all as one probability range.

Ask:

- Are the main outcomes known well enough to estimate?
- Are relevant probabilities meaningful?
- Is uncertainty mainly statistical variation?
- Are the models themselves uncertain?
- Could new technologies, behaviours, institutions or interactions create outcomes outside the current model?
- Are we close to the border of ignorance, where even the relevant state space is unclear?

Do not put a probability on something merely because a decision tool expects one.

## 5. Choose the appraisal posture [S]

Use a **conventional appraisal posture** where the assumptions of marginality, limited heterogeneity and quantifiable uncertainty are reasonable enough that they will not distort the conclusion.

This may include:

- cost-effectiveness analysis
- social cost-benefit analysis
- sensitivity analysis
- switching values
- standard option comparison

Use a **risk-opportunity posture** where one or more assumptions fail materially.

This may include:

- multi-dimensional outcome comparison
- dynamic and system reasoning
- risks and opportunities rather than one expected value
- interacting options and policy packages
- explicit treatment of fundamental uncertainty
- qualitative judgment where aggregation would mislead

The 2 postures are not enemies.

Sharpe et al. present CBA as a specific case within a broader risk-opportunity framework when its assumptions are suitable.

## 6. Do not use "transformational" as a free pass [S]

A policy should not escape disciplined appraisal because its advocates describe it as bold, strategic or transformative.

If conventional appraisal is a poor fit, the response is **different analytical discipline**, not no discipline.

Ask:

- What assumptions make conventional appraisal weak here?
- What additional analysis will replace them?
- How will risks, opportunities, uncertainty and value judgments be made inspectable?

Do not use complexity as a reason to stop analysing.

# Part 2 - State the purpose and direction

## 7. Make the desired direction explicit [S][K]

Where policy aims to change a system, appraisal needs to ask more than:

> Does this produce positive net benefit at a point in time?

Ask:

> **What direction of change are we trying to create?**

Examples might concern:

- lower-carbon production
- greater access
- prevention rather than crisis response
- more resilient infrastructure
- a different technological pathway
- a less concentrated market
- a new service model

The direction is a value choice.

Do not smuggle it into the appraisal as though it were an empirical fact.

## 8. Distinguish allocative efficiency from dynamic effectiveness [S][K]

A static or allocative question asks:

> **How should existing resources be allocated most efficiently under broadly stable conditions?**

A dynamic question asks:

> **Which action is most likely to move the system in the desired direction over time?**

Kattel et al. describe this second concern as **dynamic efficiency**, linked to innovation, investment, structural change and the creation of new capabilities or resources.

Sharpe et al. use the closely related framing of **dynamic effectiveness**.

Do not assume the option with the best static ratio is also the option most likely to create the desired long-run change.

## 9. Keep public purpose visible [K][P]

Appraisal needs a stated account of what the policy is trying to achieve for the public.

Ask:

- What public purpose is being pursued?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs, risk or disruption?
- Which values cannot be fully represented through willingness to pay?
- Are some affected interests poorly represented in the formal decision process?

Do not equate market value, monetised benefit or political support with the whole of public value.

Call the strategic-alignment or criteria method if the public-purpose claim itself needs deeper work.

# Part 3 - Understand the system enough to appraise the change

## 10. Set the system boundary deliberately [S]

Risk-opportunity analysis begins by deciding what sits inside the relevant system.

Include enough to understand:

- the main actors
- institutions
- important technologies
- rules
- markets or services
- other policies
- significant dependencies
- material interactions with the option

Exclude material that does not bear on the decision.

Do not treat the boundary as objectively given.

State what is included and what is left outside where that choice could affect the conclusion.

## 11. Identify reinforcing and balancing feedbacks [S][K]

Ask:

- What could reinforce the desired change?
- What could counteract it?
- What becomes easier if initial progress occurs?
- What becomes harder?
- Could uptake reduce costs and create further uptake?
- Could capacity limits choke off progress?
- Could opposition increase as the policy grows?

Feedbacks matter because cause and effect may not remain proportionate over time.

Do not assume the effect of doubling an intervention is simply twice the effect of the original intervention.

## 12. Look for path dependence and lock-in [K]

Past choices can shape future possibilities.

Ask:

- Which existing infrastructure, rules, habits or investments lock the system into its current path?
- Does the option deepen that lock-in?
- Does it create an alternative path?
- Does it make future action easier or harder?
- Does acting now close off useful future options?

Appraise **what the option leaves possible later**, not only its immediate outcome.

## 13. Look for non-linearity and sensitive points [K][S]

In complex systems:

- large interventions can sometimes have little effect
- small interventions can sometimes create large effects

Ask:

- Is there a threshold, bottleneck or sensitive intervention point?
- Does the option need to reach a minimum scale before feedbacks change?
- Could several smaller measures together cross a threshold?
- Is the proposed intervention below the level required to alter system behaviour?

Do not assume proportional cause and effect where there is evidence of threshold behaviour.

## 14. Look at interactions among options [S][K]

Conventional appraisal often considers each option separately.

Where effects interact, compare:

- individual measures
- packages
- portfolios
- sequencing

Ask:

- Do 2 options reinforce each other?
- Does one option make another unnecessary?
- Does one option undermine another?
- Is a weak option individually useful as part of a stronger package?

Do not reject or select components solely on isolated scores where system effects are material.

## 15. Keep the system work proportionate

The agent is not being asked to build a complete system-dynamics model for every policy decision.

Map only the interactions capable of changing:

- expected outcomes
- risk
- opportunity
- feasibility
- the choice among options

If the system model does not affect the decision, simplify it.

# Part 4 - Assess risks and opportunities

## 16. Use multiple dimensions where one metric would hide the decision [S][P]

Relevant dimensions might include:

- financial cost
- public outcome
- health
- access
- safety
- jobs
- distribution
- rights
- environmental effects
- resilience
- capability
- technological change
- legitimacy
- another case-specific criterion

Use the criteria method to decide which dimensions matter.

Do not create dimensions simply because they appear in this list.

## 17. For each significant dimension, assess direction [S][K]

Ask:

> **Does the option move this outcome in a positive, negative or unclear direction?**

For dynamic problems, also ask:

> **Does it reinforce movement in the desired system direction?**

Direction can sometimes be known more confidently than exact magnitude.

Do not let inability to calculate a precise effect prevent a useful directional judgment where the evidence supports one.

## 18. Assess magnitude where possible [S]

Estimate:

- likely scale of effect
- plausible range
- affected population
- relevant time horizon

Use numbers where the evidence warrants them.

Use calibrated qualitative descriptions where it does not.

Do not replace a material unknown with an arbitrary number.

## 19. Assess pace [S][K]

For processes of change, ask:

- How quickly might the effect appear?
- Is there an early lag?
- Could the pace accelerate?
- Could it stall?
- Does timing itself affect whether the policy succeeds?

2 options with similar eventual outcomes may have very different value if one moves too slowly to meet the policy objective.

## 20. Assess accumulation [S]

Ask how risk or opportunity changes through time.

Examples:

- a small annual benefit may compound
- an early lock-in may become increasingly costly
- delayed action may close future options
- a temporary implementation burden may fall sharply after learning
- systemic risk may build before it becomes visible

Do not appraise each year as though it were independent of the last.

## 21. Assess confidence and uncertainty [S][P]

For every significant risk or opportunity, state how well it is understood.

A useful description can distinguish:

- relatively well evidenced
- plausible but uncertain
- highly uncertain
- dependent on a weak model
- difficult to quantify
- unknown but potentially material

Do not make the verbal confidence stronger than the evidence.

## 22. Include plausible upside and downside [S]

Where uncertainty matters, do not rely on one central forecast.

Consider:

- likely outcome
- plausible better outcome
- plausible worse outcome

The purpose is not to invent dramatic scenarios.

It is to expose asymmetric possibilities that an expected value could hide.

## 23. Include systemic risk [S]

Systemic risk concerns harm that arises through interactions or system change rather than one direct policy effect.

Ask:

- Could the option create instability elsewhere?
- Could several small harms reinforce one another?
- Could failure spread through dependencies?
- Could the policy lock the system into a costly path?
- Could important capacity be crowded out?

Keep systemic risks visible even where their probability cannot be estimated precisely.

## 24. Include systemic opportunity [S][K]

Do the same for upside.

Ask:

- Could the option create capability useful elsewhere?
- Could it trigger cost reductions, learning or innovation?
- Could it open further policy choices?
- Could it create beneficial spillovers?
- Could it make complementary investments more attractive?
- Could it help move several sectors or services in the same direction?

Do not count every speculative upside.

State the mechanism and evidentiary basis.

## 25. Keep distribution visible [S][P][K]

An aggregate improvement may contain large differences among groups.

Ask:

- Who gains?
- Who loses?
- Who bears transition cost?
- Who receives long-run benefits?
- Who carries the downside if the intervention fails?
- Are those groups the same people?

Do not average away distribution where it is part of the public judgment.

# Part 5 - Challenge the forecasts

## 26. Assume forecasts may contain systematic bias [F]

Flyvbjerg and Bester's public-investment dataset shows a recurring pattern of:

- cost underestimation
- benefit overestimation

Their empirical finding is specific to the public-investment classes they study.

Do **not** generalise their exact rates to every policy domain.

Do carry forward the broader warning:

> **A forecast can be systematically biased even when it is technically sophisticated.**

## 27. Distinguish error from bias [F]

Random errors may partly cancel.

Systematic bias compounds.

Ask:

- Are estimates consistently optimistic in one direction?
- Does the organisation have a history of cost underestimation?
- Are benefits routinely forecast more confidently than they materialise?
- Is there evidence that the forecasting process itself pushes estimates in one direction?

Do not respond to systematic bias by merely adding a larger symmetric error bar.

## 28. Take the outside view [F]

For important forecast quantities, ask:

> **What happened in a relevant reference class of similar interventions?**

Possible reference classes may be defined by:

- intervention type
- scale
- delivery setting
- technology
- institutional context
- complexity
- maturity

Compare:

- forecast cost with actual cost in comparable cases
- forecast duration with actual duration
- forecast uptake with actual uptake
- forecast benefit with realised benefit

The reference class should be close enough to inform the decision.

Do not choose a class simply because it produces the desired adjustment.

## 29. Use reference-class evidence to de-bias forecasts [F]

Where a credible empirical reference class exists, use its observed distribution to challenge the inside-view estimate.

Ask:

- How exceptional would our forecast be relative to comparable outcomes?
- What specific evidence justifies believing this case will outperform the reference class?
- Has that claimed difference itself been tested?

A plan is not exceptional because the team believes it has learned from the mistakes of others.

Require evidence for the exception.

## 30. Ask what incentives surround the forecast [F]

Forecast error may be linked to:

- optimism
- overconfidence
- planning fallacy
- pressure to secure approval
- strategic misrepresentation
- weak accountability for forecast accuracy

Ask:

- Who produced the estimate?
- What happens if it is too optimistic?
- What happens if it is conservative?
- Does approval depend on crossing a threshold?
- Is the forecaster independent from the proposal owner?

Do not accuse individuals of deliberate manipulation without evidence.

Do examine whether the system rewards optimistic forecasts.

## 31. Seek independent challenge where stakes justify it [F]

Flyvbjerg and Bester argue for independent audit of major cost-benefit forecasts.

For high-stakes appraisal, ask whether important forecasts have been:

- independently reviewed
- compared with outside evidence
- challenged by people not invested in the proposal

The level of assurance should match the consequence of being wrong.

Do not impose costly external review on routine low-stakes decisions by default.

# Part 6 - Manage uncertainty and value commitments honestly

## 32. Distinguish precision, accuracy and deeper uncertainty [P]

Funtowicz and Ravetz distinguish:

- **spread / precision** - how tightly values can be estimated
- **assessment / accuracy** - how close the estimate is likely to be to the thing being measured
- **pedigree / deeper uncertainty** - how strong the underlying methods, assumptions and models are

An estimate can be precise and still be wrong.

A model can produce a narrow confidence interval while resting on weak assumptions.

Ask:

> **What kind of uncertainty does this number actually represent?**

Do not allow a narrow statistical range to imply confidence in the model itself.

## 33. Reject meaningless precision [P]

Funtowicz and Ravetz criticise calculations where highly precise numbers sit on top of much larger unquantified assumptions.

Ask:

- Does the number have more decimal places than the evidence justifies?
- Are small precise inputs being overwhelmed by one large uncertain assumption?
- Is a central estimate hiding a range that crosses materially different policy outcomes?
- Are "hunches" or ad hoc adjustments buried inside a technical model?

Use ballpark ranges where that better reflects the knowledge.

Do not manufacture confidence through formatting.

## 34. Recommendations cannot be more certain than their foundations [P]

If the evidence is tentative, the policy conclusion cannot become categorical merely because the analysis has reached its final section.

Check:

> **Has certainty increased between the evidence and the recommendation without a reason?**

If so, reduce the certainty of the conclusion or do more analysis.

## 35. Separate factual claims from value commitments [P]

Appraisal contains values.

They may appear in:

- which outcomes are included
- whose interests count
- which time horizon is chosen
- the discounting approach
- what burden of proof is required
- what is monetised
- what is treated as a constraint
- which risks are tolerated

Make these visible.

Do not present a contested normative choice as though "the model" made it.

## 36. Do not force every value into one numeraire [P][S]

Funtowicz and Ravetz challenge the assumption that all values must be translated into one common monetary language.

Sharpe et al. similarly preserve multiple dimensions in risk-opportunity analysis.

Ask:

> **Does conversion into one metric improve comparison, or hide how the decision values fundamentally different things?**

If conversion is defensible and useful, use it.

If it conceals the normative choice, keep the outcomes separate.

## 37. Make burden of proof explicit where it matters [P]

Different decision rules place different burdens on different errors.

Ask:

- What happens if we act and the concern was overstated?
- What happens if we do not act and the concern was real?
- Which error are we currently demanding stronger evidence to avoid?
- Why is that allocation of burden justified?

Do not invoke a precautionary principle as an automatic ban on action.

Funtowicz and Ravetz explicitly warn against a form of precaution that would prohibit all innovation.

## 38. Include perspectives that can improve the quality of the appraisal [P]

Where uncertainty and value disagreement are material, ask:

- Who has relevant lived or operational knowledge?
- Which affected group sees consequences the technical analysis may miss?
- Which expert discipline is absent?
- Which interests are powerful enough to shape the analysis?
- Which less powerful interests may be excluded?

This is a quality check, not a replacement for stakeholder analysis.

Use `02-stakeholders` for the full actor map.

# Part 7 - Appraise change through time

## 39. Consider intermediate milestones for long-running change [K]

For policies whose effects unfold over years, define intermediate signs that tell us whether the system is moving in the intended direction.

Good milestones should help answer:

- Are we making progress?
- Is the mechanism behaving as expected?
- Is the direction still right?
- Do we need to adapt?
- Has new evidence weakened the original case?

Do not require a long-term policy to wait until its final outcome before becoming evaluable.

## 40. Look for spillovers [K]

A policy can produce effects beyond its headline objective.

Possible positive spillovers include:

- new capability
- technology
- knowledge
- skills
- supplier development
- new infrastructure
- useful institutional relationships

Possible negative spillovers should be treated just as seriously.

Do not count an appealing spillover without a plausible mechanism.

## 41. Appraise portfolios where the policy works through several actions [K][S]

A strategic programme may depend on a portfolio rather than one intervention.

Ask:

- Do the components cover different mechanisms?
- Are they complementary?
- Is there unnecessary duplication?
- Does the portfolio spread learning and risk?
- Could weak components be stopped without collapsing the whole strategy?
- Are interactions being monitored?

Do not demand that every individual project justify the entire strategic objective by itself.

## 42. Value adaptability under high uncertainty [K]

Kattel et al. argue that where the future path cannot be optimised in advance, policy should create room to adjust as uncertainty resolves.

Ask:

- Does this option preserve future choices?
- Can it be revised?
- Can weak elements be stopped?
- Can resources move toward stronger elements?
- Does it create irreversible lock-in before the evidence warrants it?

Do not treat flexibility as costless.

But give it weight where future knowledge is likely to change the decision.

## 43. Connect appraisal to later learning [K]

For long-running or non-marginal policy, the ex-ante decision should identify:

- milestones
- important indicators
- review points
- conditions for expansion
- conditions for adaptation
- conditions for stopping

Call the uncertainty-and-learning method for detailed learning design.

Do not make an ex-ante appraisal pretend to settle a policy whose important uncertainties will only resolve during implementation.

# Part 8 - Compare and preserve accountable judgment

## 44. Compare both options and packages [S][K]

The comparison should reflect the real choice.

Possible comparison units:

- one intervention versus another
- intervention versus business-as-usual
- package A versus package B
- sequence A versus sequence B
- staged commitment versus full commitment

Do not force all analysis into individual option rows where the real decision concerns a portfolio or pathway.

## 45. Use common metrics where they are defensible [S]

A shared metric can be useful for outcomes that are genuinely comparable.

Examples:

- cost per additional case resolved
- cost per tonne reduced
- time saved
- lives or incidents affected

Use conventional appraisal tools where they clarify the decision.

Do not discard useful arithmetic merely because the wider method is dynamic.

## 46. Keep irreducibly different dimensions separate [S][P]

If important outcomes cannot be combined without embedding a contested value judgment, do not force a total score.

Instead make the choice visible:

> **Option A performs better on X and Y, but worse on Z. The decision turns on how much weight is given to Z relative to the gains in X and Y.**

This is analytically honest.

Use `07-trade-offs` to deepen the exchange.

## 47. Do not hide judgment inside weights [S][P]

If numerical weights are used, state:

- who chose them
- why
- what values they express
- how sensitive the ranking is to them

If modest changes in weights reverse the decision, say so.

Do not label the final weighted total as objective.

## 48. Leave accountable normative judgment with accountable people [S]

Sharpe et al. make the qualitative comparison of diverse risks and opportunities part of the decision maker's job where no legitimate common metric exists.

The analyst should:

- make evidence inspectable
- show risk and opportunity
- show uncertainty
- identify value judgments
- expose the consequences of different choices

The analyst should not use technical machinery to remove political or ethical responsibility from the decision.

## 49. Record the reasoning [S][K][F]

A decision record should preserve:

- the options considered
- appraisal posture used
- important dimensions
- key forecasts
- reference classes where used
- major assumptions
- material uncertainties
- significant risks and opportunities
- value judgments
- why the chosen option was preferred
- what could justify revisiting it

This matters both for accountability and for later learning.

## 50. Keep the decision open to later evidence where appropriate [K]

A decision record should identify review conditions where the case depends on uncertainty that will change over time.

Examples:

- costs exceed a threshold
- uptake remains below a milestone
- a key system feedback does not appear
- a serious downside emerges
- a new alternative becomes viable

Do not make every decision provisional forever.

Use review conditions where later evidence can genuinely change the case.

# Appraisal scaffold

Work through these in order where useful.

## A. Decide how to decide

1. **Decision:** What choice is being informed?
2. **Change:** Is it mainly marginal or non-marginal?
3. **Heterogeneity:** Do differences among actors or outcome dimensions materially matter?
4. **Uncertainty:** Are the important outcomes and probabilities sufficiently knowable?
5. **Posture:** Conventional appraisal / risk-opportunity / mixed.

## B. State purpose

6. **Public purpose:** What worthwhile result is being pursued?
7. **Direction:** What change in the system is desired?
8. **Time horizon:** When should meaningful change occur?

## C. Understand the system

9. **Boundary:** What actors, institutions, rules, technologies and related policies matter?
10. **Feedbacks:** What reinforces or balances change?
11. **Path dependence:** What is locked in?
12. **Sensitive points:** Are there thresholds or non-linear effects?
13. **Interactions:** Which options or policies affect one another?

## D. Assess the option

14. **Dimensions:** Which outcomes matter?
15. **Direction:** Positive / negative / unclear?
16. **Magnitude:** Expected scale or range?
17. **Pace:** How quickly?
18. **Accumulation:** How does the effect build?
19. **Likely outcome:** What is the central current view?
20. **Upside:** What plausible opportunity matters?
21. **Downside:** What plausible risk matters?
22. **Systemic effects:** Could interactions create wider harm or opportunity?
23. **Distribution:** Who gains and loses?
24. **Confidence:** How strong is the underlying knowledge?

## E. Challenge the analysis

25. **Outside view:** What happened in relevant comparable cases?
26. **Bias:** Are optimism, planning fallacy or strategic incentives likely?
27. **Precision:** Are the numbers more exact than the knowledge?
28. **Model uncertainty:** Could the model itself be wrong?
29. **Values:** Which normative choices are embedded?
30. **Burden of proof:** Which type of error is receiving priority?
31. **Missing perspectives:** Who could expose a material blind spot?

## F. Compare and decide

32. **Option/package comparison:** What are the serious alternatives?
33. **Commensurable outcomes:** What can defensibly be compared on a common scale?
34. **Non-commensurable outcomes:** What must remain separate?
35. **Central judgment:** Which risk-opportunity exchange actually decides the choice?
36. **Milestones:** What would show progress?
37. **Review condition:** What later evidence could change the decision?
38. **Decision record:** Why was this choice made?

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - CBA everywhere

**Failure:** the same appraisal method is used regardless of whether the policy changes underlying relationships or creates fundamental uncertainty.

**Challenge:** test marginality, heterogeneity and uncertainty first.

## Pitfall B - "Transformational" means exempt from appraisal

**Failure:** advocates escape scrutiny by describing a proposal as structural or strategic.

**Challenge:** replace inappropriate appraisal with proportionate risk-opportunity analysis.

## Pitfall C - The average actor erases real differences

**Failure:** aggregate welfare hides materially different effects across groups.

**Challenge:** preserve heterogeneity and distribution where they matter.

## Pitfall D - Unknown probabilities become invented probabilities

**Failure:** fundamental uncertainty is squeezed into a probability distribution solely to enable expected-value calculation.

**Challenge:** keep significant unknowns visible and compare plausible risks and opportunities directly.

## Pitfall E - One metric decides a multi-value problem

**Failure:** rights, distribution, resilience or other distinct values disappear into one composite figure.

**Challenge:** keep dimensions separate where conversion would hide the value judgment.

## Pitfall F - Complexity as decoration

**Failure:** the analysis mentions feedbacks, emergence and path dependence but they change nothing in the comparison.

**Challenge:** include only system features capable of changing the decision.

## Pitfall G - Options appraised in isolation despite interaction

**Failure:** individually weak or strong scores ignore complementary or conflicting effects.

**Challenge:** appraise packages and sequences where interaction matters.

## Pitfall H - Static comparison of a dynamic problem

**Failure:** one before-and-after snapshot ignores direction, pace, feedback and lock-in.

**Challenge:** appraise the process of change.

## Pitfall I - Pilot performance becomes system performance

**Failure:** protected small-scale conditions are assumed to survive wider adoption.

**Challenge:** examine scale, feedbacks, capacity and transfer conditions.

## Pitfall J - Inside-view optimism

**Failure:** the forecast relies mainly on the plan's own assumptions.

**Challenge:** ask what happened in comparable cases.

## Pitfall K - Reference class chosen for convenience

**Failure:** comparison cases are selected because they support the desired estimate.

**Challenge:** justify why the class is genuinely comparable.

## Pitfall L - Forecast bias treated as random error

**Failure:** an optimism problem is handled by adding a symmetric uncertainty interval.

**Challenge:** examine the direction of past forecast error and adjust using outside evidence where possible.

## Pitfall M - False precision

**Failure:** precise outputs conceal broad model or assumption uncertainty.

**Challenge:** match numerical precision to the quality of the underlying knowledge.

## Pitfall N - Certainty appears at the recommendation stage

**Failure:** tentative evidence becomes a firm conclusion without additional support.

**Challenge:** recommendations cannot be more certain than their foundations.

## Pitfall O - Hidden value commitments

**Failure:** discounting, monetisation, burdens of proof or weighting choices appear technically neutral.

**Challenge:** state the normative choice.

## Pitfall P - Precaution means never acting

**Failure:** deep uncertainty is turned into a blanket argument against action.

**Challenge:** compare the risks of action and inaction and state the burden of proof.

## Pitfall Q - Public participation replaces analysis

**Failure:** every perspective is treated as equally strong evidence about every factual claim.

**Challenge:** include relevant knowledge and values while preserving differences in evidentiary competence.

## Pitfall R - Appraisal ends at approval

**Failure:** the decision has no milestones or route for learning when important uncertainty remains.

**Challenge:** connect the choice to later evidence and review.

## Pitfall S - Optimality is invented where the path is open-ended

**Failure:** one future pathway is presented as mathematically best despite major novelty and system change.

**Challenge:** compare plausible pathways and value adaptability where appropriate.

## Pitfall T - The shared method swallows the Bardach sequence

**Failure:** risk-opportunity appraisal starts generating options, inventing criteria, forecasting outcomes and making the decision itself.

**Challenge:** use this method to choose and discipline the appraisal posture, then call the step that owns the detailed analytical job.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The decision and intended type of change are explicit.
- Marginality, heterogeneity and uncertainty have been considered before choosing the appraisal approach.
- Conventional appraisal has not been rejected merely because the policy is ambitious.
- Non-marginal change has not been forced into a static "all else equal" analysis where that would distort the result.
- The desired policy direction or public purpose is visible.
- Important interacting effects are considered where they can change the choice.
- Significant risks and opportunities are assessed across the dimensions that matter.
- Important non-quantifiable outcomes have not disappeared merely because they lack a price.
- Material distributional differences remain visible.
- Forecasts central to the choice have been challenged for optimism or bias where relevant.
- Numerical precision does not exceed the quality of the underlying evidence and models.
- The recommendation is no more certain than its foundations.
- Embedded value judgments are visible.
- Irreducible normative judgment has not been hidden inside a composite score.
- The analysis identifies what later evidence should cause review where the case depends on uncertainty.

## Should pass

- A useful system boundary is explicit.
- Reinforcing and balancing feedbacks have been considered.
- Path dependence and lock-in have been considered for long-lived or structural choices.
- Pace and accumulation of change have been considered, not only eventual magnitude.
- Plausible upside and downside are visible.
- Systemic risks and opportunities are considered where relevant.
- Options are compared in combination where interaction matters.
- A credible reference class has been used for important forecast quantities where one exists.
- Forecast incentives and independent challenge have been considered for high-stakes cases.
- Burden of proof has been examined where false-positive and false-negative harms differ materially.
- Missing perspectives have been considered where uncertainty and values are contested.
- Milestones or review points are defined for long-running change.
- The reasoning behind the decision can be reconstructed later.

## Process reminder

- The first appraisal question is **how should this decision be appraised?**
- CBA and cost-effectiveness remain useful where their assumptions are reasonable.
- Risk-opportunity analysis widens the frame where change is non-marginal, actors or outcomes are heterogeneous, or uncertainty cannot be reduced to reliable probabilities.
- Quantification is useful when it is credible.
- Precision is harmful when it conceals weak knowledge.
- System change is judged through direction, interaction and change through time as well as static outcomes.
- Accountable judgment remains necessary where important values cannot defensibly be converted into one measure.

# Canonical boundaries across MDEE.MD

This is a **shared appraisal method**.

It should inform several Bardach steps without replacing them.

## `01-problem`

Use this method only to notice when the problem is about:

- marginal improvement
- structural change
- a contested system direction

Do not begin appraisal before the problem is sufficiently clear.

## `02-stakeholders`

The stakeholder method owns:

- actors
- motivations
- beliefs
- resources and power
- relationships
- coalitions

This appraisal method may ask whose knowledge, interests or values are missing and which actors create heterogeneous effects.

Do not recreate the stakeholder map here.

## `03-evidence`

The evidence and uncertainty method owns:

- critical uncertainty
- evidence quality
- learning moves
- experiments

This appraisal method contributes:

- the outside view
- reference-class forecasting
- forecast-bias challenge
- false precision
- model uncertainty

Where an appraisal gap needs new learning, call `03`.

## `04-options`

The options skill owns construction of alternatives and policy packages.

This shared method can reveal that:

- interacting policies should be considered as a package
- a static option set misses a pathway
- lock-in or option preservation matters

Do not generate the full option menu here.

## `05-criteria`

The criteria skill owns the standards against which outcomes are judged.

This shared method contributes the appraisal-method gate:

- marginality
- heterogeneity
- uncertainty
- public purpose / direction
- whether one metric is appropriate

Do not define all criteria inside this method.

## `06-outcomes`

The outcomes skill owns detailed projection.

This shared method strengthens it with:

- direction
- pace
- accumulation
- feedbacks
- path dependence
- systemic effects
- outside-view forecasting
- false-precision checks

Do not duplicate the full projection scaffold here.

## `07-trade-offs`

The trade-offs skill owns the exchange between competing outcomes and values.

This shared method contributes:

- risk and opportunity
- systemic upside/downside
- multi-dimensional comparison
- warning against forced aggregation

Do not make the final trade-off judgment here.

## `08-decide`

The decision skill owns the recommendation.

This shared method contributes:

- whether the appraisal posture fit the decision
- what value judgments remain
- whether forecast bias has been challenged
- whether options remain open or create lock-in
- what review condition should be recorded

The accountable decision remains with the person or institution authorised to make it.

## `09-story`

Use only the parts needed to explain why the comparison was done this way.

Do not turn the final policy story into a lecture on appraisal theory.

## `10-evaluation`

Use this method to evaluate whether policy-appraisal behaviour in another skill was sound.

Do not use risk-opportunity analysis as the method for evaluating the AI agent itself.

# One-line reference

> Good appraisal starts by deciding how to decide: use conventional cost and benefit reasoning where marginality, limited heterogeneity and quantifiable uncertainty make it a good fit, widen to risk-opportunity analysis where those assumptions break down, challenge forecasts with outside evidence, keep deep uncertainty and contested values visible, and leave accountable judgment visible rather than hiding it inside one precise-looking number.
