# Strategic Alignment - Shared Method Guidance

> **Primary source:** John D. Donahue, *Strategic Alignment for Policy Analysis and Design*, Harvard Kennedy School Case Program, Case 2090.0 (2017). The framework has roots in Mark H. Moore's public-management work and is presented by Donahue as a way to keep attention on the fit between **public value**, **operational feasibility** and **political feasibility**. **Project role:** This note is the canonical shared method for that 3-part check across MDEE.MD. It should be called by other methods when value, capacity or support materially affects the work. It should not be copied in full into every skill. **Purpose:** Help an agent ask whether a policy proposition is worthwhile, realistically deliverable, and sufficiently supported by the people and institutions whose backing is required, then diagnose where the fit is weak and what further analysis is needed.

## How to use this document

When a live policy problem, option or recommendation needs a strategic-alignment check:

1. **Name the proposition.** What exactly is being proposed, continued, changed or stopped?
2. **Test public value.** What worthwhile public result is expected, for whom, compared with what?
3. **Test operational feasibility.** What financial, legal, technical, personnel and managerial resources are required, and are they available or realistically obtainable?
4. **Test political feasibility.** Whose support is required, what are they being asked to support, and do they believe the proposition can actually be delivered?
5. **Diagnose the misalignment.** Which dimension or relationship is weak?
6. **Ask whether the gap can realistically be changed.** Capacity and support can sometimes be built. Do not assume they will be.
7. **Treat the current fit as provisional.** Alignment can deteriorate as evidence, resources, leadership, interests or conditions change.
8. **Call deeper methods where needed.** The triangle is a cue for further analysis, not a substitute for it.

A useful output is usually a short readout, not a scorecard.

# The model

A policy is strategically aligned when 3 conditions fit together well enough.

## Public value

The proposition promises to create **net public value**.

This asks whether the policy goal and expected consequences are worthwhile.

It does not ask whether the policy is popular.

## Operational feasibility

The resources needed to make the proposition real are available or realistically obtainable.

Donahue explicitly names:

- financial resources
- legal resources
- technical resources
- personnel
- managerial resources

Operational feasibility asks whether the proposition can actually be carried out, not whether it is imaginable or technically possible in principle.

## Political feasibility

The stakeholders whose support is required:

- support the purposes of the policy
- and believe in its operational feasibility

This matters because support for an aspiration may disappear when the cost, delivery burden, timetable or implementation risk becomes clear.

Political feasibility is therefore more than public popularity or formal approval.

# Three standing caveats

Donahue gives 3 caveats that should govern every use of the framework.

## 1. Perfect alignment is rare

Almost every policy can be disputed.

People may disagree about:

- whether the goal is valuable
- whether the proposed means will cause the intended result
- whether the delivery model will work
- whether the policy deserves resources compared with another priority

The practical ambition is usually **good enough alignment**, not perfect agreement.

Do not fail a serious policy because somebody could object to it.

Ask whether the remaining disagreement or weakness is material enough to threaten the proposition.

## 2. Alignment is usually constructed

Strategic alignment is often created through:

- policy design
- evidence
- capability building
- political work
- negotiation
- sequencing
- institutional change

Do not treat the triangle as a passive diagnostic that merely reports what exists.

A weak dimension may be changeable.

The relevant question is:

> **What would have to change for this proposition to become viable?**

## 3. Alignment is unstable

The fit between purpose, capacity and support can break down.

A policy may remain viable for years, or lose alignment quickly.

Assume the current position is fragile enough to deserve review when important conditions change.

Do not present an aligned policy as permanently solved.

# Core rules

## 1. Apply the framework to a specific proposition

Weak:

> Improve social mobility.

Useful:

> Expand free childcare to children aged 1 to 2 through local-authority commissioned provision from 2027.

A broad goal can have high public value while saying almost nothing about operational or political feasibility.

Name:

- what action is proposed
- who is expected to act
- who is affected
- what public result is sought
- the relevant scale and timing where known

If the proposition is too vague to test, clarify it first.

## 2. Keep the 3 dimensions separate

Do not infer one dimension from another.

A policy can be:

- popular but low value
- valuable but undeliverable
- deliverable but unsupported
- supported in principle but unsupported once delivery costs become visible

Do not write:

> "People support it, so it creates public value."

or:

> "The technology exists, so it is operationally feasible."

or:

> "The minister approved it, so political support is secure."

Each claim needs its own basis.

## 3. Treat public value as contestable

Donahue warns that political support is an imperfect guide to value.

A policy can be supported by people with political resources while imposing costs on:

- groups with little influence
- people outside the immediate political constituency
- future generations

Some rights may also remain important even where majority opinion points elsewhere.

Ask:

- What public outcome is being claimed?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs, harms or risks?
- What alternative use of resources is being displaced?
- Which affected interests may be poorly represented in the political-support picture?
- What value judgment is built into saying this outcome is worth pursuing?

Do not treat "public value" as a synonym for "the government's objective".

## 4. Distinguish value from causal confidence

Someone may agree that an outcome would be highly valuable while rejecting the proposed route to it.

Example:

> Reducing malaria is valuable.

does not establish:

> This particular malaria intervention will produce the intended reduction.

The value judgment and causal claim are separate.

Where the causal pathway is weak, call the evidence or outcomes method.

Do not make the triangle carry causal analysis it cannot provide.

## 5. Compare value with competing priorities

Donahue notes that people may agree a goal is worthwhile but still prefer another use of scarce resources.

Ask:

> **Valuable compared with what else we could do?**

This is especially important where money, management attention, legislative time or political capital is constrained.

A proposition can have positive value and still be a weak priority.

## 6. Test operational feasibility against the resources actually required

Donahue names financial, legal, technical, personnel and managerial resources.

For the proposition at hand, ask:

### Financial

- What funding is needed?
- For how long?
- Is it already available?
- Is future funding being assumed?

### Legal

- Is there authority to act?
- Are new powers, regulations or permissions required?
- Is legality known or still uncertain?

### Technical

- What systems, infrastructure, data or specialist capability are required?
- Do they exist at the needed level?

### Personnel

- Who must do the work?
- Are enough people available?
- Do they have the required skills and incentives?

### Managerial

- Who coordinates the work?
- Are responsibilities and decision rights workable?
- Can the organisations involved manage the dependencies?

Do not expand this into a full implementation plan unless the case requires it.

The purpose is to identify whether capacity is a credible part of the proposition or an unsupported assumption.

## 7. Distinguish possible from realistically obtainable

Operational capacity is elastic.

Something impossible today may become possible through investment, time, learning or mobilisation.

Donahue's lunar-landing example illustrates this.

But extraordinary mobilisation cannot be treated as the ordinary assumption.

Ask:

- What capacity exists now?
- What must be built?
- How long would that take?
- What resources would be diverted from elsewhere?
- Who would have to make that investment?
- Is there credible reason to believe they will?

Do not write:

> "Capacity can be developed."

without a plausible path for developing it.

## 8. Test the proposition at the scale actually proposed

A policy may be feasible:

- in one team
- for one cohort
- during a protected pilot

and not feasible:

- nationally
- permanently
- under normal staffing
- across several organisations

Ask whether the evidence of capacity matches the intended:

- scale
- pace
- duration
- operating conditions

Do not use pilot feasibility as automatic evidence of system-wide feasibility.

## 9. Identify whose support is actually required

Political feasibility is about the actors whose support is needed for the proposition to happen.

This may include actors who can:

- authorise
- fund
- legislate
- regulate
- implement
- cooperate
- comply
- tolerate
- refrain from blocking

The full stakeholder ecosystem may be much larger.

Use the stakeholder method to map it where necessary.

For the triangle itself, identify the **minimum consequential set of actors whose support or non-opposition matters to viability**.

## 10. Do not reduce political feasibility to popularity

Public opinion can matter.

So can:

- elected decision makers
- legislatures
- finance ministries
- regulators
- courts
- delivery bodies
- professional groups
- organised interests
- partners
- other veto players

A popular policy can still be politically infeasible if actors with required authority or cooperation refuse it.

An unpopular policy may still be feasible under some institutional arrangements.

Analyse the relevant decision environment.

## 11. Check whether supporters believe the policy can be delivered

Donahue's definition of political feasibility contains an important second condition.

Required supporters must not only endorse the **purpose**.

They must also believe in the proposition's **operational feasibility**.

Ask:

- Do supporters believe the delivery model?
- Are they prepared to fund it?
- Will they still support it if the real implementation burden becomes visible?
- Is current support based on an unrealistically cheap, quick or easy version of the proposition?

Support built on implausible delivery assumptions is fragile.

## 12. Diagnose missing support rather than saying "political will"

Donahue explicitly challenges the common lament:

> We just need political will.

That phrase hides the reason support is absent.

Possible explanations include:

- disagreement about the value being pursued
- disagreement about the causal mechanism
- concern about operational feasibility
- competing priorities
- unwillingness to bear the cost
- distributional conflict
- concern about risk
- institutional interests

Ask:

> **What exactly is the actor unwilling to support, and why?**

Then use the stakeholder method if deeper analysis is needed.

## 13. Political feasibility can change, but usually not effortlessly

Support can be built.

Possible routes include:

- changing the proposition
- producing better evidence
- demonstrating feasibility
- changing costs or burdens
- negotiating
- sequencing
- coalition building
- waiting for a different political window

Do not assume communication alone will overcome a real disagreement about values, interests or costs.

## 14. Check for all 3 basic forms of misalignment

### Valuable + politically supported + operationally infeasible

The proposition is wanted and worthwhile, but cannot currently be carried out at the required level.

Ask:

> **What capacity is missing, and is building it a credible policy move in its own right?**

Possible responses:

- reduce scale
- change pace
- simplify the proposition
- build capability first
- change the implementation model
- test a narrower version
- choose another option

### Valuable + operationally feasible + politically unsupported

The proposition could work and is judged worthwhile, but essential support is missing.

Ask:

> **Why is support absent?**

Possible responses:

- investigate the disagreement
- change distribution of costs or benefits
- alter the proposition
- build a coalition
- demonstrate delivery
- sequence decisions differently
- accept that the current political setting cannot sustain it

### Operationally feasible + politically supported + low public value

The proposition can be done and people with influence support it, but its contribution to public value is weak or negative.

This is especially important for established programmes.

Possible responses:

- reassess outcomes
- compare opportunity costs
- redesign
- reduce
- stop
- redirect resources

Do not infer value from longevity.

## 15. Allow multiple weaknesses at once

The 3 canonical misalignment types are teaching devices.

A real proposition may be weak on 2 or all 3 dimensions.

Do not force it into one category.

State:

- which dimensions are weak
- which weakness is currently binding
- whether improving one dimension makes another easier or harder

## 16. Look at the relationships between the dimensions

The triangle is useful because the dimensions interact.

Examples:

- weak delivery credibility can erode political support
- political support can release money or legal authority and expand capacity
- new evidence of poor outcomes can weaken the value case and then support
- a redesign that improves feasibility may reduce public value
- a more ambitious value proposition may require capacity that cannot be built in time

Ask:

> **If we strengthen this corner, what changes elsewhere?**

Do not treat the dimensions as 3 independent checklist columns.

## 17. Use "good enough" as a disciplined judgment

Perfect alignment is unrealistic.

"Good enough" does not mean:

- vaguely positive
- 2 out of 3 corners look fine
- problems can be dealt with later

It means the remaining weaknesses are understood well enough that proceeding is a defensible judgment.

State:

- what remains weak
- why it is acceptable for now
- what assumption carries the most risk
- what should trigger review

## 18. Assume current alignment can break

Ask what could change:

### Public value

- new evidence about outcomes
- new harms
- different distributional effects
- changing opportunity costs

### Operational feasibility

- budget changes
- staffing changes
- technical failure
- supplier failure
- legal change
- organisational strain
- expansion in scale

### Political feasibility

- change in leadership
- loss of a coalition partner
- new opposition
- visible implementation costs
- changing public attitudes
- competing priorities

The point is not to create an exhaustive risk register.

Identify the few conditions capable of making the proposition materially less viable.

## 19. Use the framework differently for established and proposed policies

Donahue identifies 2 characteristic forms of self-deception.

### Established policies

People invested in an established policy may overlook evidence that it creates little value because:

- the operation functions
- institutions are built around it
- supporters are accustomed to it
- professional or organisational interests benefit from continuation

Ask:

> **If this activity did not already exist, would the evidence justify creating it now?**

### New proposals

Advocates of new ideas are more likely to be overconfident about:

- capacity
- support
- the speed at which either can be built

Ask:

> **Which delivery or support assumption is being treated as easier than the evidence warrants?**

These are standing scepticism checks.

## 20. Keep evidence, assumption and judgment distinct

For each dimension, distinguish:

- **evidence** - what is actually known
- **inference** - what the evidence suggests
- **assumption** - what the proposition currently depends on without enough evidence
- **value judgment** - what is being treated as worthwhile
- **unknown** - what has not yet been established

Do not fill a weak corner with plausible prose.

If the case cannot currently support a judgment, say so.

## 21. Do not turn the triangle into a scoring machine

Donahue explicitly says the familiar triangle and overlapping-circle graphics are mnemonics rather than analytical tools.

The same warning applies to numerical scoring.

Avoid:

| Value | Capacity | Support |
|---|---|---|
| 8/10 | 6/10 | 7/10 |

unless the user has a specific, defensible reason for quantification.

A composite alignment score is especially misleading.

One severe weakness can make the whole proposition fail.

Prefer a reasoned diagnosis.

## 22. Call the deeper method that owns the problem

Donahue's final observation is central:

> the alignment framework is not a stand-alone analytical tool.

It tells us **where deeper work is needed**.

Use:

- **problem method** when the public purpose or proposition is unclear
- **stakeholder method** when required support, interests or power are unclear
- **evidence method** when claims about value, capacity or support lack evidence
- **uncertainty and learning method** when a material assumption needs testing
- **options method** when misalignment suggests redesign or another course of action
- **criteria method** when public value needs to be specified more carefully
- **outcomes method** when the value claim depends on uncertain future effects
- **trade-offs method** when improving one dimension sacrifices another
- **decision method** when the proposition is good enough to choose but judgment remains
- **story method** when the reasoning needs to be communicated

The triangle directs attention.

It does not replace those methods.

# Strategic-alignment scaffold

Work through these only where useful.

## 1. Proposition

What exactly is being proposed, continued, changed or stopped?

## 2. Public value

- What public outcome is expected?
- For whom?
- Who bears costs, harms or risks?
- Compared with what alternative?
- What evidence supports the value claim?
- What is the strongest challenge to it?

## 3. Operational feasibility

- What financial resources are required?
- What legal authority is required?
- What technical capability is required?
- What personnel are required?
- What managerial capability is required?
- Which exist now?
- Which must be built?
- Is the proposed scale and pace realistic?

## 4. Political feasibility

- Whose support is required?
- Support for what exactly?
- Do those actors believe the purpose is worthwhile?
- Do they believe it can be delivered?
- What could they block, withhold or withdraw?
- Why might required support be absent or fragile?

## 5. Misalignment

Choose the best description:

- aligned well enough for the current stage
- valuable and supported, but capacity is weak
- valuable and deliverable, but support is weak
- deliverable and supported, but the public-value case is weak
- weak on several dimensions
- insufficient evidence to judge

## 6. Construction or repair

What could realistically change the weak dimension?

## 7. Interaction

Would repairing one dimension strengthen or weaken another?

## 8. Fragility

What evidence, event or condition could break the current alignment?

## 9. Next analytical move

Which deeper method should now be used?

→ **Strategic-alignment readout:** `[proposition]` has `[strength/weakness]` in public value, `[strength/weakness]` in operational feasibility, and `[strength/weakness]` in political feasibility. The binding misalignment is `[diagnosis]`. The most plausible route to improving it is `[move]`, but this depends on `[assumption]`. The next analytical move is `[method / question]`.

# Suggested compact readout

Use this as the default output when another skill calls the shared method.

### Public value

`[What worthwhile outcome is claimed, for whom, and the main challenge to that claim.]`

### Operational feasibility

`[What capacity matters most and whether it exists or can realistically be built.]`

### Political feasibility

`[Whose support is essential, how secure it is, and whether those actors believe the proposition is deliverable.]`

### Central misalignment

`[Where the proposition currently breaks or strains.]`

### What could change it

`[Specific redesign, evidence, capacity or support move.]`

### Next analytical move

`[Which method/question should follow.]`

This should usually be enough.

Do not produce a long framework explainer unless the user asks for one.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - The triangle graphic is treated as analysis

**Failure:** placing a proposition somewhere inside a triangle is presented as a finding.

**Challenge:** state the evidence and reasoning for each dimension.

## Pitfall B - Popularity equals public value

**Failure:** broad support is taken as proof the policy is worthwhile.

**Challenge:** examine outcomes, harms, distribution, rights and opportunity cost separately.

## Pitfall C - Government intent equals public value

**Failure:** an official objective is treated as self-justifying.

**Challenge:** ask what public result it creates and for whom.

## Pitfall D - Technical possibility equals feasibility

**Failure:** because something can be built, the organisation is assumed able to deliver it.

**Challenge:** test money, law, people, management and actual operating conditions.

## Pitfall E - Capacity will appear

**Failure:** missing capability is acknowledged but assumed to arrive after approval.

**Challenge:** identify who builds it, with what resources, over what period.

## Pitfall F - Pilot success proves capacity at scale

**Failure:** protected small-scale delivery is treated as proof of ordinary system capacity.

**Challenge:** test scale, duration and normal operating conditions.

## Pitfall G - Formal approval equals durable support

**Failure:** one decision or announcement is treated as a stable political environment.

**Challenge:** identify whose continued cooperation matters after approval.

## Pitfall H - "Lack of political will"

**Failure:** opposition is named but unexplained.

**Challenge:** diagnose the disagreement, interest, cost, risk or feasibility concern underneath it.

## Pitfall I - Stakeholder analysis collapses into political feasibility

**Failure:** every affected actor is included in the triangle itself.

**Challenge:** use the stakeholder method for the ecosystem; use this method for the support required for viability.

## Pitfall J - Public value collapses into criteria

**Failure:** the triangle tries to specify and weight every policy criterion.

**Challenge:** use the criteria method for detailed evaluative standards.

## Pitfall K - Operational feasibility becomes a full implementation plan

**Failure:** the shared check grows into pages of delivery design.

**Challenge:** identify the binding capacity assumptions, then call the appropriate implementation or options work.

## Pitfall L - Established policy gets a free pass

**Failure:** long-running, well-supported activity is presumed worthwhile.

**Challenge:** ask whether the evidence would justify creating it today.

## Pitfall M - New proposal optimism

**Failure:** advocates assume money, capability and supporters will materialise.

**Challenge:** ask which assumptions about capacity and support have an actual path behind them.

## Pitfall N - Perfect alignment becomes the standard

**Failure:** any disagreement or implementation issue is treated as fatal.

**Challenge:** ask whether the current fit is good enough for the next responsible move.

## Pitfall O - "Good enough" becomes hand-waving

**Failure:** known weaknesses are waved away without a stated judgment.

**Challenge:** name the weakness, why proceeding remains defensible, and what would trigger review.

## Pitfall P - Static diagnosis

**Failure:** the readout treats today's conditions as permanent.

**Challenge:** identify what could break the current alignment.

## Pitfall Q - The framework becomes the whole analysis

**Failure:** the agent produces 3 paragraphs and believes the policy has been analysed.

**Challenge:** identify which corner requires a deeper method and go there.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- The framework is applied to a **specific proposition**.
- Public value, operational feasibility and political feasibility are treated as **separate claims**.
- Support is not used as a proxy for public value.
- Technical possibility is not used as a proxy for operational feasibility.
- Political feasibility identifies the actors whose support is actually required.
- The analysis asks whether required supporters believe the proposition can be delivered.
- Missing political support is diagnosed rather than reduced to "political will".
- Missing capacity is not assumed to appear without a credible path.
- The relevant form or forms of misalignment are stated.
- Material evidence gaps and assumptions remain visible.
- The current fit is treated as potentially unstable.
- The framework points to deeper analysis where it cannot answer the question itself.

## Should pass

- Opportunity cost or competing public priorities have been considered where relevant.
- Groups with little political influence have not disappeared from the value analysis.
- Scale and pace have been considered in the capacity analysis.
- Established policies have been challenged for possible low-value persistence.
- New proposals have been challenged for optimism about capacity and support.
- Interactions between the 3 dimensions have been considered.
- A plausible route to creating or repairing alignment has been identified where one exists.
- A review trigger has been named when current alignment depends on a fragile condition.
- The output is proportionate to the user's task rather than framework-shaped by default.

## Process reminder

- Perfect alignment is rare.
- Alignment can be constructed.
- Capacity and support can sometimes be expanded.
- Neither should be assumed to expand easily.
- Alignment is unstable.
- Established policies and new proposals produce different self-deception risks.
- The triangle is a cue to use other methods, not a replacement for them.

# Canonical boundaries across MDEE.MD

This is a **shared method**, not another step in the Bardach sequence.

## `01-problem`

Use a **compact readout** to test whether a problem frame is stronger or weaker depending on public value, capacity and support.

Do not require the user to solve the whole triangle before the problem is defined.

Canonical questions:

- What public concern makes this problem worth attention?
- Does the framing assume delivery capacity that does not exist?
- Would another framing change whose support is required?
- Where is the central tension?

## `02-stakeholders`

The stakeholder method owns:

- actor identification
- motivations
- beliefs
- resources and power
- relationships
- coalitions and opposition

This shared method owns the narrower question:

> **Which actors' support, consent, funding, cooperation or non-opposition is necessary for this proposition to remain politically feasible?**

Do not recreate the whole stakeholder map here.

## `03-evidence`

Use this method to expose unsupported claims in any of the 3 dimensions.

The evidence skill decides how to investigate them.

Examples:

- Is the claimed public value evidenced?
- Is the capacity assessment assumption-heavy?
- Is the political-feasibility judgment based on actual stakeholder evidence?

## `04-options`

Use misalignment as a source of redesign.

Examples:

- change scale
- change implementation model
- change sequencing
- change who carries cost
- build capability first

The options skill owns generation of the alternatives.

## `05-criteria`

The criteria skill owns detailed specification of:

- effectiveness
- efficiency
- distribution
- rights
- process values
- practical constraints

Public value in this shared method is a **high-level challenge**, not a substitute for that work.

## `06-outcomes`

The outcomes skill owns projection of what the proposition is expected to cause.

This shared method can ask whether the value claim depends on a projection that is too uncertain to sustain it.

## `07-trade-offs`

The trade-offs skill owns choices between competing outcomes and values.

This shared method contributes a different tension:

> **Does improving value, capacity or support create a cost somewhere else in the triangle?**

Do not duplicate the full trade-off analysis.

## `08-decide`

Use the triangle as a final strategic check:

- worthwhile enough?
- deliverable enough?
- supported enough?
- what remains fragile?
- what condition would reopen the decision?

The decision skill remains responsible for the recommendation.

## `09-story`

Use a compact value / capacity / support readout only where it helps explain the decision.

Do not teach the framework in the final policy story unless the audience needs it.

## `10-evaluation`

Use this method only when evaluating whether another skill applied strategic alignment correctly.

Do not use the policy triangle to evaluate the AI agent itself.

# One-line reference

> Strategic alignment asks whether a specific policy proposition creates enough public value, has enough realistically obtainable capacity behind it, and has enough support from the actors whose backing is required, while recognising that the fit is usually imperfect, deliberately constructed and liable to break.
