# Uncertainty and Learning - Shared Method Guidance

> **Sources and provenance:** This method deliberately combines 3 sources that do different jobs. **[M]** Tara Dawson McGuinness and Anne-Marie Slaughter, *The New Practice of Public Problem Solving* (2019) - used for the shift from linear policy design towards people-centred, iterative public problem solving with live feedback, scouting, small-scale testing and early attention to scale. **[R]** Dani Rodrik, *The New Development Economics: We Shall Experiment, but How Shall We Learn?* (2008) - used for diagnostic reasoning under uncertainty, plural forms of evidence, internal and external validity, explicit theory, contextual policy design and the limits of treating randomised evaluation as a universal answer. **[D]** Demos Helsinki, *Experimentation Guidelines for the Latvian Public Sector* - used for the practical structure of experiments, exploratory versus verificatory purposes, hypothesis formation, learning outcomes, measurement, risk, small-scale testing, validation and decisions about what to do next. **Purpose:** Operating instructions for an agent that helps a user decide *whether they know enough to plan and act, what important uncertainty remains, what kind of evidence or test could reduce it, and how the result should change the next decision*. This is broader than an experimentation guide. Experimentation is one possible learning move.

## How to use this document

When a user is uncertain what to do next:

1. **Name the decision.** What real choice is the user trying to make?
2. **Ask whether uncertainty is actually the binding issue.** Some decisions are mainly about values, rights, interests, trade-offs or authority rather than missing knowledge.
3. **Diagnose the uncertainty.** Is it about the problem, lived experience, mechanism, constraint, option, implementation, effect, or transfer to this context?
4. **State competing explanations where useful.** What could be true, and what would we expect to observe under each explanation?
5. **Identify the critical uncertainty.** Which unresolved question is most capable of changing what the user should do?
6. **Define the learning outcome.** What should be known differently after the next piece of work?
7. **Choose the lightest credible learning move.** Existing evidence, field inquiry, observation, scouting, prototype, rapid test, A/B test, quasi-experiment, RCT or another method.
8. **Distinguish exploratory from verificatory work.**
9. **Write a hypothesis only where the uncertainty is specific enough to support one.**
10. **Define what evidence will be gathered and what different results would mean.**
11. **Run or design the learning move proportionately.**
12. **Interpret the result carefully.** Separate what was directly learned from what is being extrapolated.
13. **Decide what follows.** Scale, adapt, test again, stop, gather different evidence, or reframe.

The method should help the user move from **uncertainty to a useful next decision**, rather than from uncertainty automatically to an experiment.

## The overall model

The 3 sources answer different questions.

### McGuinness and Slaughter [M]

**Why might planning alone be insufficient?**

Their critique is of a linear model in which policy is researched, designed, agreed and implemented before the people affected can generate enough feedback to improve it. Their alternative emphasises:

- people-centred inquiry
- scouting for solutions already being tried
- starting small
- testing and revising
- live data and feedback
- planning for scale from the beginning

Use this source to decide whether the work needs a stronger learning loop.

### Rodrik [R]

**How should we reason when we do not already know the answer?**

His policy mindset starts with relative agnosticism, diagnoses the local problem, develops competing explanations, looks for evidence capable of distinguishing among them, and treats policy as contextual rather than governed by universal recipes.

Use this source to decide:

- what the uncertainty actually is
- what kind of evidence can inform it
- how strongly a finding should change our beliefs
- whether evidence transfers from one context to another

### Demos Helsinki [D]

**How do we turn a learning need into a structured test?**

Their method defines experimentation as a structured process for learning and iteration before scaling. It distinguishes exploratory and verificatory experiments and requires:

- a clear learning goal
- a hypothesis where appropriate
- evidence collection
- assessment criteria
- a bounded time period
- openness to an unwelcome result
- a decision about what follows

Use this source when active testing is the right learning move.

# Part 1 - Decide whether this is a planning problem or a learning problem

## 1. Start with the decision

Do not begin with:

> What experiment could we run?

Begin with:

> **What decision are we trying to make?**

Examples:

- whether to invest in an intervention
- whether to change a service
- whether a problem has been framed correctly
- whether to scale an existing pilot
- which option to develop further
- whether evidence from another place is usable here
- whether implementation is feasible

The learning activity exists to improve a decision.

If there is no decision it could affect, ask why the learning work is needed.

## 2. Ask whether missing knowledge is actually the constraint

Some decisions remain difficult because we do not know enough.

Others remain difficult because people disagree about:

- values
- rights
- distribution
- acceptable risk
- political interests
- who should bear cost
- who has authority

McGuinness and Slaughter explicitly note that some public problems are primarily contests among political and economic interests rather than problems that experimentation can resolve.

Ask:

> **Would better evidence plausibly change the decision, or is the unresolved issue mainly a value or political choice?**

If the answer is mainly a value or political choice, send the work to the relevant criteria, stakeholder, trade-off or decision method.

Do not prescribe an experiment merely because the decision is contested.

## 3. Distinguish planning-heavy from learning-heavy situations

A **planning-heavy situation** is one where:

- the problem is reasonably understood
- the important mechanism is reasonably understood
- relevant evidence transfers adequately to the current setting
- the intervention is sufficiently specified
- the main work concerns coordination, design, funding, sequencing or execution

A **learning-heavy situation** is one where important uncertainty remains about:

- what people actually experience
- what is causing the problem
- which constraint is binding
- whether an intervention is workable
- whether the proposed mechanism is real
- how people or organisations will respond
- how implementation behaves in practice
- whether the intervention causes the expected effect
- whether evidence from elsewhere applies here

Most real policy work contains some of both.

The purpose of the distinction is to identify where **additional planning would merely make an uncertain assumption more detailed**.

## 4. Look for false certainty created by linear planning

McGuinness and Slaughter describe a traditional pattern in which:

1. research is completed
2. a solution is specified
3. a law, policy or programme is agreed
4. implementation follows
5. feedback arrives much later

The danger is that important assumptions remain untested until the policy is expensive or difficult to change.

Ask:

- Which important assumption currently exists only on paper?
- What would we learn too late if we waited for full implementation?
- Which part of the proposed policy depends on behaviour we have not observed?
- Where could direct contact with people change the diagnosis?

Do not treat a detailed implementation plan as evidence that the underlying assumptions are sound.

# Part 2 - Diagnose the uncertainty

## 5. Classify the uncertainty

Use these categories to identify what is genuinely unknown.

### Problem uncertainty

**Question:** What is actually happening?

Examples:

- the scale of the condition is unclear
- several symptoms have been merged
- the observed pattern may be measurement artefact
- the problem may differ across groups or places

Likely learning moves:

- existing data
- descriptive analysis
- field inquiry
- observation
- interviews
- improved measurement

### Experience uncertainty

**Question:** What are affected people actually encountering?

McGuinness and Slaughter show how policies designed at a distance can misread the problem people face.

Likely learning moves:

- observation
- contextual inquiry
- service walkthrough
- interviews
- direct feedback
- frontline inquiry

Do not assume administrative categories describe lived experience accurately.

### Mechanism uncertainty

**Question:** Why is this happening?

There may be several plausible causal explanations.

Likely learning moves:

- competing hypotheses
- targeted evidence
- qualitative inquiry
- natural variation
- small tests
- stronger causal evaluation where warranted

Do not settle on the first plausible story.

### Constraint uncertainty

**Question:** What is actually preventing improvement?

Rodrik's diagnostic approach focuses on identifying the binding constraint rather than applying a universal recipe.

Possible constraints include:

- information
- incentives
- access
- capability
- institutional rules
- capacity
- trust
- behaviour
- coordination
- another local condition

Ask:

> **If we removed this constraint, what evidence would we expect to see change?**

### Option uncertainty

**Question:** What form of intervention might work?

The problem may be understood while the solution space remains open.

Likely learning moves:

- scouting
- co-design
- prototype
- comparative testing
- rapid-cycle experimentation

### Implementation uncertainty

**Question:** Can this intervention work in ordinary practice?

Possible unknowns:

- staff behaviour
- operational load
- workflow
- procurement
- legal process
- uptake
- channel performance
- coordination between organisations

Likely learning moves:

- live prototype
- limited rollout
- simulation
- operational test
- observation
- rapid-cycle test

### Effect uncertainty

**Question:** Does a sufficiently specified intervention cause the outcome we expect?

This is the strongest case for verificatory experimentation.

Possible methods:

- A/B test
- RCT
- quasi-experimental design
- another credible causal method

Do not ask a causal-effect question before the intervention and outcome are sufficiently specified.

### Transfer uncertainty

**Question:** Will evidence from another context hold here?

Rodrik makes this a central concern.

Ask whether relevant conditions differ across:

- population
- place
- institutional arrangements
- delivery capacity
- incentives
- prior exposure
- timing
- political conditions
- scale
- implementation model

A strong result somewhere else is evidence.

It is not automatic proof of the same result here.

## 6. State competing explanations

Where mechanism or constraint is unclear, do not jump directly to one hypothesis.

Write 2 or more plausible explanations.

For each:

> **If this explanation were true, what would we expect to observe?**

Example:

**Explanation A:** People are not using the service because they do not know it exists.  
**Expected signals:** low awareness; higher use among people exposed to information; uptake rises after targeted communication.

**Explanation B:** People know about it but the process is too difficult.  
**Expected signals:** reasonable awareness; high abandonment; repeated requests for help; take-up does not improve much after awareness activity.

This is diagnostic reasoning.

The purpose is to identify evidence capable of distinguishing between explanations.

## 7. Use theory explicitly

Rodrik argues that practical experimentation still needs theory.

Theory here does not mean a grand academic model.

It means an explicit account of:

> **why this intervention should produce this effect under these conditions**

Without that account:

- evidence is difficult to interpret
- failures are difficult to diagnose
- transfer to another context is difficult to judge

Do not accept:

> We tried it and the metric went up.

Ask:

> **What mechanism do we think produced that change?**

and:

> **What else could have produced it?**

## 8. Identify the critical uncertainty

Do not try to resolve every unknown.

Ask:

> **Which uncertainty is most capable of changing what we should do next?**

Consider:

- decision importance
- current uncertainty
- plausible effect on the choice
- cost and time of learning
- reversibility of acting without learning

A useful critical uncertainty should be specific enough that resolving it changes a decision.

Weak:

> We need to understand the service better.

Stronger:

> We do not know whether most repeat contact is caused by uncertainty about receipt or uncertainty about eligibility to continue, and those imply different interventions.

The critical uncertainty should become the focus of the next learning move.

# Part 3 - Choose the learning move

## 9. Define the learning outcome

Demos Helsinki requires a clear learning outcome.

Complete:

> **After this work, we need to know whether...**

Examples:

- ...users can understand the revised instruction without support.
- ...the current delay is primarily caused before or after case creation.
- ...staff can operate the new workflow at expected volumes.
- ...the communication causes a measurable reduction in repeat contact.
- ...a result observed elsewhere still appears under our institutional conditions.

Do not confuse the learning outcome with the policy outcome.

Policy outcome:

> Reduce avoidable contact.

Learning outcome:

> Determine whether receipt confirmation materially reduces reassurance-driven contact among the target cohort.

The learning activity exists to reduce uncertainty about the policy.

## 10. Scout before inventing where useful

McGuinness and Slaughter recommend looking for solutions already being developed in practice.

Ask:

- Who is already dealing with a similar problem?
- Is another team, place or service already trying something?
- What appears to work there?
- What contextual conditions make it work?
- What could be adapted rather than copied?

Scouting is a source of hypotheses and options.

It is not proof that a practice will transfer.

## 11. Choose evidence based on the question

Rodrik rejects a simple hierarchy in which one method is always superior.

Different evidence has different strengths.

### Existing quantitative evidence

Useful for:

- scale
- trends
- variation
- associations
- baseline conditions
- identifying candidate patterns

Watch for:

- measurement problems
- confounding
- missing variables
- selection

### Qualitative evidence

Useful for:

- local knowledge
- experience
- mechanism
- unexpected explanations
- implementation
- identifying options outsiders have missed

Watch for:

- selection of interviewees
- incentives
- interpretation
- over-generalisation

### Observational field evidence

Useful for:

- real behaviour
- service interaction
- workflow
- tacit practice
- mismatches between formal process and actual process

### Exploratory tests

Useful where the problem, mechanism or intervention is still unclear.

Examples:

- prototype
- rapid-cycle test
- small live trial
- low-fidelity simulation
- comparative concept test

### Verificatory tests

Useful where the intervention and outcome are sufficiently specified and the main question concerns causal effect.

Examples:

- A/B test
- RCT
- quasi-experimental design

Ask:

> **What evidence would most usefully change our view of this uncertainty?**

Do not ask only:

> What is the most rigorous method available?

## 12. Distinguish exploratory from verificatory work

Demos Helsinki places experiments on a continuum from exploratory to verificatory.

### Explore when:

- the problem is still unclear
- the mechanism is uncertain
- the solution space is open
- several intervention forms are plausible
- feasibility is not yet understood
- the team needs to expose assumptions

Typical methods:

- prototype
- observation
- rapid-cycle test
- co-creation
- low-cost live test

### Verify when:

- the intervention is specified
- there is preliminary evidence it may work
- the outcome is measurable
- the main remaining question is causal
- there is enough stability to make comparison meaningful

Typical methods:

- A/B test
- RCT
- quasi-experimental method
- another causal design

Do not use a verificatory method to compensate for an under-specified problem.

## 13. Separate purpose from ambition

Demos Helsinki also distinguishes:

- **exploratory ↔ verificatory**
- **incremental ↔ transformative**

Do not confuse them.

A small exploratory test can investigate a fundamental change in the system.

A large RCT can test a narrow incremental intervention.

Ask:

- Is the learning purpose exploratory or verificatory?
- Is the proposed change incremental or more fundamental?

This stops "start small" becoming "think small".

## 14. Prefer the lightest credible learning move

The next step should be proportionate to the uncertainty.

Ask:

> **What is the least costly, least risky way to learn enough to improve the decision?**

Examples:

- inspect existing administrative data before commissioning research
- observe 10 real cases before building a prototype
- prototype before running a pilot
- run a rapid test before a larger causal evaluation
- use a natural comparison before creating a complex trial

Do not run a large experiment to answer a question that 5 interviews could resolve.

Do not use 5 interviews to claim a causal effect that requires stronger evidence.

## 15. Form a hypothesis where the question supports one

Demos Helsinki defines a hypothesis as a statement linking an assumption to an observable outcome.

A useful form is:

> **If `[assumption / intervention]`, then we expect `[observable change]`, because `[mechanism]`.**

Example:

> If uncertainty about receipt is a major cause of repeat contact, then applicants receiving confirmation should make fewer receipt-status calls over the following 3 weeks, because they no longer need to check whether the application arrived.

A hypothesis should be:

- specific
- observable
- capable of being wrong
- connected to the learning question

Do not force hypothesis language onto open exploratory inquiry where the point is to discover what the relevant explanation might be.

## 16. Define the evidence before testing

Before the learning move begins, state:

- what will be observed
- how it will be measured
- over what period
- from whom or where
- what comparison matters
- what qualitative evidence matters
- what important unintended effects will be watched

Do not decide after seeing the result which metric "counts".

## 17. Define what different results would mean

This is essential.

Complete:

> **If we observe X, we will...**

> **If we observe Y, we will...**

> **If the result is ambiguous, we will...**

Possible next moves:

- develop further
- scale
- adapt
- test again
- test in another context
- stop
- return to the problem framing
- gather another kind of evidence

If no plausible result changes what the team will do, the proposed learning exercise is weak.

## 18. Keep experiments bounded

Demos Helsinki emphasises a limited time frame and a predetermined endpoint.

Define:

- start
- end
- cohort or setting
- scope
- measures
- review point

Do not allow "pilot" to become the permanent state of an intervention without a decision.

## 19. Consider ethics and harm before testing

Demos Helsinki explicitly asks public-sector experimenters to consider:

- possible negative outcomes
- whether benefits outweigh harms
- who is affected
- how stakeholders will be involved
- informed consent where relevant

Before testing, ask:

- Who bears the risk of being wrong?
- Is withholding or varying the intervention acceptable?
- Could the test create material harm?
- Are participants aware where they need to be?
- Are some groups likely to bear more risk than others?

Do not treat "small" as automatically safe.

# Part 4 - Interpret and act on the learning

## 20. Ask what was directly learned

After the learning move, state only what the evidence supports.

Separate:

### Direct finding

What happened in the actual setting.

### Interpretation

What mechanism or explanation the finding supports.

### Extrapolation

What you think may also be true elsewhere or at larger scale.

Do not collapse all 3 into:

> It worked.

## 21. Distinguish internal and external validity

Rodrik's central warning is that strong causal identification in one setting does not automatically answer the wider policy question.

### Internal validity

How credible is the claim that the intervention caused the observed effect in the setting studied?

### External validity

How credible is the claim that the result applies to:

- other people
- other places
- another scale
- another delivery model
- another institutional setting
- another time

Both matter to policy use.

Do not describe an RCT result as universally "hard evidence" without considering transfer.

## 22. Ask what contextual conditions may have produced the result

For any important result, record conditions that may matter.

Examples:

- participant characteristics
- prior exposure
- staff capability
- local relationships
- resources
- delivery channel
- incentives
- institutional rules
- political backing
- scale
- timing

Then ask:

> **Which of these conditions are also present where we want to use the result?**

This is part of deciding whether evidence travels.

## 23. Do not confuse successful testing with readiness to scale

Demos Helsinki treats scaling as an intended destination of successful experimentation.

Rodrik gives us the necessary qualification.

A positive test can support several possible conclusions:

- scale
- adapt and scale
- test at a larger scale
- test in another context
- investigate the mechanism further
- retain as local practice
- stop despite local success because conditions do not travel

Ask:

> **What new claim does scaling require beyond the claim established by the experiment?**

Possible additional claims include:

- implementation capacity exists
- the mechanism survives scale
- unit costs remain acceptable
- target populations are sufficiently similar
- political and institutional conditions are adequate

"Worked here" and "should scale" are separate propositions.

## 24. Tighten the feedback loop

McGuinness and Slaughter argue that data are useful because they shorten the distance between what people experience and what decision makers can see.

Ask:

- What signal should remain visible after the test?
- How quickly would we know if the intervention stopped working?
- Can people affected report problems directly?
- Who will review the signal?
- What action follows deterioration?

A learning loop should continue after the initial experiment where the policy remains uncertain in practice.

## 25. Treat an unwelcome result as learning

Demos Helsinki argues that a hypothesis being wrong is not itself experimental failure if useful learning was produced.

Ask:

- Which assumption was weakened?
- What new explanation became more plausible?
- What should stop?
- What should change?
- What should be tested next?

Do not relabel an unwelcome result as "inconclusive" merely to preserve the original idea.

## 26. Know when to stop learning and decide

Learning has a cost.

Ask:

- Has the critical uncertainty fallen enough to support the decision?
- Would more evidence plausibly change the choice?
- Is the next piece of learning worth its cost and delay?
- Is the remaining uncertainty unavoidable?
- Is the decision reversible if we act now?

Do not keep testing simply because uncertainty still exists.

The aim is sufficient knowledge for the next responsible decision, not certainty.

# Learning-move scaffold

Work through these in order where useful.

1. **Decision:** What real choice are we trying to make?
2. **Is learning the issue?** Would new evidence plausibly change the decision?
3. **Uncertainty type:** problem / experience / mechanism / constraint / option / implementation / effect / transfer.
4. **Competing explanations:** What could be true?
5. **Diagnostic signals:** What would we expect to observe under each explanation?
6. **Critical uncertainty:** Which unknown is most capable of changing the next decision?
7. **Learning outcome:** What do we need to know differently?
8. **Existing evidence:** What do we already know, and how well does it apply here?
9. **Scouting:** Who has already tried something relevant?
10. **Learning purpose:** exploratory or verificatory?
11. **Ambition:** incremental or more fundamental?
12. **Learning move:** What is the lightest credible way to reduce the uncertainty?
13. **Hypothesis:** If appropriate, what observable claim are we testing?
14. **Evidence plan:** What will we collect and from whom?
15. **Ethics and risk:** Who bears the risk and what safeguards are required?
16. **Decision rules:** What would different results cause us to do?
17. **Boundaries:** What is the scope and end point?
18. **Interpretation:** What did we directly learn?
19. **Transfer:** What contextual conditions limit generalisation?
20. **Next move:** scale / adapt / test again / gather different evidence / stop / reframe.

→ **Learning move:** To decide `[decision]`, we need to reduce uncertainty about `[critical uncertainty]`. We currently think `[hypothesis or competing explanations]`. The most proportionate next move is `[method]` because `[reason it fits the question]`. We will observe `[evidence]`. If `[result A]`, we will `[decision A]`; if `[result B]`, we will `[decision B]`. The main limit on using the result more widely is `[transfer condition]`.

# Anti-patterns

## Pitfall A - Experiment first, question later

**Failure:** "Let's pilot it" appears before anyone has stated what they need to learn.

**Challenge:** name the decision and critical uncertainty first.

## Pitfall B - Planning detail substitutes for knowledge

**Failure:** an uncertain intervention acquires a detailed roadmap, delivery plan and governance model before its key assumptions have been tested.

**Challenge:** identify which assumptions are still unsupported.

## Pitfall C - Treating every hard decision as an evidence problem

**Failure:** a value conflict or political disagreement is sent for more research.

**Challenge:** ask whether better evidence could actually settle the issue.

## Pitfall D - One causal story becomes the truth

**Failure:** the first plausible explanation drives the intervention.

**Challenge:** state competing explanations and expected diagnostic signals.

## Pitfall E - RCT as default

**Failure:** randomisation is treated as the obvious next step whenever uncertainty exists.

**Challenge:** match the method to the question and stage of understanding.

## Pitfall F - Qualitative evidence treated as weak by definition

**Failure:** local experience and field inquiry are dismissed because they do not estimate causal effect.

**Challenge:** ask what uncertainty the evidence is being used to reduce.

## Pitfall G - A pilot without a learning rule

**Failure:** the intervention runs for a while and the team reviews whatever happened.

**Challenge:** define the learning outcome, evidence and decision rule before starting.

## Pitfall H - Confirmation exercise

**Failure:** the team calls something an experiment but no plausible result would cause it to abandon or alter the idea.

**Challenge:** ask what evidence would prove the current view wrong.

## Pitfall I - Explore and verify are confused

**Failure:** a high-rigour causal design tests an intervention that is still conceptually unstable.

**Challenge:** explore first where problem, mechanism or option uncertainty remains high.

## Pitfall J - "It worked"

**Failure:** a local result becomes a universal claim.

**Challenge:** separate direct finding, interpretation and extrapolation.

## Pitfall K - Internal validity is treated as the whole evidence question

**Failure:** strong causal identification in one setting is assumed to settle use elsewhere.

**Challenge:** examine external validity and contextual conditions.

## Pitfall L - Copying "best practice"

**Failure:** an intervention is transferred because it succeeded elsewhere.

**Challenge:** identify the mechanism and conditions that may explain the original success.

## Pitfall M - Small means low ambition

**Failure:** starting with a limited test restricts the team to minor improvements.

**Challenge:** separate the scale of the test from the ambition of the change.

## Pitfall N - Pilot becomes permanent

**Failure:** a temporary test continues indefinitely without a scaling, stopping or redesign decision.

**Challenge:** set an end point and review decision.

## Pitfall O - Scaling follows automatically from positive results

**Failure:** local effectiveness is treated as sufficient evidence for system-wide adoption.

**Challenge:** test transfer, capacity, cost and institutional conditions separately.

## Pitfall P - Endless learning

**Failure:** uncertainty becomes a reason to keep researching rather than make a reversible decision.

**Challenge:** ask whether more evidence is likely to change the choice enough to justify the delay.

# Self-check rubric

## Must pass

- A real **decision** has been named.
- The agent has checked whether the unresolved issue is genuinely about **missing knowledge** rather than primarily values or politics.
- The **critical uncertainty** is specific enough to affect that decision.
- The learning move matches the **type of uncertainty**.
- The agent has not defaulted to experimentation where existing evidence or simpler inquiry could answer the question.
- Exploratory and verificatory purposes have been distinguished where relevant.
- A hypothesis is used only where the question is specific enough to support one.
- The evidence to be gathered is stated before the result is known.
- Different plausible results have a stated implication for the next decision.
- Important ethical or harm questions have been considered before live testing.
- The interpretation separates **direct finding, inference and extrapolation**.
- Transfer to another context is not assumed.
- A positive result is not automatically treated as permission to scale.

## Should pass

- Competing explanations have been considered where mechanism or constraint is uncertain.
- The causal mechanism is explicit enough to interpret the result.
- People directly affected or frontline actors are included where their experience can materially change the diagnosis.
- Existing practices have been scouted where relevant.
- The learning move is the lightest credible option.
- The experiment or test is bounded in time and scope.
- The result has a clear review point.
- Unwelcome results can alter the course of action.
- Important contextual conditions affecting external validity are recorded.
- The method recognises when further learning has low value and a decision should be made.

## Process reminder

- Learning is iterative.
- New evidence can send the work back to the problem, mechanism, option or implementation assumption.
- Strong evidence in one setting can still require judgment before transfer.
- The best learning move is the one that most usefully changes the next decision, not the one with the most impressive method.
- The objective is not certainty. It is enough justified confidence to make the next responsible move.

This shared method governs **uncertainty and learning across the policy-analysis journey**.

Its primary home is `../capabilities/evidence-guidance.md`, but other skills may call it for specific purposes.

### Problem — `../capabilities/problem-definition-guidance.md`

May use it to ask whether the framing is understood well enough to proceed or requires direct inquiry.

Do not turn `01` into experiment design.

### Stakeholders — `../capabilities/stakeholder-analysis-guidance.md`

May use it where stakeholder motivations, beliefs or relationships are uncertain and need evidence.

Do not use stakeholder engagement merely as a generic experiment.

### Evidence — `../capabilities/evidence-guidance.md`

Canonical home for:

- critical uncertainty
- evidence choice
- competing explanations
- exploratory inquiry
- hypothesis formation
- experiment or learning-move design
- decision rules

### Options — `../capabilities/alternatives-guidance.md`

May use scouting and experimental options.

Do not put the full evidence-design method inside option generation.

### Criteria — `../capabilities/criteria-guidance.md`

May define what evidence would count as success.

Do not use criteria to choose the learning method.

### Outcomes — `../capabilities/outcomes-guidance.md`

May call this method when projected outcomes depend on assumptions too weak to forecast responsibly.

Do not design the whole experiment inside outcomes projection.

### Trade-offs — `../capabilities/trade-offs-guidance.md`

May use unresolved uncertainty to show when the apparent trade-off is unstable.

Do not resolve the uncertainty by inventing a weight or estimate.

### Decision — `../capabilities/decision-guidance.md`

May use critical uncertainty, reversibility and decision rules to decide whether to act, test further or set a review condition.

Do not reopen every uncertainty once the decision is good enough to make.

### Story — `../capabilities/storytelling-guidance.md`

May communicate the learning logic and uncertainty.

Do not manufacture missing evidence during storytelling.

### Agent evaluation — `../capabilities/agent-evaluation-guidance.md`

Does not use this method to evaluate the agent itself.

Agent evaluation has its own method.

# One-line reference

> Good uncertainty and learning practice starts with the decision, identifies the unknown most capable of changing it, diagnoses what kind of uncertainty it is, chooses the lightest credible way to learn, defines in advance what different findings would mean, and treats every result as evidence from a particular context rather than automatic permission to scale.
