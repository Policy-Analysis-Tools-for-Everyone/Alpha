**Subject: Call for evidence: Workforce planning to deliver clean, secure energy**
**To:** [Secretary of State for Energy Security and Net Zero].
**CC:** The Energy Security and Net Zero committee.
**From:** [Author] on behalf of The Green Jobs Foundation

**Executive summary**

Demand for clean energy jobs in the UK is exponentially outpacing the available workforce—creating a rapidly emerging deficit of more than 200,000 green-skilled workers in the energy sector.[^1] At a bare minimum, the green talent pool now needs to double to keep pace with the projected demand.[^2] Otherwise, Labour is at risk of not having the available workforce to achieve its mission of making Britain a clean energy superpower by 2030 and capture the Net Zero "growth opportunity of the 21st century".[^3]

This letter recommends that, without direct intervention from the Department of Energy Security and Net Zero, it will be impossible to deliver mission-critical infrastructure that will shape a future skills and employment system capable of delivering a high-quality clean energy workforce by 2030—missing the opportunity to be seen globally as leaders in Net Zero.

**Key factors shaping the deficit**

There are an estimated 571,000 workers currently employed in the UK energy sector[^4], 47.7% (272,400) of whom are already fulfilling clean energy roles.[^5] With your commitment to create 650,000 new clean energy jobs, the UK will need to attract and recruit more than 300,000 new people into the workforce. With around 216,000 of the existing workforce needing to be reskilled, and a low expectation of employment growth over the next 5 years, the UK is facing a growing deficit of more than 200,000 high-skilled green workers.[^1] There are three main challenges contributing to this:

1. **An exclusionary green talent pipeline**
   Information and communication failures at key decision points in the education system fail to address social barriers that emphasise a lack of prestige and male-only culture around apprenticeships and trade-based careers.[^6] This leads to the exclusion of young women—projected to occupy less than 25% of green jobs by 2025[^7]—and those from marginalised communities from considering, accessing, or entering the clean energy workforce.[^8]
2. **Decreasing skills investment**
   Despite 4 million workers across all sectors needing to be reskilled by 2030[^9], UK employer training expenditure is at its lowest since 2011.[^10] This has resulted from a lack of a clear industrial strategy for workforce planning, resources to upskill staff beyond minimum requirements, strong international labour supply, and an overly complex skills landscape.[^11] With limited access to green skills training, fewer workers will transition to high-skilled clean energy roles[^2]—leading to fewer businesses having the capabilities for clean energy projects.
3. **Difficulties accessing and retaining talent**
   A negative perception of employment services, a lack of coordination between them, and higher pay demands for green jobs make it difficult for employers to access and retain talent.[^12] Compounded by a lack of sector-specific recruitment pathways, this leads to businesses relying on informal relationships, hiring under-qualified workers—worsening the UK's skills mismatch[^10]—or missing out on clean energy projects entirely.

**Criteria for delivering clean energy**

Labour has set out strong outcomes and criteria for this mission: (1) **delivering energy security**; (2) **protecting bill payers**; (3) **providing good jobs**; and (4) **climate leadership**. I recommend that future commitments to building the workforce should also consider (5) **building a "just" green-talent supply** to evaluate the initiatives needed to deliver the workforce for 2030 and beyond.

**Options**

**Option 1: Improve the workforce through incentives, flexibility and opportunity**

a. **Incentivise job creation:** Implement the British Job Bonus Fund to drive and reward energy developers for clean energy job creation.
b. **Offer flexibility:** Cooperate with DfE to identify green-skills training programmes for the Growth and Skills Levy to allow employers to spend on apprenticeships and training programmes–offering training through a single digital platform that integrates providers and demonstrates government targets.
c. **Create targeted apprenticeships:** Fund "clean energy" apprenticeships, targeted at regions with high employment disparities, with a specific focus on 16–24-year-old women and those from marginalised backgrounds. Consider regions equally to reduce location barriers.

**Assessment:** Incentivises spend, job creation and pipeline but addresses symptoms, not root causes. Does not guarantee market supply or improvement of green skills.

**Option 2: Shape the workforce through investment and new governance**

d. **Announce a 20-year Net Zero Skills and Workforce Action Plan:** Collaborate with the Department for Education and the Department for Work and Pensions to create a 20-year vision of how the UK will shape the net zero workforce for 2030 and beyond with a series of 5-year, incremental action plans that prioritise and direct resources.
e. **Invest heavily into employment infrastructure:** Building on the success of Greater London Authority's No Wrong Door initiative that integrates employment and skills systems in London, scale the approach region by region, especially in high-demand areas.
f. **Introduce new models of governance:** Embed "non-price" conditionalities into clean energy funding schemes to measure and reward businesses that: improve access to clean energy opportunities, demonstrate upskilling, and cooperate to improve local infrastructure.

**Assessment:** Politically aligned and will lay the foundations for future success, however, long-term investments carry inherent uncertainty and public scrutiny.

**Recommendation – Option 2**

**Department for Energy Security and Net Zero:** It is essential that the department plays a pivotal role in delivering mission-critical infrastructure that shapes, delivers and sustains a future clean energy workforce. Proposed actions improve the coordination between the skills and education systems and ensure funds are strategically allocated to nurture a "just" workforce pipeline.

**Office for Clean Energy Jobs:** Launching an "intergenerational" 20-year Net Zero Skills and Workforce Action Plan will create a long-term blueprint for local and national government, businesses, and educational institutions to plan how they will educate, employ, and train the future workforce for 2030, 2050 and beyond.

**Implementation and next steps**

This recommendation requires immediate action. I propose:

- The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero prototypes the No Wrong Door initiative within a select number of small regions, gathering insights and adapting accordingly.
- Work with HMRC to immediately assess how to embed "non-price" conditions into the British Jobs Bonus Fund before launching nationally.
- The Office for Clean Energy Jobs establishes a working group with the Department for Education, the Department for Work and Pensions and industry partners to review existing research and skills assessments, share insights and outline an initial 20-year skills and workforce vision with an initial 5-year action plan within the next parliamentary year.

On behalf of The Green Jobs Foundation
