**Background for Markers**

Somerville, Massachusetts is a small city directly bordering Boston. With a population of just over 81,000 people packed into only 4.12 square miles (10.7 square kilometers) it is the densest city in all of New England. Served by 7 metro stops, as well as numerous bus lines, it is incredibly well connected to Boston proper, as well as the neighboring city of Cambridge. The city is overwhelmingly liberal, with only 2.7% of residents registered as republican, which strongly influences local legislation and policymaking.

The homelessness problem in Somerville has increased dramatically over the last decade, coinciding with a 28% rise across the state. A 2024 point-in-time census of the homeless identified 26 individuals who were completely unsheltered and another 124 in temporary housing or shelters, representing the largest number since 2016. The unsheltered generally congregate in parks in and around Davis Square, the city's main center for business and entertainment. Over the last few years, there has been an uptick in crime involving the homeless population, including stabbings, open drug use, and littering (including discarded needles).

Somerville has taken some steps to address this issue, including adding three community health workers and a social worker to the city payroll, opened an overnight warming shelter, more than doubled the number of police patrols in affected areas, and worked with the Somerville Homeless Coalition, a local nonprofit, to find housing for 65 individuals since January of 2025. The city has also opened a first in the nation dual sharp disposal for needles, syringes, and sharps. In partnership with the Somerville Homeless Coalition (SHC), a local church has constructed a new 26 bed shelter, but backlash from neighbouring residents has delayed its opening, and may lead to a court battle.

**To: [Mayor of Somerville]**
**From: [Author], Concerned Citizen**
**Subject: Strategic Interventions for Sustainable Reductions in Somerville Homelessness**

Dear [Mayor],

The persistent and highly visible epidemic of homelessness across Somerville has reached a critical juncture. Somerville, particularly Davis Square, has seen a sharp uptick in semi-permanent encampments since the start of the decade. A January 2024 census recorded 26 unsheltered individuals - the largest number since 2016. While there are many causes of homelessness, this particular surge is linked to regional displacement of unhoused individuals moving along the Red Line, and the recent shuttering of the 58 bed Wellness Center in Cambridge. The consequence has been a deterioration of public perception following incidents of open drug use, discarded needles, and two back-to-back stabbings in September of 2024. Local residents are now referring to Davis Square as "seedy, disgusting, filthy, and chaotic."

Despite current efforts (doubled police patrols, new health and social service workers, sharps disposal) the increase in unsheltered individuals has resulted in widespread community frustration over violence and public disorder. You yourself have said that we need to maintain the city's compassionate, non-criminilisation policy while delivering the tangible improvements residents demand. By instituting a housing first policy while working on a regional solution, Somerville can lead the state in tackling the homelessness crisis head on, and you can fulfill your campaign promise.

**Options:**

**I. Housing First**

Housing First (HF) is the gold standard of homelessness interventions, and works by supplying permanent supportive housing (PSH) to those who need it. By ensuring that all unhoused individuals have access to non-abstinance-contingent housing, Somerville can become a beacon of compassion to the region. At an average cost of $2,200 per month per housing unit, it would cost just shy of $4M per year to permanently house all 150 individuals currently experiencing homelessness in Somerville, including all those currently in shelters. Considering the city budget of $380M and the Somerville Housing Coalition (SHC) budget of $8.4M, this cost is a drop in the bucket to immediately address the most pressing concern of the unhoused.

| Action | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| • Allocate $2M from the next city budget<br>• Partner with SHC to supply $2M<br>• Secure leases for properties across Somerville to house the homeless population | • Immediately tackles the most pressing need of the unhoused<br>• Stabilises community sentiment by getting individuals off the street and into accommodation | • Expensive, and likely to rise in cost with inflation<br>• Does not solve for co-morbidities, such as mental health and substance use<br>• Could affect the city-wide housing shortage |

**II. Homelessness Navigation Center**

In partnership with the Somerville Homeless Coalition (SHC), the city could open a single, centralised, low-barrier, 24/7 center of assistance for the homeless in Somerville. By centralising services and offering a one-stop-shop, the city can reduce the barriers for entry to all the support services available to the unhoused in Somerville.

| Action | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| • Secure and rapidly renovate a city owned property to house a 24/7 service center.<br>• Move all related city services (warming shelter, social worker offices, sharps disposal) into this single location.<br>• Hire dedicated case managers and addiction specialists to perform continuous, high touch services, beyond simple outreach. | • Highly effective at connecting individuals with individual services.<br>• Maintains the city's commitment to non-criminilisation.<br>• Offers a central and dedicated point of contact for service providers.<br>• Additional employment opportunities for case managers and specialists | • Significant up-front expenditure<br>• Vulnerable to NIMBYism and neighborhood opposition<br>• A central location could exclude those who camp further away<br>• Does not directly offer PSH |

**III. Safe Haven Strategy**

Temporarially designate Seven Hills Park as an Intensive Outreach and Stabilisation Zone, offering much needed services to the unhoused, while centralising outreach.

| Action | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| • Deploy 24/7 staffing from the city's health and human services department (in addition to the already present policing) to the park.<br>• Provide daily cleaning, portable sanitation spaces, and safe, well-lit spaces for non-permanent shelter.<br>• Establish a harm reduction station to distribute Narcan, clean syringes, and medical care<br>• Connect all individuals with the city's existing services and fast-track them for any new housing or shelter opportunities | • Lowest initial capital cost<br>• Directly addresses health and sanitation issues<br>• Maximises the compassionate, non-criminilisation policies of the city<br>• Additional employment opportunities for case managers and specialists | • Does not restore public park spaces immediately<br>• Could lead to exacerbated resident frustration<br>• Requires high sustained operational and staffing costs<br>• May be perceived as formalising the encampment |

**IV. Regional Infrastructure Initiative**

Work with Cambridge and Boston to establish a Greater Boston Homeless Transit Corridor Pact. Work regionally and combine resources to provide the wider homeless population with all the resources they need. This prevents duplicated effort or gaps in care when homeless individuals move within the Greater Boston metro area. By combining resources, the cities can ensure that no matter where a homeless person is, they have access to PSH, healthcare, food, and other resources they might need to get back on their feet.

| Action | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| • Commit initial funding to developing PSH across the three cities, contingent on matching funds from Boston and Cambridge<br>• Jointly lobby the state legislature for emergency funding to aid in the construction of PSH, prevent future shelter cuts, and create a statewide low-barrier housing voucher program for unsheltered individuals | • Greater potential to address the regional nature of the crisis<br>• Provides wider access to the gold-standard, long-term solution of PSH<br>• Shifts the political and financial burden away from Somerville alone | • Unknown time required for implementation<br>• Requires political will from Cambridge, Boston, and the State House.<br>• Immediate improvements in Somerville will be minimal while regional infrastructure develops |

**Recommendation and Implementation**

Given the time-sensitivity of this issue, option one is recommended for rapid relief while the political foundations to implement option four are established. During the next budget cycle, Somerville should identify opportunities to appropriate $2M and work directly with SHC to supply the remaining $2M to secure immediate PSH for the unhoused. Meanwhile, immediately reach out to Boston, Cambridge, and the state legislature to establish an initiative to tackle this regional problem at the regional level. Funds appropriated from option one can then be slowly diverted into the regional initiative as it takes shape, ensuring continuity of care for those experiencing homelessness.

Offering immediate housing provides immediate, tangible improvements in public health and sanitation, increases service connection rates, and stems the public safety concerns at a reasonable cost to the city and SHC. At the same time, leveraging the political capital of a new mayoral administration to initiate a regional approach to homelessness is necessary to address the systemic influx of homeless traveling along the Red Line, and prevent future, larger crises from developing. By committing to both a swift, local, health-focused solution and a long-term regional framework, your administration can demonstrate both competence and compassion.
