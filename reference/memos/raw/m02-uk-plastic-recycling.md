**Cover note to markers**

This Memo is addressed to [Director of Growth and Productivity at HM Treasury], from [Chair of HM Government's Circular Economy Taskforce], requesting support to close loopholes in the UK's plastic recycling system.

**Background on decision maker and sender**

[The Chair][^i] is the recently departed CEO of the Ellen MacArthur Foundation where he held senior relationships with businesses and policy makers and drove global efforts with business to eliminate plastic waste and pollution[^ii].

[The Director], as Director of Growth and Productivity, will be seeking solutions to deliver on the government's agenda to create economic growth while operating under significant political pressure and in a challenging economic environment[^iii]. Within this context, the idea of 'value creation' has become de rigour as the treasury seeks windows of opportunity to accelerate growth, at low levels of investment – lest they frighten the bond markets.

**Political context**

The current labour administration has worked to position itself as a strategic ally of business while also on the side of working people, yet faces criticism from the former on the cost of doing business[^iv] and the latter on the cost of living[^v].

Concurrently, a raft of new policies, focused on addressing the issue of plastic waste – including Simpler Recycling[^vi], Deposit Return Schemes[^vii], Extended Producer Responsibility[^viii], and the Plastic Packaging Tax[^ix] – have been introduced aimed at increasing recycling rates and reducing waste, a key issue for consumers[^x].

**Business context**

The recycling industry is under pressure, Veolia, the UK's largest private waste management company, publicly promoted their investment in a new £70m tray to tray recycling facility[^xi], with the announcement timed to coincide with French president Emmanuel Macron's trip to the UK. Yet their public promotion hides private concerns about the plant's longer-term viability as the European and UK recycling industry weakens year on year[^xii].

**FAO:** [Director of Growth and Productivity, HM Treasury]
**From:** [Chair of the Circular Economy Task Force]
**Subject:** Your attention to the pressing issues restricting the UK's recycling industry is requested to support the clean growth of the UK economy.

**1. The issue**

An annual shortfall of between 1650 and 2500KT[^xiii] [^xiv] of verifiably high quality, competitively priced recycled plastic is hindering business compliance with the UK's plastic packaging policies. Closing this gap is essential to deliver HM Government's ambition to facilitate economic growth via a circular economy.[^xv] This memo provides an assessment of specific regulatory and market barriers surrounding this problem and identifies solutions that support HM government's missions[^xvi] to i) deliver economic growth, and ii) position the UK as a clean-energy and resource-efficient leader.

A timely intervention from HM Treasury is critical: Current packaging policies are perceived by many businesses - including John Lewis[^xvii] and Sainsbury's[^xviii] as a substantial and unpredictable cost burden they may need to pass on to consumers. If this challenge is not strategically addressed, the government risks the perception of creating barriers to growth in an industry where it could be a value creator. Its primary objective must be to transform regulatory compliance from a cost centre into an investment trigger for UK-based advanced recycling infrastructure.

**2. Stakeholder alignment**

Closing the loop on recycled material requires aligning needs from across the value chain:

Brands & Packaging Producers: Demand transparent, verified, food-grade Post Consumer Recycled (PCR) content supply to comply with legislation at an affordable price.
Waste Management Companies (WMCs) & Producer Responsibility Organisations (PROs): Require stable, high-value, long-term price and policy signals to justify investment in further infrastructure.
The Public: Requires consistency in local collections to drive participation, confidence, and high-quality material capture at low cost.
Local Authorities (LAs): Need stable, standardised conditions to improve collection services.

**3a. Analysis: Current policy mechanisms**

The landscape for recycling infrastructure is primarily governed by two key policies, both with structural weaknesses:

**i. The Plastic Packaging Tax (PPT)**
PPT aims to stimulate demand for recycled content by placing a tax on companies that use over ten tonnes of plastic packaging containing less than 30% recycled plastic. The tax is currently priced at £223.69 per ton[^xix]. Superficially, it is possible to correlate the introduction of the tax with increased percentages of plastic packaging[^xx]. However, verification loopholes surrounding the quality and origin of low-cost imported plastics remain.[^xxi] The UK imports approximately half of all plastic packaging used[^xxii] - including recycled plastic. This is often from unverifiable sources, stifling our own recycling industry and undermining the UK's commitments to develop a circular economy.

**ii. Extended Producer Responsibility Scheme (EPR)**
From 2025 the UK has introduced EPR,[^xxiii] moving the cost of managing waste from local authorities to brands and producers - a system widely used across Europe - where schemes have proven effective in stimulating the supply of high quality recycled content through supporting standardised, efficient collection and sorting. However, there is little evidence to suggest they support demand creation for domestically recycled plastics, nor is this inherent in their design.

**3b. Analysis: Global market conditions**

The operational environment for UK recycling is challenged by high domestic energy costs[^xxiv], making current recycling methods less competitive compared to imported virgin and recycled material. The UK is not the only market facing this challenge. The picture is similar across the EU[^xxv], with industry further undermined by the significant elasticity of oil prices[^xxvi]. Conversely, The European Union's incoming PPWR Regulation[^xxvii] places mandatory targets for recycled and reusable packaging on businesses. Coinciding, there are a high number of food-grade ready recycled packaging methods businesses expect to be approved by the European Union in the coming years. Approval of these innovations would create a clear technological pathway to solving the food-grade plastic constraints and once approved, could create new demand for recycled plastics from European markets. Indeed, research suggests that the UK's plastic recycling sector could more than triple in size[^xxviii] with the right market and investment conditions.

**4. Strategic options and recommendations**

| Option | Rationale | Opportunities | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Increase Plastic Packaging Tax to £500/tonne | Creates a stronger financial incentive for businesses to use recycled content based on cost-benefit analysis. | Simpler implementation and is favoured by the waste management industry as an investment mechanism in recycling capacity. | Does not resolve fundamental issues including supply, verification, or inconsistent collections; likely to exacerbate cost pressures for businesses and consumers. |
| 2. Subsidise or fund recycling infrastructure | Position the UK to benefit from anticipated EU legislation and increased demand for new, approved food-grade recycling methods. | Potential to develop a new domestic industry that could provide long-term economic benefits. | Expensive investment in an historically volatile and globally competitive industry. |
| 3. Subsidise energy costs of recycling facilities through grants | Creates cost parity with global prices for recycled plastic (rPET/rPE), encouraging investment and increasing business confidence. | Cost analysis suggests this measure brings domestically produced recycled plastic prices in line with global markets. Could increase recycled volumes and achieve lower prices through economies of scale. | Global pricing remains volatile for both virgin and recycled plastics, making the potential depth of future price drops uncertain. |

**5. Recommendations**

We recommend that HM Government proceeds with option three: Subsidising the energy costs of existing and new recycling facilities through an initial phase until 2030. Our analysis[^xxix] indicates that an energy subsidy that reduces costs by 15-25% - representing the typical range of a recycling facility's total OPEX[^xxx] - could successfully align domestic production costs with global market prices[^xxxi] and conservatively generate between 300 and 700 KT of domestically produced recycled plastics [^xxxii]. The revenue generated by the Plastic Packaging Tax (PPT) in 2024, totalled £259 million[^xxxiii]; 0.02% of total government spending. Allocating this revenue to support the recycling sector would serve three key strategic purposes:

1. Close the supply-demand loop: Directly incentivise the growth of domestic recycled content supply.
2. Signal investment: Send a strong, clear signal to businesses about the government's commitment to the future of the recycling industry, and the use of taxes to drive value creation.
3. Build on previous innovation successes: The technique of subsidising nascent industries has multiple successful proof points[^xxxiv], notably in the development of renewable energy infrastructure[^xxxv]. Using this success to fund the recycling sector would create a powerful and positive business and public narrative.

**6. Risks**

Plastics packaging is a global, highly volatile and highly complex industry. Further careful analysis is required to determine the right types of recycling operations to subsidise.

**7. Opportunities**

The strong work the government has delivered to date to close the loop on plastics recycling through developing EPR, and simplifying recycling collection should be commended. This further tweak could prove the investment case for the recycling industry and ensure that the growth opportunity created by the government's efforts to date is fully realised. If successful, this intervention could encourage private investment in UK infrastructure and position the UK as a global leader in clean energy and resource efficiency, ready to take advantage of near-future EU demand for high quality recycled content.

**8. Immediate next steps**

Support the recommendation and present to HRO Rachel Reeves MP for ministerial approval. If granted, a working group should be set up to establish a decision-making framework for the deployment of grants.

I look forward to working together to accelerate the UK's growth ambitions and strengthen its position as a leader in green growth.

Regards,
[Chair of the Circular Economy Taskforce].
