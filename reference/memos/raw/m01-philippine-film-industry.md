**COVER NOTE**

Historically, the Philippine Film Industry (PFI) is one of the most vibrant sectors of the Philippine economy. Spanning three "Golden Ages" (1950s to present),[^1] it enjoys centrality to Filipino life, with high cultural awareness, influence, and multiplatform spillover. Movie stars help shape public discourse with some even joining the realm of politics.[^2]

Through the Film Development Council of the Philippines (FDCP), PFI has been enjoying significant government support.[^3] Its mandate includes the formulation of "policies and programs to… encourage the production of films for commercial purposes." For FY 2026, it implements 1) film location incentives, covering rebates for foreign film producers (31%); 2) local production subsidies (27.7%); 3) education (11.4%); 4) festivals (7.5%); among others.[^4]

FDCP has an operating budget of $8.4 million in FY2025 and a proposed budget of $5.1 million for FY2026 with possible augmentation since budget deliberations are ongoing as of publication.[^5]

With significant funding, strong institutional mandates, not to mention global inroads via co-production agreements,[^6] sustained breakthroughs in festivals like Cannes,[^7] and Hollywood linkages,[^8] PFI seems ready for primetime. However, its overall productivity continues to decline.

Structurally, 60% of PFI's revenues come from the box office.[^9] In 2024, PFI earned only $45.5 million as compared to $144.5 million in 2019.[^10] Ticket prices have increased from $3-5 in 2019 to $5-7 in 2024 vis-à-vis the prevailing daily minimum wage at $8.95[^11] and $10.75,[^12] respectively. This has pushed moviegoers towards more cost-efficient streaming platforms like Netflix[^13] which charges as low as $2.48/month to basically "watch all you can."

Per an FDCP commissioned survey, 67% of film consumption in 2024 came from streaming. Furthermore, only three films, *Un/Happy For You*, *Hello, Love, Again*, and *And the Breadwinner Is…* profited at the box office from a total of 40 films exhibited. This is lower than the 140-film/year production average between 1960 – 1999,[^14] a 5.1% year-on-year decline, excluding Covid.

Thus, according to Cannes Film Festival award-winning director Brillante Mendoza, "Investing in films these days is like knowingly betting on the wrong horse."[^15]

For this policy memo, I will be writing as [Congressman], incumbent chairperson of the Creative Industries Committee in the Philippine House of Representatives and a former movie personality himself. His committee's mandate includes oversight on industrial strategy and fiscal policy for PFI.[^16]

I will be addressing this memo to FDCP chairperson [name], an award-winning filmmaker and thought leader who worked extensively with different PFI sectors prior to his appointment to government.

**SYNOPSIS**

Against a backdrop of production constraints, rising ticket prices, and the dominance of Netflix, the quantity and profitability of Filipino films produced yearly is declining, placing the livelihood of over 300,000 film workers at risk.

**CAST OF CHARACTERS**

- The Cinema Exhibitors Association of the Philippines (CEAP) is the trade organization for movie theater owners and operators.[^17]
- The Entertainment Producers of the Philippines (EPOP) is a guild that consists of commercial and independent producers.[^18]
- The Inter-Guild Alliance (IGA) is a network of worker guilds, including the Directors Guild and AKTOR PH.[^19]
- Moviegoers provide the bulk of PFI's revenues.

**THE TABLE READ**

The median average cost of production in the Philippines is $160,000/film (adjusted for inflation).[^20] This excludes a 20-30% marketing expense for limited releases[^21]. PFI films such as *Heneral Luna* cost as much as $1.33 million to make, though following its box office declaration of $4 million, it at least "broke even." Films need to earn thrice their investment to recoup.[^22] On one hand, this data set is rare as PFI operates within a black box of secrecy, making it difficult for policymakers to cull datasets. On the other hand, rising production costs, expensive ticket prices, and "triple taxation" via 10% amusement, 30% income, and 12% value-added tax have compounded the issue. Despite economic spillovers, these supply-side chokepoints continue to hound PFI.

On the demand-side, PFI also faces severe challenges. Intense pressure from CEAP to perform at the box office has kept the industry on its toes, lest they get pulled out in favor of more profitable Hollywood releases.[^23] Audiences came to the rescue of *Heneral Luna* as a form of cultural nationalism amidst threats of the latter's displacement after poor showing during its first week, generating momentum.[^24] But these flash-in-the-pan moments are rare. Ticket prices have also gone up, pushing moviegoers towards Netflix. While producers may scale back production to distribute direct-to-streaming, there is no guarantee that Netflix or Amazon will license their films, typically between $80,000 to $160,000/film, according to EPOP.

**LIGHTS, CAMERA, ACTION!**

**Based on the five criteria**, the best approach for FDCP would be **Option 2: Recalibrate spending towards supply-side interventions**. Realigning a third of the agency's budget typically allotted for film location incentives ($1.53M in FY2026) in favor of piloting a Global Screen Fund like that of the British Film Institute's could unlock new PFI markets and reorientate PFI's historic reliance on domestic box office consumption. The film *Hello, Love Again*, a diasporic themed rom-com, earned $2.4M in North America alone on its opening weekend.[^30]

The said fund should be geared towards supporting sales and distribution of PFI films in territories with large concentrations of the Filipino diaspora, serving as foundational markets. The Department of Foreign Affairs recorded 10.8 million overseas Filipinos in 2024.[^31] This could activate the Filipino diaspora's higher spending power and cultural affinity. With strong marketing support, it could also activate foreign markets. The fund should also finance global business strategies that can drive international growth for PFI and other screen sectors, maybe even galvanize FDCP's existing "Film Philippines" brand towards showcasing the best of PFI globally.

FDCP's film location incentives program which produces "mission outcomes" like tourism and economic spillover may still be pursued but through a strategic partnership with the Department of Tourism (DOT). FDCP can provide technical assistance to production companies while DOT can be encouraged to allocate 0.28% of its proposed $53.2M budget for FY 2026 while pushing tourism-related conditionalities (i.e. films should creatively showcase a tourist destination). In the same vein, the economic spillover from film location incentives (1:12, as per correspondence with FDCP in 2021), should be leveraged with the Department of Finance to lobby for the relaxation of one or all of the "triple taxes" hampering PFI.

A radical, albeit more expensive, proposition would be for FDCP to investment in a three-to-five year Oscars campaign in the Best Foreign Language Film Category. If successful, this could unlock significant outcomes through the "halo effect."[^32] A successful Oscar nomination can add around $20 million to a film's total income. Wins are even more lucrative, as with *The King's Speech*. Projected to gross only $30 million, after its Oscar win, it made more than $400 million at the box office. Evidence across subsamples from a study conducted by Aray shows that wins in the Best Foreign Language Film category are positively correlated with movie production in middle-income countries.[^33] However, box office returns for any Oscar-nominated or winning PFI feature should be socialized such that FDCP can also recoup its short-term investment. Further consultations should be done with Oscar strategists[^34] and film authorities from other developing countries that have succeeded in securing nominations i.e. India (*Lagaan*, 2001) and South Africa (*Tsotsi*, 2005). FDCP should also push for **cost-sharing with the producer** as much as possible so as not to "lock-in" its funding at the expense of other programs benefiting IGA.

Sticking to "business as usual" and not exploring new interventions would be tantamount to "betting on the wrong horse" again and again, impacting PFI's productivity, and the livelihood of more than 300,000 film workers.[^35] **Option 2 would be the most effective, efficient, and future-oriented of the three.**
