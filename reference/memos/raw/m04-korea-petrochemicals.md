**Cover Note**

**Context**
For the marker's understanding, the following provides a brief explanation of the case addressed in this memo. Korea's petrochemical industry has experienced a prolonged downturn since 2022 due to global oversupply and the steady erosion of domestic firms' price competitiveness. Recognizing that neither market forces nor firms alone could resolve the crisis, the government announced a series of measures, including the "Petrochemical Industry Competitiveness Enhancement Plan" (Dec 2024)[^1] and a voluntary industry-led capacity reduction framework (Aug 2025)[^2]. Most recently, the Petrochemical Special Act[^3]—one of newly elected President Jae-myung Lee's key campaign pledges—passed the National Assembly on 2 December 2025, providing a legal foundation that makes overcapacity restructuring far easier than before. This memo is written to advise MOTI working-level officials on the follow-up policies needed in the post–Special Act environment.

**Writer**
The writer of this memo is a Senior Research Fellow at the Korea Institute for Industrial Economics and Trade (KIET), Korea's leading government-affiliated industrial policy research institute.

KIET's role is to support government decision-making through industry analysis and policy advice, and the writer—its lead petrochemical expert for over a decade—has long highlighted the sector's structural vulnerabilities while advising government–industry discussions during the current crisis. This background provides the analytical credibility and policy relevance needed to offer a well-grounded recommendation for Korea's industrial transition.

**Authorizer**
The authorizer is the Director of the Chemical Industry Team at the Ministry of Trade and Industry(MOTI), the primary unit responsible for petrochemical policy, industrial restructuring, and implementation of the newly enacted Special Act.

As the key official charged with coordinating firms, policy finance institutions, and related ministries, the authorizer holds both the administrative authority and political motivation to guide Korea's petrochemical transition effectively. This memo is therefore directed to the policymaker best positioned to evaluate and implement a forward strategy.

**Redirecting the Path of Korea's Petrochemical Industry**

**To**: Director of the Chemical Industry Team at the MOTI
**From**: Senior Research Fellow at the KIET

Dear Director,

**Issue**
Korea's petrochemical industry is facing a structural rather than cyclical crisis. Utilization rates at naphtha cracker centers (NCC) dropped sharply from 94% in 2021 to 74% in 2023, and major producers' average operating margins fell from 13.4% to 0.6% in the same period.[^4] Beyond the market deterioration caused by global oversupply, decades of path dependency—built around scale-driven commodity production—have locked firms, regions, and institutions into a model that is no longer competitive and does little to support innovation.

While the Petrochemical Industry Special Act (Dec 2025) has recently passed the National Assembly, it serves primarily as a framework for restructuring existing facilities rather than as a comprehensive innovation policy. Without additional measures to fill the strategic and financial gaps created by restructuring, the Special Act's impact risks being limited to managed downsizing rather than enabling a forward-looking industrial transformation.

Accordingly, I suggest you to reflect on how the government can reshape incentives and governance to break the sector's structural lock-in and enable an innovation-driven shift. The analysis that follows offers suggestion on how to pursue this transition effectively.

**Analysis**
The root cause of Korea's petrochemical industry crisis is not merely oversupply, but a lock-in to an NCC-centered, mass-production model focused on commodity products, which has suppressed innovation. This lock-in can be understood across three levels.

- **Innovation stagnation**: Korean petrochemical firms remain structurally inclined toward large-scale commodity investments because financing, operational certainty, and expected returns still favor NCC-based production. Even during the extended boom cycle of 2015-2021, companies continued to expand commodity production lines rather than investing in high value-added products or low-carbon innovation.[^5] R&D intensity remains low relative to global innovation leaders[^6], and the payoff structure for technological risk remains weak. As a result, companies have little economic rationale to shift their portfolios toward specialty or advanced materials, perpetuating a long-standing innovation deficit.
- **Regional dependence**: : Petrochemical clusters in Ulsan, Yeosu, and Daesan rely heavily on commodity-based operations for employment, subcontractor activity, and municipal revenue. In Yeosu, refining and chemical industries account for 67.4% of manufacturing employment and 96.3% of value added.[^6] This creates strong local resistance to portfolio changes that may reduce capacity or alter labor demand, constraining firms' willingness to pursue riskier, innovation-oriented investments.
- **Governance fragmentation**: Fragmented governance across Ministry of Trade and Industry(MOTI), Ministry of Economy and Finance(MOEF), and policy finance institutions limits Korea's ability to coordinate a strategic industrial transition. MOTI has sector expertise but lacks budgetary authority, while MOEF prioritizes fiscal discipline and shapes risk-averse lending norms. Policy banks consequently favor low-risk, collateral-based projects—typically commodity investments—over higher-risk innovation efforts unless there's strong mandate.[^7] This institutional misalignment leads to incremental policy measures.

**Windows of Opportunity**
In August 2025, major petrochemical firms agreed with the government to voluntarily reduce 2.7–3.7 million tons of ethylene capacity by 2030—about 25% of national capacity.[^2] As legacy facilities wind down, firms must determine how to redirect their freed financial and strategic resources toward future growth areas.

At the same time, global decarbonization pressures—such as carbon border measures and required CO₂-reduction investments[^8]—are reshaping value chains and creating both risks and new market opportunities for Korea's petrochemical sector.

**Stakeholders**
The key stakeholders in petrochemical transition hold varying levels of influence and divergent motivations, shaping the political feasibility of policy options.

- **Major petrochemical firms** prioritize stability and predictable returns, making them cautious toward long-cycle, uncertain innovation investments, even as market conditions increase pressure for transition.
- **Regional governments** rely heavily on petrochemical clusters for local employment and fiscal revenue, leading them to favor gradual transitions over disruptive restructuring.
- **Policy finance institutions**(e.g. Korea Development Bank) are willing to support transition but require clear government mandates and risk-sharing frameworks.
- **MOEF** exercises strong control over budgets and retains a market-oriented stance that limits subsidy-heavy or interventionist industrial policy.
- **MOTI** holds the industrial expertise and the statutory authority granted by the Special Act but lacks sufficient independent resources and must coordinate across ministries.

**Criteria**
I suggest four criteria to evaluate policy options, to make sure the policy design is sustainable and brings desirable outcome in the long term.

- **Public Value:** Does it meaningfully contribute to industrial competitiveness, low-carbon transition, and a smooth adjustment for regional economies?
- **Legitimacy:** Can it secure political sustainability by aligning the interests of firms, local communities, and government ministries?
- **Operational Capacity**: Is it implementable within Korea's existing administrative, regulatory, and policy-finance systems?
- **Fiscal Viability:** Can it be executed without significant new fiscal burdens by realigning existing policy resources or redirecting private capital flows?

**Policy Options**

- Option 1 — **Firm-led, non-directional government support**
  Continue firm-led portfolio decisions while expanding R&D funding, tax incentives, and regulatory easing focused on process efficiency and incremental upgrades. Maintain existing policy finance as demand-driven, supporting projects firms voluntarily submit. This approach preserves the current system without altering investment incentives or capital flows.

  **Low Public Value** because it does little to overcome structural lock-ins. **Medium Legitimacy** being politically safe but lacking long-term support from stakeholders expecting transformation. **High Operational Capacity and Fiscal Viability,** using existing tools with minimal cost.

- Option 2 — **Conditional, directional market-shaping**
  Issue a clear transition road-map under the Special Act and reshape capital allocation by making policy finance conditional on investment in high-value and low-carbon segments. Build commercialization infrastructure—pilot plants, certification fast-tracks, early-demand programs—to lower risks for firms shifting portfolios. This approach redirects investment incentives without mandating restructuring.

  **High Public Value**, given its ability to redirect capital and promote long-term innovation. **High-Medium Legitimacy**, depending on ability to balance government direction with region's and firms' interests. **High-Medium Operational Capacity and Fiscal Viability**, leveraging existing policy-finance tools but requiring stronger coordination.

- Option 3 — **Top-down state-led transition**
  Establish a Prime Minister–level control tower to designate target products and require major firms to adjust portfolios and capacity accordingly. Mandate policy finance institutions to channel a fixed share of lending into government-selected sectors while restricting support for commodity expansions. Implement region-specific restructuring packages, including workforce transition and plant conversion plans.

  **High Public Value** in theory due to its potential to accelerate restructuring. **Medium Legitimacy** due to resistance from firms and MOEF, despite region's possible support. **Low-Midium Operational Capacity and Fiscal Viability**, requiring significant administrative authority and funding, misaligned with Korea's post-developmental institutional environment.

**Recommendation**
Choose **Option 2**. It is the only approach that directly addresses the incentive, institutional, and governance lock-ins that have constrained the sector. It is well matched to Korea's operational capacity, particularly its strengths in policy finance and coordinated implementation. It also offers political legitimacy without requiring coercive restructuring.

**Risks and Implementation**
Option 2 carries risks of implementation, particularly if policy finance institutions remain risk-averse and slow, or if inter-ministerial coordination falters, especially as other strategic sectors such as semiconductors and AI compete for attention. Early market uncertainties and regional concerns may also slow firm participation, limiting the intended shift in investment incentives.

Despite these risks, Korea faces a rare window of opportunity: a new administration with strong political legitimacy, a Special Act providing a legal basis for structural transition, and global trends favoring high-value, low-carbon production. It is critical that MOTI act swiftly to seize this momentum; the following action plan outlines the immediate steps required, in order.

- **Publish a Transition Roadmap** outlining priority product areas and conditional financing criteria to guide ministries and policy finance institutions ahead of the next budget cycle.
- **Establish a MOTI–MOEF–policy finance coordination mechanism** to set risk-sharing rules and financing mandates, and reflect these in the 2026 budget and policy finance plans.
- **Launch pilot instruments**—conditional loans, fast-track permitting, and demonstration support—to send credible early signals and prevent a rebound to commodity-heavy investment.

**Sincerely,**
**Senior Research Fellow at the KIET**
