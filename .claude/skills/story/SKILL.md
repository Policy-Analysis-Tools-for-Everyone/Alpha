# Story

Assume `house-rules` is in play. If it is not, its two hardest rules still hold:
invent nothing, and the user decides.

## What this owns

Turning mature reasoning into something another person can understand, use and
decide from. Selection, ordering, compression, audience fit, format and final
writing quality.

The analysis itself stays upstream. This capability is where weak analysis becomes
visible, and where the temptation to paper over it is strongest.

## First, check there is a story to tell

Before drafting, check whether the case can answer: what is the problem, what
decision is being made, what serious alternatives remain, what is each expected to
cause, what trade-off matters most, what does the analysis recommend, and what
uncertainty could still change that.

If several of those are missing, the output is not ready for polish. Say which is
missing and go there. **A confident tone cannot repair a missing causal argument or
a fake option set**, and prose good enough to hide the gap is worse than prose that
exposes it.

**The Grandma Bessie test.** Explain the analysis in plain language to an
intelligent person who knows nothing about the issue. In about a minute they should
understand the problem, why it matters, the choice, the answer and why the answer
follows. If that needs a long institutional history or several minutes of technical
language, something is still unclear, and it is usually the reasoning rather than
the wording.

The short version is a test of reasoning first and the core of the eventual summary
second. Do not turn it into a slogan.

## Start with audience and purpose

Who will read this? Who has to decide? Who advises that person? What do they
already know? What will they challenge? What do they need before they can act?

The same analysis needs different treatment for a minister, a senior official, a
board, a technical group, delivery partners, affected groups and a public audience.
The person who commissioned the work is not always the audience that matters.

**Name the job of the output.** Complete: *after reading this, the audience should
be able to `[understand / decide / approve / challenge / act]`.* If that sentence
is vague, the document will be too.

**Think about how it travels.** Which sentence might be quoted alone? Which caveat
would be dangerous to lose? Which term could be misread? Does the recommendation
still make sense outside this conversation? Write so the core reasoning survives
being repeated by someone else, and never soften the evidence because the document
may travel.

## Choose the output mode

**A policy story** where the user needs the reasoning more than a template: a
briefing note, a short narrative, an email, speaking notes, a presentation
structure, a decision summary. Order it by what the audience needs, and **never
reproduce the analytical method as headings.**

**PPC memo mode** where a compact professional memo is required, or the user asks
for it by name. Eight sections in order: Issue, Stakeholders, Analysis, Criteria,
Options, Recommendation, Risks and Uncertainties, Implementation. Typically around
2 pages, with endnotes outside that total.

**Another format** where the context imposes one: a submission, ministerial advice,
a board paper. Use that structure, and do not force the PPC headings onto a format
that has its own contract. The storytelling principles still apply.

## Build the story

**Open with the issue.** What is happening, who is affected, scale or direction,
why it warrants attention, what decision follows. Never open with a long history.

**Use background only where it changes the decision:** why the problem exists, a
critical driver, an institutional constraint, why an option would work, why this
decision exists now. Cut background that only proves you did the research. A
generic "Background" section is where material with no job in the argument goes to
hide.

**Give every section a job.** For each paragraph: what question does this answer
that the audience now needs answered? If there is no answer, cut it, move it to
supporting material, or merge it with the section it actually supports. Never make
the reader assemble the argument from accurate but disconnected facts.

**Show only serious alternatives.** Not exploratory ideas, decoys, obviously
infeasible options or cosmetic variants. Include business-as-usual where it remains
a serious choice. Compare the recommendation with the strongest realistic
competitor, because a comparison against a weak one tells the reader nothing.

**Tie evidence to the claim it supports.** For each important factual claim: what
establishes this, and why does the audience need it here? Never make the reader
infer why a statistic appears.

**Keep four things distinguishable:** what is observed, what is expected to happen,
what remains unknown or contested, and why the analysis prefers one course.
Compression must never turn *evidence suggests* into *will happen*. That is the
single most damaging thing this capability can do, because it happens invisibly and
the reader has no way to detect it.

**Make the central trade-off visible.** The audience should be able to say what we
gain and what we give up. Do not bury the main exchange inside a dense options
table. If one value judgement decides the choice, state it.

**Make the recommendation easy to find:** what should happen, the main reason, and
any condition that could change the answer. Never make the reader infer it from the
final paragraph.

**Address the strongest serious objection.** What would the most credible critic
say? State it fairly, address the evidence, say whether it changes the
recommendation, and preserve what is unresolved. A collection of weak objections
for the appearance of balance is worse than none.

## PPC memo mode, section by section

Use this only in PPC mode. **The 8 sections are a format, not a claim about the
workflow the user followed.**

- **Issue.** The concise problem definition. Anything from a phrase to a few
  sentences, depending on what the reader already knows. What is wrong or at
  stake, for whom, how large, and why a decision is needed. Not background
  history.
- **Stakeholders.** The prominent actors with concise analysis of their
  motivations, beliefs and resources, compressed to what changes political
  feasibility or the decision. Where the decision maker has an important interest
  that is not obvious, include it without turning the section into lobbying.
- **Analysis.** The critical drivers and dynamics of the issue: causes and
  mechanisms, the system dynamics that matter, visible risks and opportunities,
  and why the problem persists. Separate from the option comparison, and not a
  place for background the reader already has.
- **Criteria.** The compact set of standards that makes the later comparison
  intelligible: the core policy goal, material legal, financial or technical
  feasibility, material political feasibility, other decision-relevant values.
  Not the whole criteria analysis.
- **Options.** The serious courses of action. For each: what it is, the main
  mechanism, projected outcome, important advantage, important disadvantage, and
  how it performs against the criteria. Use descriptive titles that do not assume
  success. The convention often runs to 3 options including some form of
  continuing current policy, but **do not manufacture a third to satisfy the
  convention.** Use the number the analysis supports and say so.
- **Recommendation.** Short. Recommended option, decisive rationale, material
  condition if there is one. Not a repeat of the options section.
- **Risks and Uncertainties.** The few unknowns capable of changing the case. A
  useful entry: *`[uncertainty]` matters because `[decision consequence]`. We
  would reduce it through `[evidence, test or review condition]`.* Reflect on
  value, capacity and support when choosing which unknowns to include. Do not make
  every part of the recommendation sound unknowable, and do not claim more
  confidence than the analysis supports.
- **Implementation.** Who, what, when, where, building from the recommendation
  rather than restating the options. Early-stage memos give more space to
  de-risking; later-stage memos give more to implementation. Include only actions
  the analysis supports, and never invent delivery detail to fill the section.

**Use the page limit as a decision about priority.** Sections may be longer,
shorter or omitted depending on the case. A 2-page memo is not a shrunken 20-page
report, and 8 equal boxes means nobody decided what matters.

## Compress without damaging the analysis

Cut the research diary: every source consulted, every rejected option, every
version of the problem, every dead end. Keep what establishes the problem, the
mechanism, the important evidence, the options, the consequences, the trade-off,
the recommendation and the uncertainty.

**Cut repetition before cutting reasoning.** Remove duplicated claims, repeated
context, several examples proving one point, restated option descriptions, repeated
caveats. Protect the causal logic, the important magnitudes, the trade-off, the
material uncertainty and the sentence that explains why the evidence supports the
conclusion. That sentence is the one most often lost to length pressure and the one
most worth keeping.

**Qualify where it matters, not everywhere.** Use calibrated wording once, such as
likely, roughly, current evidence suggests, within the tested setting, conditional
on, then group the fuller uncertainty where it belongs. Repeated caveats make an
argument unreadable and, oddly, less trustworthy rather than more.

**Use a table only when it reduces the reader's work.** A table is weak when it
needs a long explanation, when cell labels are vague, or when magnitude, uncertainty
or the central trade-off disappears into it. Never use a matrix because policy
memos are expected to look analytical.

## The writing pass

Apply this after the structure is sound, never as a substitute for it. The full
specification is `reference/writing/anti-ai-writing-style.md` and is worth reading
when the output is substantial prose. These are the rules that matter most.

- UK spelling. Numbers as digits.
- Short paragraphs, 1 to 3 sentences. Vary sentence length: even pacing is the
  clearest signal of machine text. Short, then longer, then a fragment, then a
  sentence that earns its length.
- Contractions where they fit. Active voice. Direct address.
- **No em dashes.** Commas, colons, semicolons, full stops or brackets.
- **Say "is" and "has".** Not serves as, stands as, represents, marks a, boasts,
  features, offers.
- **No negated reframes.** "This isn't X, it's Y", "not just X but Y", "the
  question isn't X, it's Y", "less X, more Y", and the disguised versions: "while
  X might seem right, Y is actually", "sure, X works, but Y is where". Delete
  everything before the positive claim. This is the most reliable machine tell
  there is.
- **No dead vocabulary.** delve, realm, harness, unlock, tapestry, landscape,
  intricate, crucial, pivotal, leverage, robust, seamless, holistic, foster,
  underscore, showcase, transformative, empower, streamline, elevate,
  data-driven, unprecedented, testament, commendable.
- **No filler or meta-commentary.** No "it's important to note", "in today's", "in
  order to", "let's explore", "in this section we will".
- **No mechanical transitions.** No furthermore, moreover, additionally, that
  said, with that in mind.
- **No puffery.** State the fact and let the reader judge its significance. No
  pivotal moments, significant shifts or key turning points.
- **No rule of three by reflex.** Three adjectives in a row is a tell. Use 2, or
  4, or the one that matters.
- **No fake ranges.** "From ancient traditions to modern innovations" means
  nothing.
- **Sentence case in headings**, not Title Case.
- **Use the real subject.** *DVLA would change the rule*, not *a change to the
  rule would be undertaken by DVLA*. *The evidence is weak*, not *there are
  limitations in relation to the available evidence base*.
- **Stop when the point is made.** No closing paragraph restating the
  recommendation for the third time.
- Formatting like salt. Headers, bullets and bold only when they earn it.

**Two things outrank the style rules.** Accuracy: never drop a magnitude, a
caveat, a distinction or a necessary technical term to satisfy a writing rule.
And precision: keep technical language where it is precise, where the audience
knows it, where replacing it would take more words, or where the concept itself
matters. Explain or replace jargon that makes the reader work for nothing. Plain
language does not mean stripping out every policy term.

## Boundaries and handoffs: when the story breaks, go upstream

Writing is the final test of the analytical chain, and a section that will not
write is usually a section that was never finished. Continue the work rather than
announcing a handoff.

- The issue cannot be stated plainly, mixes several problem levels, hides a
  solution, or has no magnitude or public significance, so go to `problem`. Do not
  wordsmith around it.
- Stakeholder motivations are guessed, or political feasibility cannot be
  summarised responsibly, so go to `stakeholders`. Do not fill the section with
  generic labels.
- The analysis section cannot say what drives the issue, what is known or what
  remains uncertain, so go to `evidence`.
- The criteria are generic, or the options are cosmetic variants, or there is a
  straw alternative, or business-as-usual is missing without reason, so go to
  `criteria` and `options`. Sharper labels do not fix a weak option set.
- The story describes interventions but cannot say what they would cause, so go to
  `outcomes`. It cannot state the central exchange, so go to `trade-offs`. It
  cannot say why the preferred option wins or what would change the answer, so go
  to `decide`.

## Self-check

**Must pass:**

- audience and purpose are clear, and the output mode is explicit
- the story passes the Grandma Bessie test
- the issue appears early enough for the reader to see why the work matters
- the decision or recommendation is easy to find
- only serious alternatives get substantial attention
- evidence is tied to the claims it supports
- projection stays distinguishable from established evidence
- the central trade-off is visible
- material uncertainty is preserved
- the recommendation follows from the underlying analysis
- the preferred option is compared with the strongest credible alternative
- the strongest serious objection has not been ignored
- the structure does not narrate the analytical method
- no weak analytical section has been repaired with invented prose
- the writing pass has been applied

**PPC mode must also pass:**

- Issue is concise and suited to what the reader already knows
- Stakeholders carries motivations, beliefs and resources at a proportionate level
- Analysis explains critical drivers rather than generic background
- Criteria is compact enough to prepare the option comparison
- Options project outcomes and relate advantages and disadvantages to the criteria
- the number of options reflects the real choice, not a convention
- Recommendation is short and locatable
- Risks focuses on decision-relevant unknowns and what would reduce them
- Implementation states who, what, when and where at a level the analysis supports
- space is allocated by what the decision needs, not evenly

**Should pass:**

- technical detail proportionate to the audience
- tables reduce the reader's work
- supporting detail moved out of the core story where appropriate
- another person could repeat the argument accurately
- citations or endnotes follow the requested format
- the ending stops once the decision and next action have landed

## Failure modes

- **The method becomes the table of contents.**
- **Background dump.** History arriving before the reader knows why it matters.
- **Evidence dump.** Facts and citations with no claim attached.
- **Every working option surviving** into the final output.
- **Recommendation hidden** in the last paragraph.
- **Uncertainty stripped out for force.** A tentative projection becoming certain
  prose.
- **Caveats swamping the argument.**
- **PPC as 8 equal boxes.**
- **A manufactured third option** to satisfy the template.
- **Implementation repeating the option** with verbs added.
- **The risks section as a disclaimer**, where everything is uncertain.
- **Polish hiding a gap.** Elegant prose over a weak mechanism, a fake trade-off
  or an unsupported recommendation. The most dangerous failure available here.
- **Machine voice.** Filler, hype, negated reframes, mechanical transitions,
  metronome rhythm.
